Packaging, three-dimensional integration and wiring

The package is a microwave cavity

A silicon die between conducting surfaces supports box modes. For a dielectric slab of side lengths \(a, b\) and small height, with ideal walls, the lowest modes are at

$$ f_{mn} = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\sqrt{\Big(\frac{m}{a}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{n}{b}\Big)^2}, \qquad f_{11}\big|_{a=b} \approx \frac{62.6\ \mathrm{GHz\cdot mm}}{a}\quad(\varepsilon_r = 11.45). $$

A 5 mm research die has its first mode near 12.5 GHz, safely above the qubits. A 10 mm die puts it at 6.3 GHz, inside the transmon band, and larger dies place many modes there (@fig:boxmode). A qubit detuned by \(\Delta_m\) from a mode of linewidth \(\kappa_m\) with coupling \(g_m\) decays into it at \(\Gamma \approx \kappa_m(g_m/\Delta_m)^2\), the same Purcell form as @eq:chi. This is why scaling beyond a few tens of qubits forced the move to through-silicon vias [@yost2020] and bump-bonded multi-chip stacks [@rosenberg2017]. Grounded vias at pitch \(p \ll \lambda/2\) divide the substrate into cells whose modes lie far above the qubit band. Careful package design is itself a substantial literature [@huang2021].

Three-dimensional integration adds design rules with no planar counterpart: bump pitch and keep-out zones, via density, the spacing of flux-trapping holes in ground planes, and the mismatch in thermal contraction between bonded dies cooled from 300 K to 10 mK. IBM's Loon processor adds multiple low-loss routing layers for the long-range couplers its qLDPC codes need (§9). Modular roadmaps (IBM's Cockatoo, Google's Atlantic Quantum stack, photonic and ion interconnects) add inter-module links whose loss and crosstalk must be modelled alongside the chip. Today the check that no package mode lies within \(\Delta_{\min}\) of any qubit frequency is a manual eigenmode study per package. It should be an automated sign-off rule, run against the qubit frequency list.

Wiring: a heat budget and a noise budget

Each control or readout line conducts heat and dissipates signal power in its attenuators. At every refrigerator stage \(s\),

$$ \sum_{\text{lines}}\Big(P^{\mathrm{pass}}_{s} + P^{\mathrm{act}}_{s}\Big) \le P^{\mathrm{cool}}_s , $$

where available cooling power falls from about a watt at 4 K, to tens of milliwatts at the still (about 0.8 K), to about a milliwatt at 100 mK, and to tens of microwatts at the 10–20 mK mixing chamber, for a large commercial dilution refrigerator. Each line also carries thermal noise from room temperature, where a 5 GHz mode holds \(\bar n(300\,\mathrm K) = (e^{hf/k_BT}-1)^{-1} \approx 1.25\times10^3\) photons. An attenuator of power ratio \(A\) at temperature \(T_s\) maps

$$ \bar n_{\mathrm{out}} = \frac{\bar n_{\mathrm{in}}}{A} + \Big(1 - \frac1A\Big)\,\bar n(T_s), $$

so roughly 60 dB of attenuation, distributed across stages, is needed to bring the residual population well below \(10^{-3}\) [@krinner2019; @yeh2017]. Residual photons in a readout resonator dephase the qubit at [@clerk2007]

$$ \Gamma_\varphi \simeq \bar n_{\mathrm{th}}\,\kappa\,\frac{4\chi^2}{\kappa^2 + 4\chi^2}\qquad(\bar n_{\mathrm{th}}\ll1). $$

More attenuation means less noise but more heat. Krinner et al. worked through these budgets for a cryogenic setup at the 100-qubit scale [@krinner2019]. Every lab repeats the analysis in a spreadsheet; it could be generated from the chip netlist.

The long-term answer is classical control electronics inside the refrigerator: cryogenic CMOS [@charbon2016; @patra2018; @bardin2019; @xue2021] or single-flux-quantum logic [@mcdermott2018]. Google's 28 nm cryo-CMOS controller dissipated under 2 mW at 3 K [@bardin2019]. A power of 1–2 mW per qubit therefore allows about a thousand qubits per watt at 4 K, before multiplexing. Cryogenic CMOS needs transistor models valid near 4 K. Cadence, Semiwise and sureCore have produced such models for GlobalFoundries' 22FDX process in the Spectre simulator [@web_cadence_cryo], and Cadence reports Equal1 designing a silicon quantum processor with its standard flow [@web_cadence_equal1]. Superconducting digital logic has its own design-automation tradition [@krylov2022; @seda2026]. The incumbents are absorbing this layer. What remains open is the system-level step: generating the line plan, attenuation plan, stage heat loads and per-qubit dephasing budget directly from the chip design, and co-simulating qubit and controller across vendors. Reilly's review frames the broader quantum–classical interface problem [@reilly2015].
