Introduction

The industrial moment

Three events in 2024–2026 mark the transition of quantum hardware from physics experiment to manufactured product. First, the coherence of qubits made on production equipment caught up with laboratory devices. imec showed that transmons patterned with optical lithography and subtractive etching on 300 mm wafers can match the coherence of devices made by electron-beam lithography and shadow evaporation [@vandamme2024]. Intel showed that cryogenic probing of 300 mm spin-qubit wafers yields statistics over thousands of devices [@neyens2024]. IBM moved its primary processor fabrication to the 300 mm line at Albany [@web_ibm_roadmap]. Second, quantum error correction crossed a threshold that matters for engineering: Google's Willow processor showed logical error falling exponentially with surface-code distance, with a suppression factor \(\Lambda = 2.14\) per distance step [@google2025]. Third, the industry began to separate design from fabrication. IBM created Anderon, a pure-play 300 mm quantum foundry with a US$1 billion CHIPS award plus US$1 billion of its own money, open to outside customers [@web_anderon; @web_anderon_ieee]. IonQ bought the US foundry SkyWater for about US$1.8 billion [@web_ionq_skywater]. Photonic companies moved their designs onto GlobalFoundries and Tower production lines [@web_xanadu_gf; @web_xanadu_tower].

The design layer between a qubit concept and a manufacturable wafer has not kept pace. Classical chip design solved the same separation problem in three stages. Mead and Conway's design rules let designers work without knowledge of the process [@meadconway1980]. SPICE and its compact device models let them predict circuit behaviour before fabrication [@nagel1975]. Once the foundry model emerged, the process design kit (PDK) and the sign-off checks became the contract between a fabless designer and a fab the designer does not control. When variability came to dominate below about 100 nm, the industry added statistical timing and design-for-manufacturability methods [@blaauw2008; @orshansky2008]. Quantum processors need all three layers at once, because they are variability-limited from the first device.

Thesis

The review develops three claims.

  1. The binding constraint is moving from the best device to the distribution of devices. Record coherence times are no longer what limits processor scale. What limits it is the spread of Hamiltonian parameters across hundreds to thousands of nominally identical elements, and the rare outliers in the tails of that spread. Junction physics (§4) makes this spread exponentially sensitive to atomic-scale variation.
  2. The data needed to manage the distribution already exists but is not connected. Every fab measures junction resistances at room temperature, many measure resonator quality factors, and some probe whole wafers cryogenically. These data rarely flow back into design models, and design intent rarely flows forward into test planning (§7, §11).
  3. Error correction supplies the objective function. Because roadmaps are now specified in logical qubits, a die's value is determined by its logical error rate. In principle this can be computed from a layout, the fab's variation statistics and a defect map (§9). That makes the whole flow, from design to test, optimisable against one quantity.

Scope and method

Superconducting circuits serve as the reference modality, because they have the largest number of independent chip makers, the newest foundries and the most mature design literature. Trapped ions, photonics, silicon spins, neutral atoms and topological qubits are treated comparatively in §8. We cover the physical layer, from device design to the classical control loop. We do not cover compilers, algorithms or application software, except where error correction couples them to hardware. The industrial survey draws on company releases, regulatory filings and trade press to 11 November 2026, and we flag figures that are press-reported rather than disclosed. The technical review draws on the peer-reviewed literature; key quantitative claims are cross-checked by the figure-generation script.

Notation. \(f\) denotes a frequency in Hz and \(\omega = 2\pi f\). Energies are quoted in frequency units, \(E/h\), unless stated otherwise. \(\Phi_0 = h/2e\) is the flux quantum and \(R_Q = h/4e^2 \approx 6.45\ \mathrm{k\Omega}\) the superconducting resistance quantum. Bold symbols denote vectors of design or Hamiltonian parameters: \(\mathbf{x}\) for geometry and process, \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) for the Hamiltonian parameters extracted from it.

Structure

§2 surveys companies, technologies, deals and roadmaps. §3–§7 follow a superconducting processor through design, variability and yield, materials, packaging and control, and test and calibration. §8 extends the analysis to other modalities, and §9 treats error correction as the layer shared by all of them. §10 maps the existing tool landscape against the flow. §11 states seven open problems formally. §12 discusses commercial scope and timing, and §13 concludes. Appendices give the parameters behind each computed figure, a glossary, and a structured reading guide.
