Test, characterisation and calibration

What is measured

The figures of merit are standard [@krantz2019; @eisert2020]: energy relaxation \(T_1\); dephasing \(T_2\), with \(1/T_2 = 1/2T_1 + 1/T_\varphi\); gate error from randomised benchmarking; and readout assignment fidelity. Randomised benchmarking fits the decay of sequence fidelity over random Clifford sequences of length \(m\) [@magesan2011]:

$$ F(m) = A\,p^m + B, \qquad r = \frac{(1-p)(d-1)}{d}, \quad d = 2^n, $$

which makes the error per Clifford \(r\) insensitive to state-preparation and measurement errors. Assignment fidelity is \(F_a = 1 - \tfrac12[P(1\mid0)+P(0\mid1)]\).

Cold test is the scarce resource

A dilution refrigerator takes a day or more to cycle and holds a handful of dies, so full characterisation is slow. Intel's 300 mm cryogenic prober, developed with Bluefors and Afore, collects statistics on thousands of spin-qubit devices per wafer [@neyens2024]. Bluefors now sells such probers commercially, operating below 2 K on wafers up to 300 mm, and CEA-Leti and Fraunhofer IAF have installed them [@web_bluefors]. At 1–2 K, however, a superconducting qubit's coherence cannot be measured. Superconducting lines therefore rely on warm proxies (junction resistance, resonator response at higher temperature) and must decide which few dies deserve a full cooldown. Because \(T_1\) fluctuates in time [@klimov2018], a single measurement of a die is a sample, not a characterisation.

Framed statistically, test planning is Bayesian experimental design [@chaloner1995; @rainforth2024]. Choose the next measurement to maximise expected information about the die's parameters per unit of instrument time:

$$ m^\star = \arg\max_m \frac{\mathbb E_{y_m}\big[\,H(\boldsymbol\theta) - H(\boldsymbol\theta\mid y_m)\,\big]}{t_m} , $$

stopping when the posterior probability that the die meets specification leaves an indecision band, \(\Pr(\text{good}\mid\mathbf y)\notin(p_{\mathrm{lo}},p_{\mathrm{hi}})\). The same measurements can recalibrate the simulators. The extraction and loss models of §3 and §5 have parameters \(\boldsymbol\phi\) (effective permittivities, kinetic inductance, interface loss tangents) that are imperfectly known. Cold data constrain them through \(p(\boldsymbol\phi\mid\mathbf y)\propto p(\mathbf y\mid\boldsymbol\phi)p(\boldsymbol\phi)\). When the likelihood is available only through an expensive simulator, this is simulation-based inference [@cranmer2020]. Over successive wafers, the bias between predicted and measured frequency should shrink. That is the measurable sign that the flow is learning.

Calibration

A processor of \(N\) qubits and \(E\) couplers carries on the order of ten calibrated parameters per element: frequencies, pulse amplitudes and phases, DRAG coefficients, readout settings and coupler biases. That is about \(10^4\) parameters at \(N=1000\). DRAG is the canonical example. Adding a derivative component on the quadrature channel, \(\Omega(t) = \Omega_x(t) + i\beta\,\dot\Omega_x(t)/\alpha\), cancels leakage to the second excited state, and \(\beta\) must be tuned per qubit [@motzoi2009]. Google formalised calibration as a directed acyclic graph of experiments with cheap checks, re-running a node only when its check or a dependency fails [@kelly2018]. Bayesian and online Hamiltonian learning [@granade2012] and integrated model-based toolsets [@wittler2021] reduce the number of experiments.

Calibration is the best-served stage of the flow commercially. Quantum Machines, Qblox, Zurich Instruments (whose ZQCS platform, launched in March 2026, packs more than a thousand channels per rack [@web_zi_zqcs]), Keysight, Q-CTRL, Qruise and QuantrolOx all offer automation. With error correction, classical decoding joins the loop and must keep pace with syndrome generation (§9). A design-tool company has one distinctive contribution here: the two-way link to design. Seeding calibration from the extracted model starts the search inside tolerance, and returning fitted values updates the model.
