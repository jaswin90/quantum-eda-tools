From geometry to Hamiltonian: superconducting circuit design

Circuit quantisation and the transmon

A superconducting processor is a network of capacitors, inductors and Josephson junctions, quantised in the node-flux representation [@devoret1997; @vool2017; @rasmussen2021]. With node fluxes \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\), the Lagrangian of a lumped network is

$$ \mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 \dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{C}\, \dot{\boldsymbol\Phi} - \tfrac12 \boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T} \mathbf{L}^{-1} \boldsymbol\Phi + \sum_{j} E_{J,j} \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{\Phi_0}\,\Phi_{b(j)}\right), $$

where \(\mathbf{C}\) is the Maxwell capacitance matrix, \(\mathbf{L}^{-1}\) the inverse inductance matrix and \(\Phi_{b(j)}\) the branch flux across junction \(j\). A Legendre transform gives \(H = \tfrac12 \mathbf{Q}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\mathbf{Q} + U(\boldsymbol\Phi)\). Every term in the Hamiltonian is a functional of the geometry through \(\mathbf{C}\) and \(\mathbf{L}\), and of the process through \(E_J\). That is what makes superconducting design an EDA problem.

For the transmon, a single junction shunted by a large capacitance [@koch2007],

$$ H = 4E_C\,(\hat n - n_g)^2 - E_J \cos\hat\varphi, \qquad E_C = \frac{e^2}{2C_\Sigma}, \qquad E_J = \frac{\hbar I_c}{2e}. $$

In the regime \(E_J/E_C \sim 50\text{–}100\), the low-lying spectrum is that of a weakly anharmonic oscillator:

$$ \hbar\omega_{01} \simeq \sqrt{8E_JE_C} - E_C, \qquad \hbar\alpha \equiv \hbar(\omega_{12}-\omega_{01}) \simeq -E_C . $$

The charge dispersion of level \(m\) is suppressed exponentially [@koch2007],

$$ \varepsilon_m \simeq (-1)^m E_C \frac{2^{4m+5}}{m!}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\left(\frac{E_J}{2E_C}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}+\frac34} e^{-\sqrt{8E_J/E_C}}, $$

which is why the transmon tolerates charge noise. The price is a small anharmonicity, typically \(\alpha/2\pi \approx -200\) to \(-340\) MHz at \(f_{01} \approx 4\text{–}6\) GHz. A small \(\alpha\) is what makes frequency crowding (§4) the dominant scaling problem. Fluxonium, which adds a large superinductance, trades this regime for much larger anharmonicity and lower frequency [@manucharyan2009; @nguyen2019]. That is relevant to Google's absorption of Atlantic Quantum, because fluxonium has different sensitivities and needs different cell libraries.

The parameter vector a designer must hit

A multi-qubit device is described, to the accuracy that matters for gates, by an effective Hamiltonian

$$ H = \sum_i \Big(\omega_i\, a_i^\dagger a_i + \tfrac{\alpha_i}{2} a_i^\dagger a_i^\dagger a_i a_i\Big) + \sum_{\langle ij\rangle} g_{ij}\,(a_i^\dagger a_j + a_i a_j^\dagger) + \sum_i \Big(\omega_{r,i}\, b_i^\dagger b_i + g_{r,i}(a_i^\dagger b_i + a_i b_i^\dagger)\Big), $$

with readout resonators \(b_i\) coupled to a Purcell-filtered feedline [@blais2021; @krantz2019]. The designer controls the vector \(\boldsymbol\theta = (\omega_i, \alpha_i, g_{ij}, \omega_{r,i}, g_{r,i}, \kappa_i)\). Performance depends on derived quantities. In the dispersive regime \(|\Delta_{ir}| = |\omega_i-\omega_{r,i}| \gg g_{r,i}\), the readout shift and the Purcell decay are

$$ \chi_i \simeq \frac{g_{r,i}^2}{\Delta_{ir}}\,\frac{\alpha_i}{\Delta_{ir}+\alpha_i}, \qquad \Gamma^{\mathrm{P}}_i \simeq \kappa_i \left(\frac{g_{r,i}}{\Delta_{ir}}\right)^{2}, $$

and the static \(ZZ\) interaction between directly coupled transmons with detuning \(\Delta = \omega_1 - \omega_2\) is

$$ \zeta = \frac{2 g^2 (\alpha_1+\alpha_2)}{(\Delta+\alpha_1)(\alpha_2-\Delta)} . $$

These expressions define the design tension. Fast cross-resonance gates [@rigetti2010; @sheldon2016] need \(g\) large and \(\Delta\) inside the "straddling" window \(|\Delta| \lt |\alpha|\). Low crosstalk needs \(\zeta\) small, which pushes the other way. Fast readout needs large \(\chi\) and \(\kappa\), which raise Purcell loss. Tunable couplers [@yan2018; @mundada2019] break the tension by making \(g\) controllable and cancelling static \(\zeta\), at the cost of a flux line per coupler and a new sensitivity to flux noise.

The sensitivity that matters most for manufacture follows from @eq:freq:

$$ \frac{\delta\omega_{01}}{\omega_{01}+E_C/\hbar} \simeq \frac12\frac{\delta E_J}{E_J} - \frac12 \frac{\delta C_\Sigma}{C_\Sigma}. $$

A 1% error in either the junction critical current or the island capacitance moves a 5 GHz qubit by roughly 25 MHz. That is comparable to, or larger than, the frequency-collision windows of §4. The design problem is therefore not only to choose \(\boldsymbol\theta\) but to choose it so that the manufactured distribution of \(\boldsymbol\theta\) satisfies the constraints.

Extraction: three formulations

The map \(\mathbf{x} \mapsto \boldsymbol\theta\) from layout and stack to Hamiltonian is computed by one of three families of method.

Quasi-static lumped models. Solve Laplace's equation \(\nabla\!\cdot(\varepsilon\nabla\phi)=0\) for the capacitance matrix, add geometric and kinetic inductances, and insert the result into @eq:lagr. This is accurate when structures are small compared with the wavelength (about 24 mm at 5 GHz on silicon). It is the basis of Q3D-style extraction and of the modular "lumped oscillator model" [@minev2021lom]. Its weakness is distributed elements, such as resonators, long couplers and package modes.

Full-wave eigenmodes with energy participation. Replace each junction by its linear inductance \(L_J = (\Phi_0/2\pi)^2/E_J\) and solve the source-free Maxwell eigenproblem

$$ \nabla\times\big(\mu^{-1}\nabla\times\mathbf{E}\big) = \omega^2\varepsilon\,\mathbf{E} $$

for the linearised modes \(\omega_m\). The energy-participation ratio \(p_{mj}\), the fraction of mode \(m\)'s inductive energy stored in junction \(j\), then determines the nonlinear Hamiltonian. For a single junction [@minev2021epr],

$$ H = \sum_m \hbar\omega_m a_m^\dagger a_m - E_J\Big[\cos\hat\varphi_J + \tfrac{\hat\varphi_J^2}{2}\Big], \quad \hat\varphi_J = \sum_m \varphi_m (a_m + a_m^\dagger), \quad \varphi_m^2 = \frac{p_m \hbar\omega_m}{2E_J}, $$

and to leading order \(\alpha_m \simeq p_m^2 \hbar\omega_m^2/8E_J\) and \(\chi_{mn} \simeq p_m p_n \hbar\omega_m\omega_n/4E_J\). EPR is accurate for distributed structures and is the method behind pyEPR and the most common HFSS-based workflows.

Impedance (black-box) quantisation. Compute the admittance \(Y(\omega)\) seen by the junction port. Its zeros give the modes, with effective impedances \(Z_p = 2/(\omega_p\,\mathrm{Im}\,Y'(\omega_p))\) [@nigg2012]. Exact network synthesis makes the procedure rigorous for lossy, multiport environments [@solgun2014]. This formulation is natural for model-order reduction, because a pole–residue fit of \(Y(\omega)\) is what reduced-order models produce.

All three are implemented. Commercial solvers (Ansys HFSS and Q3D, now part of Synopsys; COMSOL; Sonnet; Keysight's QuantumPro EM) supply the field solutions [@web_keysight_qp]. Open-source projects supply the quantum layer and increasingly the solver too: scqubits, QuCAT and SQcircuit at the Hamiltonian level [@groszkowski2021; @gely2020; @rajabzadeh2023], QuTiP for dynamics [@johansson2013], Quantum Metal (formerly Qiskit Metal) and IQM's KQCircuits for parametrised layout [@quantummetal; @kqcircuits], AWS Palace as a scalable finite-element solver [@palace], and SQDMetal, which chains Quantum Metal, Gmsh and Palace [@sqdmetal2026]. SQuADDS adds a database of simulated and measured designs [@shanto2024]. The physics is settled. What is not settled is accuracy control and scale.

Accuracy requirements and their numerical cost

From @eq:sens, holding a frequency to \(\pm 10\) MHz requires relative errors in \(C_\Sigma\) and \(L_J\) of about \(4\times10^{-3}\). Two numerical facts make that harder than it looks. First, the electric field at the edge of a thin superconducting film diverges as \(r^{-1/2}\), so quantities that integrate \(|\mathbf{E}|^2\) near edges converge slowly under uniform mesh refinement and need adaptive or graded meshes [@jin2014]. This matters even more for surface-loss participation (§5). Second, kinetic inductance of thin films and the effective permittivity of the substrate are known only to a few percent. That model-form uncertainty is comparable to the target and cannot be reduced by refining the mesh. In current practice convergence is judged by eye, and extracted parameters are reported without uncertainty.

The scaling wall

A processor spans five decades of length: junction leads of about 0.1 µm, coplanar gaps of about 10 µm, qubit pads of a few hundred µm, resonators of several mm and a die of 10–20 mm. The die is comparable to the guided wavelength, so a single lumped model is invalid at die scale. Meanwhile the number of finite-element unknowns grows roughly as \(V/h^3\). Even with aggressive grading, a full-wave model of a 100-qubit die with its package runs to \(10^8\)–\(10^9\) unknowns, and a sparse direct solve in three dimensions costs \(O(N^2)\) time. Engineers therefore cut chips into pieces by hand and assume the pieces do not interact.

Classical EDA solved the analogous problem for interconnect with hierarchical extraction and passive model-order reduction. Krylov-subspace projection (PRIMA) preserves passivity [@odabasioglu1998]; vector fitting yields pole–residue models from sampled responses [@gustavsen1999]; the general theory is in [@antoulas2005]. Domain decomposition couples the pieces through their interfaces [@toselli2005]. Partition the die into cells \(c\) with interior unknowns \(\mathbf{u}_c\) and interface unknowns \(\mathbf{u}_\Gamma\). Eliminating the interiors gives the Schur complement

$$ \mathbf{S}(\omega)\,\mathbf{u}_\Gamma = \mathbf{f}_\Gamma, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Gamma} - \sum_c \mathbf{A}_{\Gamma c}\,\mathbf{A}_{cc}^{-1}\,\mathbf{A}_{c\Gamma}, $$

where each \(\mathbf{A}_{cc}^{-1}\) can be precomputed once per parametrised cell type and compressed. A processor contains a handful of cell types repeated hundreds of times, so the one-off cost of a cell library is amortised and the die-level cost becomes approximately linear in cell count. Two conditions are essential and are not met by current quantum tools. The reduced models must be passive, since a non-passive surrogate can predict unphysical gain and wrong mode frequencies. And the truncation, both of the reduced models and of weak long-range couplings, must carry an a posteriori error estimate that propagates into the uncertainty of \(\boldsymbol\theta\).

Inverse design and the role of learning

Designers actually need the inverse map: given targets \(\boldsymbol\theta^*\) with tolerances, find \(\mathbf{x}\). Two ingredients make this tractable. The first is adjoint sensitivity. For the discretised generalised eigenproblem \(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{e} = \lambda\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{e}\) with \(\lambda=\omega^2\),

$$ \frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial x_k} = \frac{\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf T}\Big(\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial x_k} - \lambda\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial x_k}\Big)\mathbf{e}}{\mathbf{e}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{B}\,\mathbf{e}} , $$

so the full gradient \(\partial\omega/\partial\mathbf{x}\) costs no more than one solve. That gradient is also the Jacobian \(\mathbf{J}=\partial\boldsymbol\theta/\partial\mathbf{x}\) needed for statistical sign-off in §4. The second ingredient is a surrogate. Over the parameter range of a cell, the forward map is smooth and expensive, which is the setting for Gaussian-process regression [@rasmussen2006] and Bayesian optimisation [@frazier2018]. Menke et al. used such methods to discover coupler circuits automatically [@menke2021]. For field-level surrogates, operator learning [@li2021fno; @lu2021deeponet] and mesh-based graph networks [@pfaff2021] are the natural architectures, and physics-informed losses can enforce the governing equations [@raissi2019].

Where learning helps and where it does not. A learned surrogate is useful for sign-off only if its error is bounded on the distribution of designs actually submitted. Two properties matter more than raw accuracy. The first is calibrated uncertainty: conformal prediction [@angelopoulos2023] gives distribution-free coverage guarantees given exchangeable calibration data, which suits a foundry's growing library of measured cells. The second is detection of distribution shift, when a new design or a new foundry leaves the training support. A surrogate without both should be used to propose designs, never to approve them. That distinction mirrors classical EDA, where fast estimators drive placement and only the slow, accurate extractor signs off.
