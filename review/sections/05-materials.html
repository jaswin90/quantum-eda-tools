Materials, interfaces and the coherence budget

Participation-weighted loss

The energy-relaxation rate of a transmon decomposes into independent channels:

$$ \frac1{T_1} = \omega\sum_i p_i\tan\delta_i \;+\; \Gamma^{\mathrm P} \;+\; \Gamma_{\mathrm{qp}} \;+\; \Gamma_{\mathrm{rad}} \;+\;\dots, \qquad p_i = \frac{\int_{V_i}\varepsilon_i|\mathbf E|^2\,\dd V}{\int_V\varepsilon|\mathbf E|^2\,\dd V}. $$

Here \(p_i\) is the fraction of electric energy stored in dielectric region \(i\): the bulk substrate, and the nanometre-thick metal–substrate (MS), substrate–air (SA) and metal–air (MA) interface layers. \(\tan\delta_i\) is that region's loss tangent [@martinis2005; @wenner2011; @wang2015; @gambetta2017]. The participation is a property of the geometry, and the loss tangent a property of the process. For a typical interface layer, \(p\sim10^{-4}\) and \(\tan\delta\sim10^{-3}\), so \(Q^{-1}\sim10^{-7}\) and \(T_1 = Q/\omega \approx 0.3\) ms at 5 GHz. Interfaces that hold one part in \(10^4\) of the field energy dominate coherence.

@eq:t1 explains the evolution of transmon geometry. Wider gaps and larger pads dilute the surface field, at the cost of footprint and stronger coupling to spurious modes. It also explains why materials changes can dominate design changes: tantalum films, by lowering the MA and MS loss tangents, raised transmon \(T_1\) above 0.3 ms and then toward 0.5 ms [@place2021; @wang2022ta].

Two-level systems and the stochasticity of \(T_1\)

Interface loss is dominated by two-level systems (TLS), atomic-scale tunnelling defects in amorphous oxides [@muller2019]. In the standard tunnelling model their contribution saturates with power and temperature:

$$ \tan\delta_{\mathrm{TLS}}(\bar n,T) = \tan\delta_0\,\frac{\tanh\!\big(\hbar\omega/2k_BT\big)}{\sqrt{1+\bar n/n_c}}, $$

which is how resonator measurements separate TLS loss from other channels [@mcrae2020]. Individual near-resonant TLS also diffuse spectrally, so a qubit's \(T_1\) fluctuates by large factors on timescales of minutes to hours [@klimov2018]. For yield and for error correction this matters more than the mean: \(T_1\) is a random process, and a die's coherence is a distribution over both space and time. Correlated losses from quasiparticles generated by ionising radiation produce bursts of errors across a chip [@vepsalainen2020; @mcewen2022]. These violate the independent-noise assumptions of most error-correction analyses.

From recipe to coherence

Reviews of materials for quantum hardware [@deleon2021; @siddiqi2021; @murray2021] converge on the same diagnosis: process steps (surface cleans, etch chemistry, film deposition, ambient exposure) set the interface loss tangents, and these are measured inconsistently across labs. Two things are missing from the design flow. The first is a foundry-published set of interface loss tangents \(\{\tan\delta_i\}\), measured on standard resonator test structures and versioned with the process. These would be the quantum equivalent of the device-parameter tables in a classical PDK. The second is a design-time tool that computes \(\{p_i\}\) for any layout, with the singular-field accuracy of §3.4, and reports a \(T_1\) budget by channel. Together they would let designers trade footprint against coherence knowingly, and give the fab a quantitative target.

Learning the map from process parameters and inline metrology (film resistivity, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, cross-sectional TEM, resonator \(Q\)) to \(\tan\delta_i\) is a multi-fidelity regression problem with few, expensive, confounded samples. Process recipes are chosen deliberately, not randomly, so a naive regression conflates correlation with effect. This is a setting for designed experiments and Bayesian modelling with physical priors, not for large unsupervised models. It is also the slowest of the problems in this review to commercialise, because it needs a foundry's data and its cooperation.
