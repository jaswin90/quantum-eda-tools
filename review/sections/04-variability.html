Variability, frequency allocation and yield

Junction physics: why sub-ångström variation matters

The Ambegaokar–Baratoff relation ties the critical current of a superconductor–insulator–superconductor junction to its normal-state resistance [@ambegaokar1963]. At \(T \ll T_c\),

$$ I_c R_n = \frac{\pi\Delta}{2e} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_J = \frac{\hbar I_c}{2e} = \frac{R_Q}{2R_n}\,\Delta, $$

with \(\Delta\) the superconducting gap (about 180 µeV for thin-film aluminium, so \(\Delta/h \approx 44\) GHz). The resistance \(R_n\) can be measured at room temperature in milliseconds, which is what makes warm screening of qubit frequencies possible. The junction is an aluminium-oxide tunnel barrier about 1–2 nm thick. For a rectangular barrier of height \(U\), thickness \(d\) and area \(A\), the WKB tunnelling probability gives [@simmons1963]

$$ I_c \propto \frac{1}{R_n} \propto A\, e^{-2\kappa d}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2 m^* U}}{\hbar} \approx 5\ \mathrm{nm}^{-1}\ \ (U\approx 1\ \mathrm{eV}), $$

so that

$$ \frac{\delta I_c}{I_c} = \frac{\delta A}{A} - 2\kappa\,\delta d, \qquad \frac{\delta f}{f} \approx \frac12\frac{\delta I_c}{I_c}. $$

A change of 0.01 nm in the mean barrier thickness, a few percent of one atomic layer, changes \(I_c\) by about 10% and moves a 5 GHz qubit by about 250 MHz (@fig:junction). Real barriers are amorphous and have a distribution of local thicknesses, and transport is dominated by the thinnest regions, so the effective \(\kappa\) differs from the idealised value. The exponential sensitivity, however, is robust, and it is the root of the problem.

Area variation adds a second, geometric term. By analogy with transistor mismatch, where threshold-voltage spread scales as \(\sigma \propto 1/\sqrt{A}\) for uncorrelated local fluctuations [@pelgrom1989], small junctions are intrinsically less reproducible. Measured wafer-scale resistance spreads are of order a few percent, with a smooth gradient across the wafer plus a local random component [@kreikebaum2020]. Junction resistance also drifts for days after fabrication as the oxide relaxes. The process matters: shadow-evaporated Dolan-bridge junctions [@dolan1977] are flexible but sensitive to resist geometry, while the subtractive 300 mm flows now entering production trade flexibility for uniformity [@vandamme2024].

Combining @eq:jjsens with a resistance spread \(\sigma_R/R\) of 2–4% gives \(\sigma_f/f \approx 1\text{–}2\%\), or \(\sigma_f \approx 50\text{–}100\) MHz at 5 GHz. Collision windows are a few to a few tens of MHz. As fabricated, fixed-frequency transmons therefore miss their targets by more than the tolerance. Post-fabrication tuning or tunable elements are mandatory, not optional.

The frequency-collision model

For fixed-frequency transmons driven by cross-resonance, IBM identified a set of forbidden frequency relations between a control qubit \(j\), a target \(k\) and their spectators [@brink2018; @hertzberg2021]. They include near-degeneracy \(|f_j - f_k| \lt \delta_1\); near-resonance of the control's two-photon \(0\to2\) transition with the target, \(|f_j - f_k - \alpha/2| \lt \delta_2\); near-resonance of a \(1\to2\) transition, \(|f_j - f_k - \alpha| \lt \delta_3\); the requirement that the pair lie in the straddling regime \(|f_j-f_k| \lt |\alpha|\); and further conditions among spectators that share a control. Window widths \(\delta_k\) of a few to a few tens of MHz are set by the gate time and the tolerated error.

Let \(\Delta_e = f_i - f_j\) be the detuning on coupler edge \(e\), with design value \(\mu_e\) and random fabrication error. If frequency errors are independent Gaussians of standard deviation \(\sigma_f\), then \(\Delta_e \sim \mathcal N(\mu_e, 2\sigma_f^2)\), and the probability that edge \(e\) falls into window \(k\) (centre \(c_k\), half-width \(\delta_k\)) is

$$ p_{e,k} = \Phi\!\left(\frac{c_k + \delta_k - \mu_e}{\sqrt2\,\sigma_f}\right) - \Phi\!\left(\frac{c_k - \delta_k - \mu_e}{\sqrt2\,\sigma_f}\right). $$

Treating edges as independent, the probability that a chip with edge set \(E\) has no collision is

$$ Y_{\mathrm{coll}} = \prod_{e\in E}\Big(1 - \sum_k p_{e,k}\Big) \;\approx\; \exp\!\Big(-\sum_{e,k} p_{e,k}\Big). $$

@fig:yield evaluates @eq:yield for heavy-hexagon edge counts at 65, 127 and 1,000 qubits. The yield curve is a cliff whose position moves left as the chip grows. In this model, at \(\sigma_f = 14\) MHz (the order of the post-anneal precision IBM reports [@hertzberg2021; @zhang2022]) the zero-collision yield is about 94% at 65 qubits, 89% at 127 and 40% at 1,000. At 20 MHz it is 49%, 24% and essentially zero.

Two features of @eq:yield deserve emphasis for a statistically minded reader. First, the expected number of collisions is \(\lambda_{\mathrm{coll}} = \sum_{e,k}p_{e,k}\), which is linear in edge count. Holding yield fixed while the chip grows tenfold requires each \(p_{e,k}\) to fall tenfold. Because \(p_{e,k}\) is a Gaussian tail probability, a tenfold reduction needs only a modest reduction in \(\sigma_f\), but it is entirely a statement about the tails. Second, those tails are exactly where the Gaussian model is least trustworthy. Outlier junctions from particles, resist defects or oxide inhomogeneity produce heavy tails, and spatial correlation couples neighbouring edges. A yield model for 1,000-qubit chips must therefore be fitted to the measured tail behaviour of a specific line, not assumed.

Frequency allocation as optimisation

Published approaches choose a small set of frequency classes and assign them to the lattice so that the nominal pattern avoids all windows. Morvan et al. cast allocation as an optimisation over the lattice [@morvan2022], and later work studies mitigation strategies [@osman2023]. These methods generally assume a single \(\sigma_f\) and fix the pattern before considering post-fabrication tuning. A formulation that uses what a foundry actually knows is a chance-constrained programme:

$$ \max_{\boldsymbol\mu\in[f_{\mathrm{lo}},f_{\mathrm{hi}}]^N} \ \Pr_{\boldsymbol\delta}\Big[\,\boldsymbol\mu + \boldsymbol\delta - \mathbf{s}(\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\delta) \notin \mathcal C \,\Big], \qquad \boldsymbol\delta \sim \mathcal N\big(\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r}),\, \boldsymbol\Sigma_f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}')\big), $$

where \(\mathcal C\) is the union of forbidden regions, \(\mathbf{m}(\mathbf{r})\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_f(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}')\) are the spatial mean and covariance of fabrication error at die positions \(\mathbf{r}\), and \(\mathbf{s}(\cdot)\) is the post-fabrication trim policy (below). Chance constraints of this kind are non-convex in general, since \(\mathcal C\) is a union of disjoint slabs. They are nonetheless tractable by sample-average approximation, which replaces the probability with an empirical frequency over sampled \(\boldsymbol\delta\) and solves the resulting mixed-integer programme [@luedtke2008], or by convex conservative approximations [@nemirovski2006]. The spatial covariance matters because collisions only involve neighbours, and neighbouring junctions are positively correlated, which reduces the variance of their difference. Using a fitted \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_f\) instead of an isotropic \(\sigma_f\) is therefore not a refinement; it can change the optimal pattern.

Post-fabrication trimming

IBM adjusts individual junctions by laser annealing, which raises \(R_n\) and so lowers \(f\) by a controlled amount [@hertzberg2021; @zhang2022]. The move is one-sided and bounded. Given predicted frequencies \(\hat{\mathbf f}\) from warm resistance measurements, the trim plan solves

$$ \min_{\mathbf s} \ \sum_{e\in E}\mathbb 1\big[\hat f_i - s_i - (\hat f_j - s_j) \in \mathcal C_e\big] \quad \text{s.t.}\quad 0 \le s_i \le s_{\max}, \ \ \sum_i \mathbb 1[s_i \gt 0] \le K, $$

an integer programme on the coupling graph. With uncertainty in \(\hat{\mathbf f}\), the objective becomes the expected number of collisions under the posterior of §4.5. IBM's results show that the physics works on one company's line. They are not yet a method that a foundry customer can apply without that company's process knowledge.

A hierarchical model for warm-to-cold prediction

Semiconductor manufacturing models wafer-level variation as a smooth spatial trend plus nested random effects, and estimates it by kriging, which is Gaussian-process regression [@rasmussen2006; @orshansky2008]. For junction \(i\) on die \(d\) of wafer \(w\),

$$ \log R_{n,i} = m_w(\mathbf r_i) + u_d + \epsilon_i, \qquad m_w \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf z_w, k_\ell\big), \quad u_d\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau_d^2), \quad \epsilon_i \sim t_\nu(0,\tau_\epsilon^2), $$

with a Student-\(t\) residual to accommodate the heavy tails discussed above, and with process covariates \(\mathbf z_w\) (oxidation pressure and time, resist thickness). Through @eq:ab and @eq:freq the posterior on \(R_{n,i}\) induces a posterior on each cold frequency, \(p(f_i\mid\mathcal D)\). The same model supplies \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_f\) for @eq:ccp, the uncertainty for @eq:trim, and a known-good-die rule: package die \(d\) only if \(\Pr(\text{no collision}\mid\mathcal D) \ge P_{\min}\). This is the yield-management layer that classical fabs buy as a product [@stapper1983; @wu2015wafer]. No quantum equivalent exists.

Statistical sign-off

Classical design moved from corner-based to statistical static timing analysis when variability became comparable to the margins [@blaauw2008]. The quantum analogue is direct. Let \(\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf x_0,\boldsymbol\Sigma_x)\) describe geometric and junction variation as characterised by the foundry. To first order,

$$ \boldsymbol\Sigma_\theta \simeq \mathbf J\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_x\,\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \mathbf J = \frac{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}{\partial\mathbf x}\Big|_{\mathbf x_0}, $$

with \(\mathbf J\) obtained by the adjoint method of @eq:adjoint. A layout passes sign-off if

$$ \Pr_{\mathbf x}\big[\,|\boldsymbol\theta(\mathbf x)-\boldsymbol\theta^*| \le \boldsymbol\tau \ \wedge\ \boldsymbol\theta(\mathbf x)\notin\mathcal C\,\big] \ \ge\ Y_{\min}. $$

The linearisation fails exactly where it matters most, in the tails near collision boundaries. A practical implementation therefore needs importance sampling or subset simulation for the rare-event probability, using the surrogate of §3.6 with conformal error bounds to keep the cost manageable.
