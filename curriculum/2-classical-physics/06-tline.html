Transmission lines, resonators and microwave networks

Goals

  • Understand when a wire must be treated as a transmission line, and derive the telegrapher's equations.
  • Find the characteristic impedance and phase velocity of a line, including the coplanar waveguide used on chips.
  • Compute reflections, standing waves and input impedances, and use S-parameters.
  • Design quarter- and half-wave resonators and read a resonator's transmission trace.
  • See how a distributed resonator reduces to an LC circuit near resonance, which is what lets us quantise it.

Every signal that reaches a superconducting qubit, and every measurement that comes back, travels along a transmission line: coaxial cables in the fridge, then flat "coplanar" lines on the chip. The readout resonators themselves are short pieces of transmission line. This chapter is the working microwave engineering a quantum-chip designer needs: what impedance means for a wave, why mismatches reflect, how a few millimetres of line become a resonator, and how S-parameters describe any component. The mathematics is Chapter 3's oscillators and Chapter 5's waves, combined.

When wires become waveguides

In ordinary circuit theory a wire is an ideal connection: the voltage is the same everywhere along it, instantly. That is an approximation, valid when the circuit is much smaller than the wavelength, so that the signal has the same phase everywhere at any instant. Such circuits are called lumped. At 5 GHz on a chip the wavelength is about 24 mm, and a readout resonator is several millimetres long, a sizeable fraction of a wavelength. The voltage then varies along the wire, and we must treat it as a transmission line: two conductors (a signal and a ground) that guide a wave.

A rough rule: if a structure is longer than about a tenth of a wavelength, think in terms of waves. Below that, lumped circuits work. On a chip at 5 GHz, a tenth of a wavelength is about 2 mm. Qubit pads (0.5 mm) are lumped; resonators and feedlines (several mm) are not.

Model a short piece of line, of length \(\dd z\), as a tiny series inductor \(\mathcal L\,\dd z\) (current flowing along the line creates magnetic flux) and a tiny shunt capacitor \(\mathcal C\,\dd z\) (signal and ground conductors face each other). Here \(\mathcal L\) and \(\mathcal C\) are the inductance and capacitance per unit length, computed from the cross-section with Chapter 4's methods. A line is a long chain of these LC cells. Kirchhoff's voltage law across the inductor and current law at the capacitor give the telegrapher's equations:

$$ \frac{\partial V}{\partial z} = -\mathcal L\frac{\partial I}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial I}{\partial z} = -\mathcal C\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} . $$

Differentiate the first with respect to \(z\) and substitute the second: \(\partial_z^2V = \mathcal L\mathcal C\,\partial_t^2V\). This is the wave equation of Chapter 5. Its solutions travel in both directions at the phase velocity \(v_p = 1/\sqrt{\mathcal L\mathcal C}\). For a wave travelling in the \(+z\) direction, the voltage and current are locked together in a fixed ratio:

$$ Z_0 = \frac{V^+}{I^+} = \sqrt{\frac{\mathcal L}{\mathcal C}}, \qquad v_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal L\mathcal C}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}}} . $$

\(Z_0\) is the characteristic impedance. It is a real number in ohms for a lossless line, yet no energy is dissipated: it simply says how much current a given voltage drives into the line, with the energy carried away down the line rather than turned into heat. (Compare the characteristic impedance \(\sqrt{L/C}\) of an LC oscillator in Chapter 2; this is the per-length version.) Microwave equipment is standardised at 50 Ω, a compromise between the minimum-loss and maximum-power impedances of air coax. A wave going the other way has \(V^-/I^- = -Z_0\) (the current flows backwards).

The quantity \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is an effective permittivity: if the field lives partly in substrate and partly in air, the wave travels at a speed in between.

Real lines have loss: a small series resistance and shunt conductance per length. These make the propagation constant complex, \(\gamma = \alpha + \ii\beta\): the wave varies as \(\ee^{-\gamma z}\), so \(\beta = 2\pi/\lambda\) sets the phase and \(\alpha\) the attenuation per unit length. Cables in a dilution refrigerator are deliberately lossy on input lines (to attenuate thermal noise, Chapter 8) and as low-loss as possible on output lines.

The coplanar waveguide

Superconducting chips use the coplanar waveguide (CPW): a centre strip of width \(w\) separated by gaps \(s\) from ground planes on either side, all in one metal layer on a substrate. Viewed from above it looks like a single line with a thin slot on each side. It is popular because it is made in a single lithography step (no vias), and its ground planes are right next to the signal, which keeps the field confined near the line. For a substrate much thicker than the gaps, conformal mapping gives [@simons2001; @goppl2008]

$$ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx \frac{1+\varepsilon_r}{2}, \qquad Z_0 = \frac{30\pi}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}}}\,\frac{K(k')}{K(k)}, \qquad k = \frac{w}{w+2s}, \quad k' = \sqrt{1-k^2}, $$

where \(K\) is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind (a standard special function; every numerical library has it). Three things to notice:

  • Half the field is in the substrate and half in the vacuum above, hence \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx(1+\varepsilon_r)/2\approx6.2\) on silicon.
  • A centre width of 10 µm with 6 µm gaps on silicon gives close to 50 Ω.
  • Only the ratio \(k = w/(w+2s)\) matters, so the same impedance can be made at many sizes. Smaller lines save area; larger lines put less field at the lossy metal edges (Chapter 4), giving higher \(Q_i\). Wider gaps raise \(Z_0\).

A superconducting CPW also has kinetic inductance (Chapter 5), which adds to \(\mathcal L\), raises \(Z_0\) and lowers \(v_p\), by a fraction that depends on film thickness and temperature. For thick aluminium or niobium it is a percent-level correction; for thin NbTiN it can dominate.

Reflections and input impedance

What happens when a wave reaches the end of a line, where a load \(Z_L\) is connected? At the load, the total voltage and current must satisfy \(V = Z_LI\). A single forward wave has \(V/I = Z_0\), so if \(Z_L\ne Z_0\) a reflected wave must appear to make up the difference. Writing \(V = V^+ + V^-\) and \(I = (V^+ - V^-)/Z_0\) and imposing \(V = Z_LI\) gives the reflection coefficient

$$ \Gamma = \frac{V^-}{V^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} . $$

The forward and reflected waves interfere and form a standing wave: points where they add (voltage maxima) and points where they cancel (minima), spaced by \(\lambda/2\). The ratio of maximum to minimum, the voltage standing-wave ratio, is \((1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)\).

Seen from a distance \(\ell\) back along the line, the load is transformed by the round-trip phase. It looks like the input impedance

$$ Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + \ii Z_0\tan\beta\ell}{Z_0 + \ii Z_L\tan\beta\ell}, \qquad \beta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} . $$

This chapter follows the microwave-engineering convention \(\ee^{+j\omega t}\), writing \(j\) as \(\ii\), so an inductor has impedance \(+\ii\omega L\) and a capacitor \(1/\ii\omega C\), as in [@pozar2011]. Chapters 3 and 5 and the quantum books use the physicists' \(\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\). The physics is the same; only the sign of \(\ii\) flips. When comparing formulas from different sources, check the convention first.

Three special cases explain most resonator designs:

  • A short-circuited line of length \(\ell\) has \(Z_{\mathrm{in}} = \ii Z_0\tan\beta\ell\). When short it looks like an inductor; at \(\ell = \lambda/4\) its impedance is infinite (an open circuit).
  • An open-circuited line has \(Z_{\mathrm{in}} = -\ii Z_0\cot\beta\ell\). When short it looks like a capacitor; at \(\ell = \lambda/4\) it looks like a short.
  • A quarter-wave section transforms any load to \(Z_0^2/Z_L\), turning shorts into opens and vice versa. This "impedance inverter" appears in filters and matching networks everywhere.

Transmission-line resonators

A length of line with reflecting ends traps waves bouncing back and forth. Resonance occurs when a whole number of round trips fits, like a guitar string:

  • Both ends open (or both shorted): \(\ell = n\lambda/2\), a half-wave resonator, \(f_n = nv_p/2\ell\).
  • One end shorted, the other open: \(\ell = (2n-1)\lambda/4\), a quarter-wave resonator, \(f_n = (2n-1)v_p/4\ell\). Half as long for the same frequency, and its higher modes are at 3, 5, 7… times the fundamental.

Most readout resonators on transmon chips are quarter-wave CPW lines a few millimetres long, meandered to save area, shorted to ground at one end (where the current is maximal) and capacitively coupled to a qubit at the other (where the voltage is maximal, giving the strongest capacitive coupling).

Near its fundamental resonance a transmission-line resonator behaves exactly like a lumped LC circuit. For a half-wave resonator (open ends), expanding the input impedance near \(\omega_0\) gives an equivalent parallel LC with

$$ C_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{\mathcal C\,\ell}{2}, \qquad L_{\mathrm{eq}} = \frac{2\mathcal L\ell}{\pi^2}, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{eq}}C_{\mathrm{eq}}}} = \frac{\pi v_p}{\ell} . $$

Where does the \(1/2\) in \(C_{\mathrm{eq}}\) come from? The voltage along the resonator follows a standing-wave pattern \(\cos(\pi z/\ell)\), and the stored energy involves the average of \(\cos^2\), which is \(\tfrac12\). Each higher mode is another independent LC oscillator with its own \(C_{\mathrm{eq}}\) and \(L_{\mathrm{eq}}\).

This is the route by which a distributed electromagnetic structure becomes a set of harmonic oscillators, each of which can be quantised (Book 5 for photons, Book 6 for circuits). The review's energy-participation and black-box methods are systematic versions of this reduction (@lf:sec-design-extract).

Worked example: equivalent circuit of a resonator. A half-wave CPW resonator on silicon, \(Z_0 = 50\) Ω, \(v_p = 1.2\times10^8\) m/s, 7 GHz. Its length is \(\ell = v_p/2f = 8.6\) mm. Per-length values: \(\mathcal C = 1/(Z_0v_p) = 167\) pF/m and \(\mathcal L = Z_0/v_p = 417\) nH/m. So \(C_{\mathrm{eq}} = 167\times10^{-12}\times8.6\times10^{-3}/2 = 0.72\) pF and \(L_{\mathrm{eq}} = 2\times417\times10^{-9}\times8.6\times10^{-3}/\pi^2 = 0.73\) nH. Check: \(1/2\pi\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{eq}}C_{\mathrm{eq}}}\approx7.0\) GHz. The resonator's impedance \(\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{eq}}/C_{\mathrm{eq}}}\approx32\) Ω is far below \(R_Q\): it is a very linear, "classical-looking" oscillator, as a readout resonator should be.

S-parameters

For a microwave component, voltages and currents are awkward to measure, but incoming and outgoing waves are natural. A component with \(N\) ports (connection points) is described by its scattering matrix: the amplitudes of waves leaving each port, \(\mathbf b\), in terms of those entering, \(\mathbf a\), normalised so that \(|a_i|^2\) is power:

$$ \mathbf b = S\,\mathbf a, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix}S_{11} & S_{12}\\ S_{21} & S_{22}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{(two ports)}. $$

\(S_{11}\) is the reflection at port 1 (the \(\Gamma\) of the previous section) and \(S_{21}\) the transmission from port 1 to port 2. S-parameters are usually quoted in decibels, \(20\log_{10}|S_{ij}|\). Two general properties:

  • A lossless network has a unitary S-matrix, \(S^\dagger S = I\): every watt that goes in comes out somewhere.
  • A reciprocal network (anything made of ordinary conductors and dielectrics, without magnets) has \(S = S^{\mathsf T}\): transmission from 1 to 2 equals transmission from 2 to 1. Circulators and isolators, which use magnetised ferrites, break reciprocity on purpose.

Network analysers measure S-parameters directly, and electromagnetic solvers compute them. Chaining components is done with the related ABCD (transfer) matrices, which simply multiply. The unitarity of a lossless S-matrix is the same mathematics as the unitarity of a quantum gate; a beam splitter in Book 5 is a \(2\times2\) unitary S-matrix.

A resonator side-coupled to a through line (a "hanger" or "notch" geometry, used for multiplexed readout of many resonators on one feedline, each at a slightly different frequency) has, near resonance,

$$ S_{21}(\omega) = 1 - \frac{Q_L/Q_e}{1 + 2\ii Q_L\,\dfrac{\omega-\omega_0}{\omega_0}} . $$

Far from resonance the signal passes untouched (\(S_{21} = 1\)). At \(\omega_0\) the resonator absorbs and re-radiates, and the re-radiated wave interferes destructively with the through wave: \(S_{21}\) dips to \(S_{21}^{\min} = Q_L/Q_i\). In the complex plane it traces a circle (@fig:c-notch), just like the reflection circle of Chapter 3. Fitting the circle gives \(\omega_0\), \(Q_i\) and \(Q_e\), and is the standard measurement of materials loss [@mcrae2020]. Real data add an impedance mismatch that rotates the circle and a cable delay that winds it, both corrected in the fit.

Try it in Python

Compute CPW impedances from the elliptic-integral formula, the length of a quarter-wave resonator, and watch the input impedance of a shorted line blow up at a quarter wavelength.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import ellipk     # note: scipy's ellipk takes m = k**2

c0 = 299792458.0

def cpw(w, s, eps_r):
    """Impedance and phase velocity of a CPW on a thick substrate."""
    k = w / (w + 2 * s)
    kp = np.sqrt(1 - k**2)
    eps_eff = (1 + eps_r) / 2
    Z0 = 30 * np.pi / np.sqrt(eps_eff) * ellipk(kp**2) / ellipk(k**2)
    return Z0, c0 / np.sqrt(eps_eff)

for w, s in [(10e-6, 6e-6), (20e-6, 12e-6), (4e-6, 2.4e-6), (10e-6, 20e-6)]:
    Z0, vp = cpw(w, s, 11.45)
    print(f"w = {w*1e6:4.1f} um, s = {s*1e6:4.1f} um:  Z0 = {Z0:5.1f} ohm")

Z0, vp = cpw(10e-6, 6e-6, 11.45)
f0 = 7e9
print(f"quarter-wave length at {f0/1e9} GHz: {vp / f0 / 4 * 1e3:.2f} mm")

# Input impedance of a shorted line near a quarter wavelength
L = vp / f0 / 4
for f in (6.9e9, 6.99e9, 7.0e9 - 1e3, 7.01e9, 7.1e9):
    beta = 2 * np.pi * f / vp
    print(f"f = {f/1e9:.5f} GHz: Zin = {Z0 * np.tan(beta * L):12.1f} j ohm")

The first three lines have the same ratio \(w/(w+2s)\) and the same 51 Ω. The input impedance is large and inductive just below 7 GHz, flips sign through infinity, and is capacitive just above: exactly the behaviour of a parallel LC circuit at resonance, which is the content of @eq:c-lceq.

A superconducting chip's microwave design is a transmission-line problem: feedlines, readout resonators, Purcell filters, coupling buses, flux and drive lines, wirebond pads and the package. The S-parameters of the whole assembly, simulated before fabrication, predict resonator frequencies, coupling \(Q\)s and crosstalk. The quantum parameters (qubit–resonator coupling \(g\), Purcell decay) are derived from these classical responses (@lf:eq-chi).

  • Longer than about \(\lambda/10\): treat as a transmission line. Telegrapher's equations give waves at \(v_p = 1/\sqrt{\mathcal L\mathcal C}\) with \(Z_0 = \sqrt{\mathcal L/\mathcal C}\).
  • CPW: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}\approx(1+\varepsilon_r)/2\); \(Z_0\) depends only on \(w/(w+2s)\).
  • \(\Gamma = (Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)\); a line transforms impedances; a quarter wave inverts them.
  • Half-wave and quarter-wave resonators; near resonance each mode is an LC circuit with \(C_{\mathrm{eq}} = \mathcal C\ell/2\).
  • S-matrices: unitary if lossless, symmetric if reciprocal; a notch resonator's \(S_{21}\) circle gives \(Q_i\) and \(Q_e\).

Exercises

  1. A CPW on silicon has \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}} = 6.2\). How long is a quarter-wave resonator at 7 GHz?
    Solution

    \(v_p = 3\times10^8/\sqrt{6.2} = 1.205\times10^8\) m/s, \(\lambda = v_p/f = 17.2\) mm, \(\ell = \lambda/4 = 4.3\) mm.

  2. A 50 Ω line is terminated by 75 Ω. What fraction of incident power is reflected?
    Solution

    \(\Gamma = 25/125 = 0.2\), reflected power \(|\Gamma|^2 = 4\%\), about \(-14\) dB return loss.

  3. Show from @eq:c-zin that a shorted line of length \(\ell\ll\lambda\) looks like an inductor \(L = \mathcal L\ell\).
    Solution

    \(Z_{\mathrm{in}} = \ii Z_0\tan\beta\ell\approx\ii Z_0\beta\ell = \ii\sqrt{\mathcal L/\mathcal C}\,\omega\sqrt{\mathcal L\mathcal C}\,\ell = \ii\omega\mathcal L\ell\), the impedance of an inductor \(\mathcal L\ell\) in the engineering convention.

  4. For the notch resonator, find the minimum of \(|S_{21}|\) and show it equals \(Q_L/Q_i\). For \(Q_i = 10^5\), \(Q_e = 2\times10^4\), what is the dip in dB?
    Solution

    At \(\omega = \omega_0\), \(S_{21} = 1 - Q_L/Q_e\). Since \(1/Q_L = 1/Q_i + 1/Q_e\), \(1 - Q_L/Q_e = Q_L/Q_i\). Here \(Q_L = 16667\), \(S_{21}^{\min} = 0.167\), i.e. \(-15.6\) dB.

  5. Show that \(S = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \ee^{\ii\phi}\\ \ee^{\ii\phi} & 0\end{pmatrix}\) (a matched line of electrical length \(\phi\)) is unitary.
    Solution

    \(S^\dagger S = \begin{pmatrix}0&\ee^{-\ii\phi}\\ \ee^{-\ii\phi}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\ee^{\ii\phi}\\ \ee^{\ii\phi}&0\end{pmatrix} = I\).

  6. A quarter-wave section of 70.7 Ω line connects a 50 Ω line to a 100 Ω load. What impedance does the 50 Ω line see?
    Solution

    \(Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_0^2/Z_L = 5000/100 = 50\) Ω: a perfect match (at the design frequency). This is the quarter-wave transformer.

  7. Ten readout resonators share one feedline, spaced 50 MHz apart around 7 GHz, each with \(Q_L = 10^4\). Are their dips well separated?
    Solution

    Linewidth \(7\times10^9/10^4 = 0.7\) MHz, much less than the 50 MHz spacing: yes, about 70 linewidths apart. This is frequency multiplexing, how one line reads many qubits.
