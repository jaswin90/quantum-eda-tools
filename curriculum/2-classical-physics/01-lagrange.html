From Newton to Lagrange: the principle of least action

Goals

  • Understand why physicists rewrite Newton's laws in terms of energy, and what "least action" means.
  • Derive the Euler–Lagrange equation step by step, and use it with any convenient coordinates.
  • Write the Lagrangian of an electrical circuit using node fluxes, the first step in designing a superconducting qubit.
  • Connect symmetries to conserved quantities, including the conserved charge on a superconducting island.

Newton's \(F = ma\) is perfect for a ball on a table. It becomes clumsy for a pendulum (forces in the string you do not care about), for constrained motion, and especially for things that are not particles at all, such as the voltages and currents in a circuit. The Lagrangian method replaces forces with energies: write down the kinetic and potential energy of the system in whatever variables are natural, apply one universal equation, and the equations of motion fall out. It also provides exactly what quantum mechanics needs to "quantise" a system. For superconducting quantum chips this is not a curiosity: the Hamiltonian of every qubit in the review is derived by first writing the circuit's Lagrangian, as this chapter shows.

Why reformulate Newton

Newton's second law, \(m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F\), says acceleration equals force over mass. Three situations make it awkward:

  • Natural variables are not positions. A pendulum is described by an angle; a rotating object by angles; a circuit by voltages and currents.
  • Constraints. A bead on a wire or a pendulum bob on a rod is pushed by forces (from the wire, the rod) that you neither know nor care about.
  • Quantum mechanics. To build a quantum description you need the system's energy function and a pair of variables, a coordinate and its "conjugate momentum", to turn into operators. Forces do not provide these.

The Lagrangian formulation fixes all three. Its only ingredients are the kinetic energy \(T\) and potential energy \(V\), written in any coordinates you like.

The action and the Euler–Lagrange equation

For a system described by one coordinate \(q(t)\) (a position, an angle, a flux), define the Lagrangian as kinetic minus potential energy, \(L(q,\dot q) = T - V\). For any imagined path \(q(t)\) from time \(t_1\) to \(t_2\), define its action:

$$ S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L\big(q(t), \dot q(t)\big)\,\dd t . $$

The action is a single number for each path. The principle of stationary action says: of all paths that start and end at the given points, the one nature actually follows is the one for which small changes to the path do not change \(S\) (to first order), like the bottom of a valley where the ground is locally flat.

Let us turn that into an equation. Change the path slightly, \(q\to q+\delta q\), keeping the end points fixed (\(\delta q(t_1)=\delta q(t_2)=0\)). To first order the Lagrangian changes by \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\delta\dot q\), so

\[ \delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\frac{\dd(\delta q)}{\dd t}\Big)\dd t . \]

Integrate the second term by parts. The boundary term \(\big[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\delta q\big]_{t_1}^{t_2}\) vanishes because \(\delta q\) is zero at both ends, leaving

\[ \delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} - \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\Big)\delta q\,\dd t . \]

For this to vanish for every small change \(\delta q(t)\), the bracket must be zero at every moment. That gives the Euler–Lagrange equation:

$$ \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0 . $$

Worked example: recovering Newton. A particle with \(L = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 - V(x)\). Then \(\partial L/\partial\dot x = m\dot x\) (momentum), so \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x} = m\ddot x\); and \(\partial L/\partial x = -V'(x)\), which is the force \(F\). The Euler–Lagrange equation reads \(m\ddot x - (-V') = 0\), i.e. \(m\ddot x = F\). Newton's law is recovered. For several coordinates \(q_1,\dots,q_n\), there is one Euler–Lagrange equation per coordinate.

Why "least" action? The real path makes \(S\) stationary, not always a minimum. The deeper reason it is singled out comes from quantum mechanics: Feynman showed that a quantum particle explores every path, each with a complex amplitude \(\ee^{\ii S/\hbar}\) (Book 1, Chapter 1). Paths near a stationary point have nearly equal phases and add up; paths elsewhere have wildly varying phases and cancel. When \(S\) is huge compared with \(\hbar\), only the neighbourhood of the classical path survives. Classical mechanics is the constructive-interference limit of quantum mechanics.

Generalised coordinates

The power of @eq:c-el is that \(q\) can be any set of numbers that pins down the configuration: angles, distances, fluxes. We call them generalised coordinates. Constraint forces never appear, because the coordinates already respect the constraints.

Worked example: the pendulum. A mass \(m\) on a rigid rod of length \(\ell\), angle \(\theta\) from vertical. Speed \(\ell\dot\theta\), so \(T = \tfrac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2\); height \(-\ell\cos\theta\), so \(V = -mg\ell\cos\theta\). Then \(\partial L/\partial\dot\theta = m\ell^2\dot\theta\) and \(\partial L/\partial\theta = -mg\ell\sin\theta\), giving

\[ m\ell^2\ddot\theta = -mg\ell\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{\ell}\sin\theta . \]

We never needed the tension in the rod. For small angles \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\) and the pendulum is a harmonic oscillator with \(\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}\). For large angles it is nonlinear: its frequency drops as the swing grows. Remember this example: the energy of a Josephson junction is also \(-E_J\cos\varphi\), so a transmon qubit is, mathematically, a quantum pendulum (Book 6), and its nonlinearity is exactly the pendulum's.

The Lagrangian of a circuit

Circuits obey the same mathematics. The trick is choosing the right coordinate. For each circuit node (a point where components meet), define the node flux as the running time integral of its voltage relative to ground:

$$ \Phi(t) = \int_{-\infty}^t V(t')\,\dd t', \qquad V = \dot\Phi . $$

(It is called a "flux" because, by Faraday's law, a voltage is a rate of change of magnetic flux, Chapter 5.) Now look at the energies:

  • A capacitor stores \(\tfrac12CV^2 = \tfrac12C\dot\Phi^2\). It depends on the rate of change of \(\Phi\), like kinetic energy \(\tfrac12m\dot x^2\). So capacitance plays the role of mass.
  • An inductor obeys \(V = L\dot I\); integrating, \(\Phi = LI\), so it stores \(\tfrac12LI^2 = \Phi^2/2L\). It depends on \(\Phi\) itself, like the potential energy \(\tfrac12kx^2\) of a spring. So inverse inductance plays the role of the spring constant.

The Lagrangian of an LC circuit is therefore

$$ \mathcal L = \frac12 C\dot\Phi^2 - \frac{\Phi^2}{2L} . $$

The Euler–Lagrange equation gives \(C\ddot\Phi = -\Phi/L\), i.e. \(\ddot\Phi + \Phi/LC = 0\): a harmonic oscillator at \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). The analogy is exact, term by term:

For a circuit with many nodes, each node \(i\) gets a flux \(\Phi_i\). A capacitor between nodes \(i\) and \(j\) stores \(\tfrac12C_{ij}(\dot\Phi_i-\dot\Phi_j)^2\) (it sees the voltage difference). Adding all the capacitors gives a quadratic form \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}\), where \(\mathbf C\) is the capacitance matrix (Chapter 4). Inductors similarly give \(V = \tfrac12\boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T}\mathbf L^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi\). A Josephson junction, Book 6 shows, adds \(-E_J\cos(2\pi\Phi_{\text{branch}}/\Phi_0)\). Put together, that is precisely the Lagrangian at the start of the review's design section (@lf:eq-lagr). You now know where it comes from.

Worked example: two capacitively coupled LC oscillators. Two LC circuits \((C_1, L_1)\) and \((C_2, L_2)\) share a small coupling capacitor \(C_g\) between their top nodes. With node fluxes \(\Phi_1,\Phi_2\):

\[ \mathcal L = \tfrac12C_1\dot\Phi_1^2 + \tfrac12C_2\dot\Phi_2^2 + \tfrac12C_g(\dot\Phi_1-\dot\Phi_2)^2 - \frac{\Phi_1^2}{2L_1} - \frac{\Phi_2^2}{2L_2} . \]

Expanding \((\dot\Phi_1-\dot\Phi_2)^2\) and collecting terms, the kinetic part is \(\tfrac12\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}\) with

\[ \mathbf C = \begin{pmatrix}C_1+C_g & -C_g\\ -C_g & C_2+C_g\end{pmatrix}. \]

The off-diagonal \(-C_g\) couples the two oscillators: moving one drags on the other. Every coupling between qubits and resonators on a superconducting chip enters exactly this way, and its strength is set by a number that an electrostatic simulator computes from the drawn geometry.

In the analogy, what plays the role of a heavy mass, and what of a stiff spring?

Answer

A large capacitance is a heavy mass (hard to change its voltage quickly); a small inductance is a stiff spring (a small flux produces a large current, i.e. a large restoring "force"). Both large \(C\) and large \(L\) lower the frequency \(1/\sqrt{LC}\), as heavy masses and soft springs do.

Symmetry and conservation

If the Lagrangian does not depend on some coordinate \(q_k\) (only on its rate \(\dot q_k\)), the Euler–Lagrange equation reduces to \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(\partial L/\partial\dot q_k) = 0\): the quantity \(p_k = \partial L/\partial\dot q_k\), the conjugate momentum, never changes. More generally, Noether's theorem says every continuous symmetry gives a conserved quantity: if the physics is unchanged by shifting position, momentum is conserved; by rotating, angular momentum; by shifting time, energy.

In circuits: a node connected to the rest only through capacitors (an "island") appears in the Lagrangian only through \(\dot\Phi\), never through \(\Phi\) itself. Its conjugate momentum, which is its charge, is therefore conserved: charge cannot leak on or off the island except by tunnelling through a junction. In a superconductor, charge tunnels in units of Cooper pairs, so the island's charge is a whole number of pairs. That is the starting point of the Cooper-pair box and the transmon (Book 6).

Try it in Python

Integrate the pendulum's equation from the Lagrangian and watch the period grow with amplitude (the nonlinearity that makes a transmon a qubit).

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

g, l = 9.81, 1.0
def rhs(t, y):                       # y = (theta, theta_dot)
    return [y[1], -g / l * np.sin(y[0])]

for amp_deg in (5, 45, 90, 150):
    th0 = np.radians(amp_deg)
    # integrate one swing and find when theta_dot changes sign (half period)
    sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, 10), [th0, 0], max_step=1e-3, dense_output=True)
    t = np.linspace(0.01, 10, 100000)
    v = sol.sol(t)[1]
    half = t[np.argmax(np.sign(v[:-1]) != np.sign(v[1:]))]
    print(amp_deg, "deg: period", 2 * half, "s  (small-angle:", 2 * np.pi * np.sqrt(l / g), ")")

At 5° the period matches \(2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\approx2.006\) s; at 150° it is about 75% longer. A transmon is a quantum pendulum swinging at small amplitude, where this frequency drop shows up as a small, crucial difference between its energy-level spacings.

A circuit-quantisation tool takes a netlist (nodes, capacitors, inductors, junctions), builds \(\mathbf C\) and \(\mathbf L^{-1}\), writes the Lagrangian, and hands it to the next chapter's Legendre transform. The capacitances come from an electrostatic simulation of the layout. If those numbers are 1% off, the qubit frequency is about 0.5% off (@lf:eq-sens), which is tens of MHz.

  • Lagrangian \(L = T - V\); the real path makes the action \(\int L\,\dd t\) stationary, giving \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\).
  • Any coordinates work; constraint forces disappear.
  • Circuits: node flux is the coordinate, capacitance the mass, inverse inductance the spring; many nodes give a capacitance matrix.
  • A coordinate missing from \(L\) has a conserved momentum; an island's charge is conserved.

Exercises

  1. A mass \(m\) slides on a frictionless ring of radius \(R\) rotating about a vertical diameter at angular velocity \(\Omega\). Using the angle \(\theta\) from the bottom, show that \(L = \tfrac12mR^2(\dot\theta^2 + \Omega^2\sin^2\theta) + mgR\cos\theta\) and find the equation of motion.
    Solution

    Velocity components \(R\dot\theta\) along the ring and \(R\Omega\sin\theta\) around the axis; height \(-R\cos\theta\). Euler–Lagrange: \(mR^2\ddot\theta = mR^2\Omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta - mgR\sin\theta\). For \(\Omega^2 > g/R\) the bottom becomes unstable and the bead settles at \(\cos\theta = g/R\Omega^2\).

  2. Find the resonance frequency of an LC circuit with \(L = 10\) nH and \(C = 80\) fF. What capacitance halves the frequency?
    Solution

    \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{8\times10^{-22}}\approx3.54\times10^{10}\) rad/s, \(f_0\approx5.6\) GHz. Four times the capacitance, 320 fF.

  3. For the coupled oscillators with \(C_1 = C_2 = C\), \(L_1=L_2=L\), find the two normal-mode frequencies.
    Solution

    In-phase \((1,1)\): the coupling capacitor sees no voltage, \(\omega_+ = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). Out-of-phase \((1,-1)\): kinetic energy \((C + 2C_g)\dot\Phi^2\) per node pair, so \(\omega_- = 1/\sqrt{L(C+2C_g)}\). Splitting \(\approx\omega_0C_g/C\).

  4. Show that if \(L\) has no explicit time dependence, \(E = \sum_k\dot q_k\,\partial L/\partial\dot q_k - L\) is conserved, and equals \(T+V\) when \(T\) is quadratic in velocities.
    Solution

    Differentiate and use the Euler–Lagrange equations: all terms cancel. For \(T = \tfrac12\sum M_{jk}\dot q_j\dot q_k\), \(\sum\dot q_k\partial T/\partial\dot q_k = 2T\), so \(E = 2T - (T - V) = T+V\).

  5. Write the Lagrangian of a single node connected to ground by a capacitor \(C\) and a Josephson junction of energy \(E_J\) (energy \(-E_J\cos(2\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)\)). What is the equation of motion, and when does it reduce to an LC oscillator?
    Solution

    \(\mathcal L = \tfrac12C\dot\Phi^2 + E_J\cos(2\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)\). Euler–Lagrange: \(C\ddot\Phi = -\frac{2\pi E_J}{\Phi_0}\sin(2\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)\), a pendulum. For small \(\Phi\), \(\sin x\approx x\), giving an LC oscillator with \(1/L_J = (2\pi/\Phi_0)^2E_J\).
