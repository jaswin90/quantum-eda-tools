From Newton to Lagrange: the principle of least action

Goals

  • Restate Newton's laws as a variational principle and derive the Euler–Lagrange equations.
  • Choose generalised coordinates, including ones that are not positions, and write Lagrangians for mechanical and electrical systems.
  • Write the Lagrangian of an LC circuit in terms of node flux, the first step of circuit quantisation.
  • Connect symmetries to conservation laws.

Why reformulate Newton

Newton's second law, \(m\ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F\), works perfectly for particles in Cartesian coordinates. It becomes awkward when the natural variables are angles, when there are constraints (a bead on a wire), or when the "particle" is not a particle at all but a voltage on a capacitor. Quantum mechanics also needs more than forces: it needs the energy function of a system and a pair of variables (a coordinate and its conjugate momentum) to turn into operators. The Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations supply both. For quantum engineering the payoff is concrete: the Hamiltonian of a superconducting circuit is derived by writing its Lagrangian, and every term in the result is a capacitance or inductance computed from the layout (@lf:sec-design-quant).

The action and the Euler–Lagrange equation

For a system described by a coordinate \(q(t)\), define the Lagrangian as kinetic minus potential energy, \(L(q,\dot q) = T - V\), and the action of a path between times \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) as

$$ S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L\big(q(t), \dot q(t)\big)\,\dd t . $$

The principle of stationary action states that the path the system actually follows makes \(S\) stationary: changing the path slightly, \(q\to q+\delta q\) with \(\delta q(t_1)=\delta q(t_2)=0\), changes \(S\) only at second order. Compute the first-order change:

\[ \delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\delta\dot q\Big)\dd t = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} - \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\Big)\delta q\,\dd t , \]

where the second form integrates by parts (the boundary term vanishes because \(\delta q\) is zero at the ends). For this to vanish for every \(\delta q\), the bracket must be zero at every time:

$$ \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0 . $$

This is the Euler–Lagrange equation. Check it on a particle: \(L = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 - V(x)\) gives \(\partial L/\partial\dot x = m\dot x\) and \(\partial L/\partial x = -V'(x)\), so \(m\ddot x = -V'(x) = F\). Newton's law is recovered. For several coordinates \(q_1,\dots,q_n\) there is one equation per coordinate.

Why "least" action? The classical path is a stationary point of \(S\), not always a minimum. The deeper reason it is singled out comes from quantum mechanics: Feynman showed that a quantum particle explores every path with amplitude \(\ee^{\ii S/\hbar}\). Paths near a stationary point have nearly equal phases and add constructively, while other paths cancel. When \(S\gg\hbar\), only the neighbourhood of the classical path survives. Classical mechanics is the constructive-interference limit of quantum mechanics.

Generalised coordinates

The power of @eq:c-el is that \(q\) can be any set of variables that specifies the configuration. For a pendulum of length \(\ell\), the angle \(\theta\) is enough: \(T = \tfrac12m\ell^2\dot\theta^2\), \(V = -mg\ell\cos\theta\), and the Euler–Lagrange equation gives \(m\ell^2\ddot\theta = -mg\ell\sin\theta\) without ever computing the tension in the string. For small angles \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\) and the pendulum is a harmonic oscillator of frequency \(\sqrt{g/\ell}\); for large angles it is a nonlinear oscillator whose frequency drops with amplitude. Keep this example in mind: the potential energy of a Josephson junction is also \(-E_J\cos\varphi\), so a transmon is, mathematically, a quantum pendulum (Book 6).

The Lagrangian of a circuit

For electrical circuits, a convenient generalised coordinate is the node flux, the time integral of the node's voltage relative to ground:

$$ \Phi(t) = \int_{-\infty}^t V(t')\,\dd t', \qquad V = \dot\Phi . $$

A capacitor stores energy \(\tfrac12CV^2 = \tfrac12C\dot\Phi^2\), which depends on the "velocity" \(\dot\Phi\), so it plays the role of kinetic energy. An inductor carries current \(I = \Phi/L\) (because \(V = L\dot I\) integrates to \(\Phi = LI\)), and stores energy \(\tfrac12LI^2 = \Phi^2/2L\), which depends on the "position" \(\Phi\): a potential energy. The Lagrangian of an LC circuit is therefore

$$ \mathcal L = \frac12 C\dot\Phi^2 - \frac{\Phi^2}{2L} . $$

The Euler–Lagrange equation gives \(C\ddot\Phi = -\Phi/L\), i.e. \(\ddot\Phi + \Phi/LC = 0\): a harmonic oscillator at \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). The analogy is exact: capacitance is mass, inverse inductance is spring constant, flux is position, and, as the next chapter shows, charge is momentum.

For a network with many nodes, each node \(i\) gets a flux \(\Phi_i\). Each capacitor between nodes \(i\) and \(j\) contributes \(\tfrac12C_{ij}(\dot\Phi_i-\dot\Phi_j)^2\), and collecting all of them gives \(T = \tfrac12\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf C\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}\), where \(\mathbf C\) is the capacitance matrix of Chapter 4. Inductors give \(V = \tfrac12\boldsymbol\Phi^{\mathsf T}\mathbf L^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi\). A Josephson junction, as we will show in Book 6, adds \(-E_J\cos(2\pi\Phi_{\text{branch}}/\Phi_0)\). This is precisely the Lagrangian at the start of the review's design section (@lf:eq-lagr). You now know where it comes from.

Worked example: two capacitively coupled LC oscillators. Two LC circuits \((C_1, L_1)\) and \((C_2, L_2)\) share a coupling capacitor \(C_g\) between their top nodes. With node fluxes \(\Phi_1,\Phi_2\):

\[ \mathcal L = \tfrac12C_1\dot\Phi_1^2 + \tfrac12C_2\dot\Phi_2^2 + \tfrac12C_g(\dot\Phi_1-\dot\Phi_2)^2 - \frac{\Phi_1^2}{2L_1} - \frac{\Phi_2^2}{2L_2} = \tfrac12\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi}^{\mathsf T}\begin{pmatrix}C_1+C_g & -C_g\\ -C_g & C_2+C_g\end{pmatrix}\dot{\boldsymbol\Phi} - \ldots \]

The off-diagonal element \(-C_g\) of the capacitance matrix couples the two oscillators. Every coupling between qubits and resonators on a superconducting chip enters this way, and its strength is fixed by a number that an electrostatic solver computes from the geometry.

Symmetry and conservation

If \(L\) does not depend on some coordinate \(q_k\) (the coordinate is cyclic), the Euler–Lagrange equation says \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(\partial L/\partial\dot q_k) = 0\): the conjugate momentum \(p_k = \partial L/\partial\dot q_k\) is conserved. More generally, Noether's theorem states that every continuous symmetry gives a conserved quantity: translation invariance gives momentum, rotation invariance gives angular momentum, and invariance under shifts in time gives energy. In circuits, a node that is connected to the rest only through capacitors (an "island") has a Lagrangian independent of its flux, so its conjugate momentum, which is its charge, is conserved. That is why the charge on a superconducting island is quantised in units of Cooper pairs, the starting point of the Cooper-pair box and the transmon.

A circuit-quantisation tool takes a netlist (nodes, capacitors, inductors, junctions), builds \(\mathbf C\) and \(\mathbf L^{-1}\), writes the Lagrangian, and hands it to the next chapter's Legendre transform. The capacitances come from an electrostatic simulation of the layout. If those numbers are 1% off, the qubit frequency is about 0.5% off (@lf:eq-sens), which is tens of MHz.

Exercises

  1. A mass \(m\) slides on a frictionless ring of radius \(R\) rotating about a vertical diameter at angular velocity \(\Omega\). Using the polar angle \(\theta\) from the bottom, show that \(L = \tfrac12mR^2(\dot\theta^2 + \Omega^2\sin^2\theta) + mgR\cos\theta\) and find the equation of motion.
    Solution

    Velocity components: \(R\dot\theta\) along the ring and \(R\Omega\sin\theta\) around the axis, so \(T = \tfrac12mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\Omega^2\sin^2\theta)\); height \(-R\cos\theta\) gives \(V = -mgR\cos\theta\). Euler–Lagrange: \(mR^2\ddot\theta = mR^2\Omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta - mgR\sin\theta\). For \(\Omega^2 > g/R\) the bottom becomes unstable and the bead settles at \(\cos\theta = g/R\Omega^2\).

  2. Find the resonance frequency of an LC circuit with \(L = 10\) nH and \(C = 80\) fF. What would \(C\) need to be to halve the frequency?
    Solution

    \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC} = 1/\sqrt{8\times10^{-22}}\approx3.54\times10^{10}\) rad/s, so \(f_0\approx 5.6\) GHz. Halving \(f\) needs four times the capacitance, 320 fF.

  3. For the coupled oscillators of the worked example with \(C_1 = C_2 = C\) and \(L_1=L_2=L\), find the two normal-mode frequencies.
    Solution

    Equations of motion: \(\mathbf C\ddot{\boldsymbol\Phi} = -\boldsymbol\Phi/L\). Try \((1,1)\): the coupling term vanishes, so \(C\ddot\Phi = -\Phi/L\), \(\omega_+ = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). Try \((1,-1)\): the effective capacitance is \(C+2C_g\), \(\omega_- = 1/\sqrt{L(C+2C_g)}\). The splitting is \(\approx\omega_0C_g/C\) for small \(C_g\).

  4. Show that if \(L\) has no explicit time dependence, the quantity \(E = \sum_k\dot q_k\,\partial L/\partial\dot q_k - L\) is conserved, and that it equals \(T+V\) when \(T\) is quadratic in velocities.
    Solution

    \(\frac{\dd E}{\dd t} = \sum_k\big[\ddot q_kp_k + \dot q_k\dot p_k\big] - \sum_k\big[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}\dot q_k + p_k\ddot q_k\big]\), with \(p_k = \partial L/\partial\dot q_k\). Using \(\dot p_k = \partial L/\partial q_k\) everything cancels. For \(T = \tfrac12\sum M_{jk}\dot q_j\dot q_k\), \(\sum\dot q_kp_k = 2T\), so \(E = 2T - (T - V) = T+V\).
