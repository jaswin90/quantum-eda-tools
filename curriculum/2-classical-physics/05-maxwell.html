Maxwell's equations, waves and materials

Goals

  • State Maxwell's equations in differential and integral form and derive the wave equation.
  • Describe plane waves, the speed of light in a medium, impedance of free space and the Poynting vector.
  • Model lossy dielectrics with a complex permittivity and loss tangent, and lossy metals with the skin depth.
  • Know the phenomenology of superconductors that matters for microwave design: zero DC resistance, the London penetration depth and kinetic inductance.

The four equations

Faraday found that a changing magnetic flux induces a voltage around a loop, and Maxwell found that a changing electric field acts like a current. Adding these to Gauss's and Ampère's laws gives Maxwell's equations, which in a linear medium with permittivity \(\varepsilon\) and permeability \(\mu\) read

$$ \nabla\cdot(\varepsilon\mathbf E) = \rho, \quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0, \quad \nabla\times\mathbf E = -\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t}, \quad \nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu\mathbf J + \mu\varepsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}. $$

In integral form, the third says that the voltage around a loop equals minus the rate of change of flux through it, \(\oint\mathbf E\cdot\dd\boldsymbol\ell = -\dot\Phi\): this is why we called \(\Phi = \int V\dd t\) a "flux" in Chapter 1. Flux quantisation in superconducting loops (Book 6) and the flux-tuning of qubits both rest on this law.

Electromagnetic waves

In a region without charges or currents, take the curl of Faraday's law and use the identity \(\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf E = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E) - \nabla^2\mathbf E\) with \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0\):

$$ \nabla^2\mathbf E = \mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}, \qquad v = \frac1{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r\mu_r}} . $$

Maxwell recognised \(1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\approx3\times10^8\) m/s as the speed of light. Light, microwaves and radio are the same phenomenon at different frequencies. A plane wave \(\mathbf E = \mathbf E_0\ee^{\ii(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)}\) has \(|\mathbf k| = \omega/v = 2\pi/\lambda\), with \(\mathbf E\), \(\mathbf B\) and \(\mathbf k\) mutually perpendicular. The ratio \(|\mathbf E|/|\mathbf H|\) (with \(\mathbf H = \mathbf B/\mu\)) is the wave impedance \(\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}\), equal to \(\eta_0\approx377\) Ω in vacuum. Energy flows along the Poynting vector \(\mathbf S = \mathbf E\times\mathbf H\), whose time average gives the intensity.

For time-harmonic fields (\(\propto\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\)) the wave equation becomes the Helmholtz equation, and inside a closed metal box only discrete frequencies satisfy the boundary conditions (tangential \(\mathbf E = 0\) on a perfect conductor). These are the box's eigenmodes, found by solving

$$ \nabla\times\big(\mu^{-1}\nabla\times\mathbf E\big) = \omega^2\varepsilon\,\mathbf E . $$

This is exactly the eigenproblem a 3D electromagnetic eigenmode solver discretises (@lf:eq-curl). For a rectangular box of sides \(a,b\) the lowest modes have \(f = \frac{v}{2}\sqrt{(m/a)^2+(n/b)^2}\), which places the first mode of a 10 mm chip cavity filled with silicon near 6 GHz, right among the qubits (@lf:eq-box).

Lossy dielectrics

Real dielectrics absorb a little energy each cycle because their microscopic dipoles lag behind the field. This is captured by a complex permittivity,

$$ \varepsilon = \varepsilon' + \ii\varepsilon'' = \varepsilon'(1 + \ii\tan\delta), \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{\varepsilon''}{\varepsilon'} . $$

(With the physicists' \(\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\) convention used here, loss appears as a positive imaginary part; engineering texts using \(\ee^{+j\omega t}\) write \(\varepsilon'-j\varepsilon''\). Either way \(\varepsilon''>0\) means absorption.) The average power dissipated per unit volume is \(\tfrac12\omega\varepsilon''|\mathbf E|^2\), while the stored energy density is \(\tfrac14\varepsilon'|\mathbf E|^2\) on average in the electric field. If the material fills a resonator completely, the quality factor is therefore \(Q = 1/\tan\delta\). Crystalline sapphire and high-resistivity silicon have \(\tan\delta\) below \(10^{-6}\) at millikelvin temperatures. Amorphous oxides have \(10^{-3}\) or worse, dominated by microscopic two-level systems (Book 6) that saturate at high power and high temperature [@gao2008].

Normal metals and the skin effect

In a metal with conductivity \(\sigma\), the current \(\mathbf J = \sigma\mathbf E\) dominates the displacement current at microwave frequencies, and the wave equation becomes a diffusion equation. Fields decay into the metal over the skin depth

$$ \delta_s = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu_0\sigma\omega}} , $$

about 1 µm for copper at 5 GHz. Current flows only in this thin layer, so the effective resistance of a conductor rises as \(\sqrt\omega\), and the surface resistance is \(R_s = 1/(\sigma\delta_s)\). A copper resonator at room temperature has \(Q\) of only a few thousand at microwave frequencies, which is why quantum devices use superconductors.

Superconductors, phenomenologically

Below a critical temperature \(T_c\) (1.2 K for aluminium, 9.2 K for niobium, 4.4 K for tantalum), certain metals lose all DC resistance and expel magnetic fields. For microwave design four facts are enough here; Book 6 explains their microscopic origin.

  1. Penetration depth. Magnetic fields penetrate only a distance \(\lambda_L\) (the London penetration depth, 50–100 nm in Al and Nb). Inside, the London equation \(\nabla^2\mathbf B = \mathbf B/\lambda_L^2\) makes the field decay exponentially.
  2. Kinetic inductance. The supercurrent is carried by paired electrons with mass. Their kinetic energy \(\tfrac12 n_s m v^2\) adds to the magnetic energy and acts as an extra inductance. For a film of thickness \(t\ll\lambda_L\), the sheet kinetic inductance is \(L_k\approx\mu_0\lambda_L^2/t\) per square. Disordered films such as NbN, NbTiN and granular Al have \(L_k\) of tens to thousands of pH per square and are used to make compact high-impedance resonators and "superinductors".
  3. Finite loss at finite frequency. At \(T>0\) a small population of unpaired quasiparticles absorbs microwave energy, giving a surface resistance that falls exponentially with temperature (roughly \(\propto\ee^{-\Delta/k_BT}\), with gap \(\Delta\approx1.76k_BT_c\)). At 20 mK and 5 GHz, aluminium's intrinsic loss is negligible compared with dielectric loss; the practical limits are surfaces, interfaces and non-equilibrium quasiparticles.
  4. Flux quantisation. The flux through a superconducting loop is quantised in units of \(\Phi_0 = h/2e\approx2.07\times10^{-15}\) Wb. This is the basis of SQUIDs and flux-tunable qubits.

The full-wave formulation of extraction in the review (@lf:sec-design-extract) solves @eq:c-curlcurl with material models for \(\varepsilon\), \(\tan\delta\) and the superconductor's surface impedance. Kinetic inductance and the effective permittivity of the substrate are known only to a few percent, a "model-form" uncertainty that mesh refinement cannot remove. A design tool should propagate it into the predicted frequencies.

Exercises

  1. What is the wavelength of a 5 GHz wave in vacuum and in silicon (\(\varepsilon_r = 11.45\))? Compare with a 10 mm chip.
    Solution

    Vacuum: \(3\times10^8/5\times10^9 = 60\) mm. Silicon: \(60/\sqrt{11.45}\approx17.7\) mm. A 10 mm chip is more than half a wavelength across, so lumped-element (quasi-static) models fail at the chip scale.

  2. Show that a completely filled resonator has \(Q = 1/\tan\delta\) using the time-averaged stored energy (electric plus magnetic, equal at resonance) and dissipated power.
    Solution

    At resonance the time-averaged electric and magnetic energies are equal, so total stored energy is \(W = 2\times\tfrac14\varepsilon'\int|\mathbf E|^2 = \tfrac12\varepsilon'\int|\mathbf E|^2\). Power loss \(P = \tfrac12\omega\varepsilon''\int|\mathbf E|^2\). \(Q = \omega W/P = \varepsilon'/\varepsilon'' = 1/\tan\delta\).

  3. Compute the skin depth in copper (\(\sigma = 6\times10^7\) S/m) at 5 GHz, and the surface resistance.
    Solution

    \(\delta_s = \sqrt{2/(4\pi\times10^{-7}\times6\times10^7\times2\pi\times5\times10^9)}\approx0.92\,\mu\)m. \(R_s = 1/(\sigma\delta_s)\approx18\) mΩ per square.

  4. A NbTiN film 10 nm thick has \(\lambda_L = 300\) nm. Estimate \(L_k\) per square, and the kinetic inductance of a 1 µm wide, 1 mm long wire.
    Solution

    \(L_k\approx\mu_0\lambda_L^2/t = 1.257\times10^{-6}\times9\times10^{-14}/10^{-8}\approx11\) pH/□. The wire has 1000 squares, so about 11 nH, roughly ten times its geometric inductance. This is how compact high-impedance resonators are made.
