Oscillators: damping, driving, coupling and nonlinearity

Goals

  • Describe a lossy resonator with internal and external quality factors and the input–output picture.
  • Treat coupled oscillators: normal modes, avoided crossings, and the dispersive limit.
  • Understand the nonlinear (Duffing) oscillator, whose frequency depends on amplitude, as the classical shadow of a transmon.
  • Meet parametric driving, the principle behind quantum-limited amplifiers.

Loss: internal and external

Book 1 described a damped oscillator with one decay rate \(\gamma\). A real resonator loses energy through several independent channels, and the rates add: \(\gamma = \sum_i\gamma_i\), or equivalently

$$ \frac1{Q_L} = \frac1{Q_i} + \frac1{Q_e}, $$

where the internal quality factor \(Q_i\) collects unwanted loss (dielectrics, metal surfaces, radiation) and the external or coupling quality factor \(Q_e\) describes deliberate coupling to a measurement line. The loaded \(Q_L\) is what sets the observed linewidth. A designer chooses \(Q_e\) by geometry (a coupling capacitor or a length of line running parallel to a feedline). Fabrication and materials set \(Q_i\). Measuring both separately from a single resonance trace is the standard way to characterise materials loss (Chapter 6 and @lf:sec-mat-part).

In terms of the complex amplitude \(a(t)\) of the resonator (normalised so \(|a|^2\) is the stored energy in units of \(\hbar\omega_0\), i.e. the photon number), coupled to an input line carrying amplitude \(a_{\mathrm{in}}\), the input–output equations read

$$ \dot a = -\ii\omega_0a - \frac{\kappa_i+\kappa_e}{2}\,a + \sqrt{\kappa_e}\,a_{\mathrm{in}}, \qquad a_{\mathrm{out}} = a_{\mathrm{in}} - \sqrt{\kappa_e}\,a , $$

with \(\kappa_{i,e} = \omega_0/Q_{i,e}\). At steady state with a drive at \(\omega\), \(a = \sqrt{\kappa_e}a_{\mathrm{in}}/[\ii(\omega_0-\omega) + \kappa/2]\), so the reflection coefficient is

$$ r(\omega) = \frac{a_{\mathrm{out}}}{a_{\mathrm{in}}} = 1 - \frac{\kappa_e}{\ii(\omega_0-\omega) + (\kappa_i+\kappa_e)/2} . $$

On resonance \(r = (\kappa_i - \kappa_e)/(\kappa_i+\kappa_e)\). If \(\kappa_e = \kappa_i\) (critical coupling) nothing is reflected: all the incoming power is absorbed. If \(\kappa_e\gg\kappa_i\) (overcoupled) almost everything comes back, but with its phase flipped by \(\pi\), which is how a readout resonator encodes information in phase. Plotted in the complex plane as \(\omega\) sweeps, \(r(\omega)\) traces a circle whose diameter is \(2\kappa_e/\kappa\); fitting that circle separates \(\kappa_i\) from \(\kappa_e\). The same equations, with operators in place of numbers, are the quantum input–output theory of Book 5.

Coupled oscillators and avoided crossings

Two oscillators with amplitudes \(a_1, a_2\), frequencies \(\omega_1, \omega_2\) and a weak coupling \(g\) obey, in the same complex-amplitude language,

$$ \ii\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\omega_1 & g\\ g & \omega_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix}. $$

This is the "coupled-mode" form: it keeps only the slowly varying parts of the motion and is accurate when \(g\ll\omega_{1,2}\). It has exactly the structure of the Schrödinger equation for a two-level system. The normal-mode frequencies were found in Book 1: \(\omega_\pm = \bar\omega\pm\tfrac12\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}\). @fig:c-avoided plots them as one oscillator is tuned through the other. The uncoupled frequencies cross; the coupled ones repel, with a minimum gap of \(2g\). Far from the crossing, each mode is pushed away by \(g^2/\Delta\), a shift called the dispersive shift. It is small but nonzero, and it is how a qubit is read out without being directly measured: the qubit's state shifts the resonator's frequency by a state-dependent amount (Book 6).

On resonance, energy put into one oscillator swaps completely into the other in a time \(\pi/2g\) and back. Off resonance the swap is incomplete: the maximum fraction transferred is \(4g^2/(\Delta^2+4g^2)\). This simple formula is the classical version of the Rabi formula of Book 3 and the basis of "frequency collisions" in the review: two qubits that are accidentally close in frequency exchange energy when they should not (@lf:sec-var-collisions).

Nonlinearity: the Duffing oscillator

A pendulum's potential \(-\cos\theta\) is harmonic only for small angles: \(-\cos\theta\approx-1+\theta^2/2-\theta^4/24\). The quartic term softens the restoring force at large amplitude. The equation \(\ddot x + \omega_0^2x - \beta x^3 = 0\) (the Duffing oscillator, here with a softening sign) has a frequency that falls with amplitude \(A\):

$$ \omega(A) \approx \omega_0 - \frac{3\beta A^2}{8\omega_0}. $$

(Derivation: substitute \(x = A\cos\omega t\), use \(\cos^3 = \tfrac34\cos+\tfrac14\cos3\), keep the term at \(\omega\).) Because energy is proportional to \(A^2\), the frequency decreases linearly with energy. In quantum mechanics energy comes in quanta, so each successive level is spaced a little less than the one below. The spacing between the first two levels can then be addressed without touching the next: that is what makes an anharmonic oscillator usable as a qubit. A transmon is exactly a Josephson-junction pendulum with a large capacitance, and its anharmonicity \(\alpha\approx-E_C/\hbar\) is the quantum version of @eq:c-duffing (@lf:eq-freq).

Driven strongly, a Duffing oscillator develops bistability: two stable amplitudes for the same drive, with hysteresis as the drive frequency is swept. Josephson bifurcation amplifiers exploit this for readout, and it also limits how hard you can drive a readout resonator coupled to a qubit before it misbehaves.

Parametric driving and amplification

Instead of pushing an oscillator, one can modulate one of its parameters. A child on a swing pumps by raising and lowering their centre of mass twice per cycle. Mathematically, \(\ddot x + \omega_0^2[1 + \epsilon\cos(2\omega_0t)]x = 0\) (the Mathieu equation) has solutions that grow exponentially when the pump is at twice the resonance frequency. One quadrature of the motion is amplified, by a gain \(\ee^{\epsilon\omega_0t/2}\) to first order, while the other is de-amplified. This is degenerate parametric amplification. If the oscillator also has loss, gain and loss compete, and below threshold the device acts as a stable amplifier for weak incoming signals. A Josephson parametric amplifier (JPA) modulates the junction inductance with a pump; a travelling-wave parametric amplifier (TWPA) does it along a transmission line of many junctions [@macklin2015]. These amplifiers add only the minimum noise allowed by quantum mechanics and are what makes single-shot qubit readout possible. Book 5 shows that the de-amplified quadrature is a squeezed state of light.

A chip contains dozens to thousands of coupled oscillators: qubits, couplers, readout resonators, Purcell filters and parasitic package modes. The design problem is to keep every pair either strongly coupled on purpose or far enough apart that their dispersive shifts \(g^2/\Delta\) are harmless. That is a constraint-satisfaction problem over the whole frequency plan, made statistical by fabrication spread (@lf:sec-var-alloc).

Exercises

  1. A resonator has \(Q_i = 10^6\) and \(Q_e = 10^4\) at 7 GHz. What are \(Q_L\), the linewidth, and the depth of the reflection dip?
    Solution

    \(1/Q_L = 10^{-6} + 10^{-4}\), \(Q_L\approx9900\). Linewidth \(7\times10^9/9900\approx707\) kHz. On resonance \(r = (\kappa_i-\kappa_e)/(\kappa_i+\kappa_e) = (1 - 100)/(1+100)\approx -0.98\): almost total reflection with a phase flip. The magnitude barely dips, which is why phase, not amplitude, is used for overcoupled resonators.

  2. Show from @eq:c-reflect that \(|r|=1\) for all \(\omega\) when \(\kappa_i = 0\).
    Solution

    With \(\kappa_i=0\), \(r = 1 - \kappa_e/(\ii\delta+\kappa_e/2)\) with \(\delta = \omega_0-\omega\). Combining, \(r = (\ii\delta - \kappa_e/2)/(\ii\delta + \kappa_e/2)\): numerator and denominator have the same modulus. A lossless resonator only delays the signal.

  3. Two oscillators with \(g/2\pi = 10\) MHz are detuned by \(\Delta/2\pi = 400\) MHz. What is the dispersive shift, and what fraction of energy can leak from one to the other?
    Solution

    \(g^2/\Delta = 100/400 = 0.25\) MHz. Maximum transfer \(4g^2/(\Delta^2+4g^2) = 400/(160000+400)\approx0.25\%\). If the detuning had been only 20 MHz the transfer would be 50%.

  4. Derive @eq:c-duffing by substituting \(x = A\cos\omega t\) into \(\ddot x + \omega_0^2x - \beta x^3 = 0\) and keeping only the \(\cos\omega t\) terms.
    Solution

    \(-\omega^2A\cos\omega t + \omega_0^2A\cos\omega t - \beta A^3(\tfrac34\cos\omega t + \tfrac14\cos3\omega t) = 0\). Matching \(\cos\omega t\): \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - \tfrac34\beta A^2\), so \(\omega\approx\omega_0 - 3\beta A^2/8\omega_0\).
