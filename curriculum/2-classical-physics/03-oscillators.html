Oscillators: damping, driving, coupling and nonlinearity

Goals

  • Describe a lossy resonator with internal and external quality factors and the input–output picture.
  • Read a resonator's reflection trace: under-, critically and over-coupled.
  • Treat coupled oscillators: normal modes, avoided crossings, energy swapping and the dispersive limit.
  • Understand the nonlinear (Duffing) oscillator, whose frequency depends on amplitude, as the classical shadow of a transmon.
  • Meet parametric driving, the principle behind quantum-limited amplifiers.

A superconducting quantum chip is, from a classical point of view, a collection of oscillators: qubits (slightly nonlinear oscillators), readout resonators (linear oscillators), couplers, filters, and unwanted "box modes" of the package. Almost every property a designer cares about is an oscillator property: its frequency, how fast it loses energy (its quality factor), how strongly it talks to its neighbours, and how nonlinear it is. This chapter covers all four classically. Book 3 then re-does each with quantum mechanics, and the formulas barely change.

Loss: internal and external

Book 1 described a damped oscillator with one energy decay rate. The quality factor \(Q\) is the standard way to quote it: \(Q = \omega_0/\kappa\), where \(\kappa\) is the energy decay rate (energy falls as \(\ee^{-\kappa t}\)). Roughly, \(Q/2\pi\) is the number of oscillations before the energy falls by a factor \(\ee\). A guitar string has \(Q\sim10^3\); a good superconducting resonator \(Q\sim10^6\), meaning it rings for a million cycles.

A real resonator loses energy through several independent channels at once, and rates add (just as two leaks in a bucket drain it faster than either alone): \(\kappa = \sum_i\kappa_i\). Since \(Q\propto1/\kappa\), inverse quality factors add:

$$ \frac1{Q_L} = \frac1{Q_i} + \frac1{Q_e}. $$
  • The internal quality factor \(Q_i\) collects unwanted loss: absorption in dielectrics and surface layers, resistance in metals, radiation into the package. Fabrication and materials set it.
  • The external (coupling) quality factor \(Q_e\) describes deliberate coupling to a measurement line. A designer sets it by geometry: a coupling capacitor or a length of line running beside a feedline.
  • The loaded \(Q_L\) is what you see: it sets the width of the resonance.

Because \(1/Q_L\) is dominated by the smaller of the two, a resonator with \(Q_i = 10^6\) and \(Q_e = 10^4\) has \(Q_L\approx10^4\): the measurement port dominates and the materials loss is hard to see. Measuring both separately from one resonance trace is the standard way to characterise materials loss (Chapter 6 and @lf:sec-mat-part). Here is how that works.

Describe the resonator by a complex amplitude \(a(t)\), normalised so that \(|a|^2\) is the stored energy in units of \(\hbar\omega_0\) (i.e. the number of photons). It is fed by an incoming wave \(a_{\mathrm{in}}\) on a transmission line and emits an outgoing wave \(a_{\mathrm{out}}\), each normalised so \(|a_{\mathrm{in}}|^2\) is photons per second. The input–output equations are

$$ \dot a = -\ii\omega_0a - \frac{\kappa_i+\kappa_e}{2}\,a + \sqrt{\kappa_e}\,a_{\mathrm{in}}, \qquad a_{\mathrm{out}} = a_{\mathrm{in}} - \sqrt{\kappa_e}\,a , $$

with \(\kappa_{i,e} = \omega_0/Q_{i,e}\). Read them term by term:

  • \(-\ii\omega_0a\): free oscillation (the phasor spins).
  • \(-\tfrac{\kappa}2a\): the amplitude decays at half the energy rate (energy is amplitude squared).
  • \(+\sqrt{\kappa_e}a_{\mathrm{in}}\): the incoming wave pushes in through the same port that leaks out. The same \(\kappa_e\) appears in both, a fact called reciprocity.
  • \(a_{\mathrm{out}} = a_{\mathrm{in}} - \sqrt{\kappa_e}a\): what goes back down the line is the reflected input plus whatever leaks out of the resonator, which interferes with it.

Drive at frequency \(\omega\), so \(a_{\mathrm{in}}\propto\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\) and in steady state \(a\propto\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\) too. Then \(\dot a = -\ii\omega a\), and the first equation becomes algebra: \(a = \sqrt{\kappa_e}a_{\mathrm{in}}/[\ii(\omega_0-\omega) + \kappa/2]\). Substituting in the second gives the reflection coefficient:

$$ r(\omega) = \frac{a_{\mathrm{out}}}{a_{\mathrm{in}}} = 1 - \frac{\kappa_e}{\ii(\omega_0-\omega) + (\kappa_i+\kappa_e)/2} . $$

On resonance (\(\omega = \omega_0\)) this simplifies to \(r = (\kappa_i - \kappa_e)/(\kappa_i+\kappa_e)\). Three regimes:

A qubit readout resonator is deliberately overcoupled, so that information arrives as a phase shift of a strong reflected signal. Plotted in the complex plane as \(\omega\) sweeps, \(r(\omega)\) traces a circle that starts and ends at \(r=1\) far from resonance and has diameter \(2\kappa_e/\kappa\). Fitting that circle (the "circle fit" used in every materials lab) gives \(\kappa_e\) from its size and \(\kappa = \kappa_i+\kappa_e\) from how fast the point moves round it, and hence \(\kappa_i\) and \(Q_i\). The same equations, with operators in place of numbers, are the quantum input–output theory of Book 5.

A resonator has \(Q_i = Q_e = 10^5\). What fraction of incoming power on resonance is reflected?

Answer

None: it is critically coupled, \(r = 0\). All the power is absorbed in the resonator's internal loss.

Coupled oscillators and avoided crossings

Two oscillators with complex amplitudes \(a_1, a_2\), frequencies \(\omega_1, \omega_2\) and a weak coupling \(g\) (from a shared capacitor, Chapter 1) obey

$$ \ii\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\omega_1 & g\\ g & \omega_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix}. $$

This is the "coupled-mode" form. It keeps only the slowly varying, positive-frequency parts of the motion, and is accurate when \(g\ll\omega_{1,2}\). Look at its shape: \(\ii\,\dd\mathbf a/\dd t = M\mathbf a\) with a Hermitian matrix \(M\). That is exactly the Schrödinger equation for a two-level system with \(\hbar = 1\). Everything you learn here about two coupled oscillators transfers directly to a qubit in Book 3.

The normal modes are the eigenvectors of \(M\), and the normal-mode frequencies its eigenvalues (Book 1, Chapter 2): with \(\bar\omega = (\omega_1+\omega_2)/2\) and detuning \(\Delta = \omega_1 - \omega_2\),

\[ \omega_\pm = \bar\omega\pm\tfrac12\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}. \]

@fig:c-avoided plots them as oscillator 1 is tuned through oscillator 2.

Three things to read off the figure:

  1. At resonance (\(\Delta = 0\)) the uncoupled lines would cross, but the coupled frequencies repel: they are split by exactly \(2g\). This is an avoided crossing, and measuring its gap is how experimenters measure couplings. The modes there are equal mixtures, \((a_1\pm a_2)/\sqrt2\).
  2. Far from resonance (\(|\Delta|\gg g\)) each mode is mostly one of the original oscillators, but pushed away from the other by \(g^2/\Delta\). Expand the square root: \(\tfrac12\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}\approx\tfrac12|\Delta|+g^2/|\Delta|\). This small push is the dispersive shift.
  3. Mode character swaps: the upper branch is oscillator 2 on the left of the figure and oscillator 1 on the right.

The dispersive shift is how a qubit is read out without being directly measured. The qubit's state changes the resonator's frequency by a state-dependent amount of order \(g^2/\Delta\), and that changes the phase of a reflected signal (Book 6).

What happens in time? Start with all energy in oscillator 1. On resonance the energy swaps completely into oscillator 2 in a time \(\pi/2g\) and back, like two pendulums joined by a weak spring. Off resonance the swap is incomplete: the maximum fraction transferred is

\[ P_{\max} = \frac{4g^2}{\Delta^2+4g^2}, \]

and it swaps back and forth at the faster rate \(\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}\). This is the classical version of the Rabi formula of Book 3, and the basis of "frequency collisions" in the review: two qubits that are accidentally close in frequency exchange energy when they should not (@lf:sec-var-collisions).

Think of two people pushing each other on swings. If they swing at the same rhythm, every small push adds up and energy flows fully from one to the other. If their rhythms differ, the pushes go in and out of step, and only a little energy changes hands before the timing reverses the flow. The detuning \(\Delta\) is the mismatch of rhythms; \(g\) is how hard they push.

Nonlinearity: the Duffing oscillator

So far every oscillator has been linear: its frequency does not depend on how hard it swings. A linear oscillator cannot be a qubit, because all its energy levels are equally spaced (Book 3): a pulse at the frequency that takes it from level 0 to level 1 also takes it from 1 to 2, 2 to 3, and so on. We need nonlinearity.

A pendulum's potential \(-\cos\theta\) is harmonic only for small angles. Its Taylor series (Book 1) is \(-\cos\theta\approx-1+\theta^2/2-\theta^4/24\). The quartic term softens the restoring force at large amplitude. The general model is the Duffing oscillator, \(\ddot x + \omega_0^2x - \beta x^3 = 0\) (here written with a softening sign, \(\beta\gt0\)). Its frequency falls with amplitude \(A\):

$$ \omega(A) \approx \omega_0 - \frac{3\beta A^2}{8\omega_0}. $$

Derivation. Guess \(x\approx A\cos\omega t\). Then \(x^3 = A^3\cos^3\omega t = A^3(\tfrac34\cos\omega t + \tfrac14\cos3\omega t)\), using a trig identity. Substitute and keep only the terms at frequency \(\omega\) (the \(3\omega\) term is a small correction): \(-\omega^2A + \omega_0^2A - \tfrac34\beta A^3 = 0\), so \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - \tfrac34\beta A^2\). Taking the square root with \(\sqrt{1-\epsilon}\approx1-\epsilon/2\) gives @eq:c-duffing.

Because energy is proportional to \(A^2\), the frequency decreases linearly with energy. In quantum mechanics energy comes in quanta, so each successive level is spaced a little less than the one below. The spacing between levels 0 and 1 differs from that between 1 and 2 by the anharmonicity. A pulse tuned to the 0→1 transition then does not drive 1→2: the oscillator behaves as a two-level system, a qubit. A transmon is exactly a Josephson-junction pendulum with a large capacitance, and its anharmonicity \(\alpha\approx-E_C/\hbar\) (about \(-200\) to \(-300\) MHz) is the quantum version of @eq:c-duffing (@lf:eq-freq).

Driven strongly, a Duffing oscillator develops bistability: for the same drive there are two stable amplitudes, and sweeping the drive frequency up and then down traces different curves (hysteresis). The resonance peak leans over and breaks. Josephson bifurcation amplifiers exploit this for readout, and it also limits how hard you can drive a readout resonator coupled to a qubit before it misbehaves.

Nonlinearity is a double-edged sword. A qubit needs enough of it to be addressable, but readout resonators, filters and couplers inherit some nonlinearity from the junctions they are coupled to. If you drive them too hard, their frequency shifts and the readout calibration fails. Designers therefore compute not only frequencies but also "self-Kerr" and "cross-Kerr" shifts (the quantum names for Duffing shifts) for every mode.

Parametric driving and amplification

Instead of pushing an oscillator, one can modulate one of its parameters. A child on a swing pumps by raising and lowering their centre of mass, twice per swing, without anyone pushing. Mathematically,

\[ \ddot x + \omega_0^2[1 + \epsilon\cos(2\omega_0t)]\,x = 0 \]

(the Mathieu equation) has solutions that grow exponentially when the pump is at twice the resonance frequency. Why twice? The pump must stiffen the spring each time the swing passes through the bottom with maximum speed, and soften it at each turning point, and that happens twice per cycle.

A key subtlety: only one quadrature is amplified. Write the motion as \(x = X_1\cos\omega_0t + X_2\sin\omega_0t\). Depending on the pump phase, \(X_1\) grows as \(\ee^{\epsilon\omega_0t/4}\) while \(X_2\) shrinks at the same rate (to first order). This is degenerate parametric amplification. If the oscillator also has loss, gain and loss compete; below the threshold where gain wins, the device is a stable amplifier for weak incoming signals.

A Josephson parametric amplifier (JPA) modulates the junction inductance with a pump; a travelling-wave parametric amplifier (TWPA) does it along a transmission line of many junctions [@macklin2015]. These amplifiers add only the minimum noise allowed by quantum mechanics and are what makes single-shot qubit readout possible. Book 5 shows that the de-amplified quadrature is a squeezed state of light: quieter than the vacuum in one quadrature, noisier in the other.

Try it in Python

Compute the reflection coefficient of a resonator in the three coupling regimes, and check the size of the circle it traces.

import numpy as np

f0 = 7e9
w0 = 2 * np.pi * f0
f = np.linspace(f0 - 2e6, f0 + 2e6, 2001)
w = 2 * np.pi * f

for name, Qi, Qe in [("under", 1e5, 4e5), ("critical", 1e5, 1e5), ("over", 1e5, 1e4)]:
    ki, ke = w0 / Qi, w0 / Qe
    r = 1 - ke / (1j * (w0 - w) + (ki + ke) / 2)
    r0 = r[len(r) // 2]
    diam = np.max(np.abs(r - 1))           # circle passes through r = 1
    print(f"{name:8s} |r0|={abs(r0):.3f}  phase={np.degrees(np.angle(r0)):6.1f} deg"
          f"  diameter={diam:.3f}  (2ke/k = {2 * ke / (ki + ke):.3f})")

The diameter matches \(2\kappa_e/\kappa\) in every case, and only the overcoupled resonator flips the phase by 180° on resonance. Plot r.real against r.imag to see the circles.

A chip contains dozens to thousands of coupled oscillators: qubits, couplers, readout resonators, Purcell filters and parasitic package modes. The design problem is to keep every pair either strongly coupled on purpose or far enough apart that their dispersive shifts \(g^2/\Delta\) are harmless. That is a constraint-satisfaction problem over the whole frequency plan, made statistical by fabrication spread (@lf:sec-var-alloc).

  • Loss rates add: \(1/Q_L = 1/Q_i + 1/Q_e\); \(Q_i\) from materials, \(Q_e\) from design.
  • Reflection \(r(\omega)\) traces a circle of diameter \(2\kappa_e/\kappa\); fitting it separates internal from external loss.
  • Coupled oscillators: avoided crossing of gap \(2g\), dispersive shift \(g^2/\Delta\), maximum transfer \(4g^2/(\Delta^2+4g^2)\).
  • Duffing nonlinearity makes frequency depend on amplitude: the classical origin of qubit anharmonicity.
  • Pumping a parameter at \(2\omega_0\) amplifies one quadrature and squeezes the other.

Exercises

  1. A resonator has \(Q_i = 10^6\) and \(Q_e = 10^4\) at 7 GHz. What are \(Q_L\), the linewidth, and the depth of the reflection dip?
    Solution

    \(1/Q_L = 10^{-6} + 10^{-4}\), \(Q_L\approx9900\). Linewidth \(7\times10^9/9900\approx707\) kHz. On resonance \(r = (\kappa_i-\kappa_e)/(\kappa_i+\kappa_e) = (1 - 100)/(1+100)\approx -0.98\): almost total reflection with a phase flip. The magnitude barely dips, which is why phase, not amplitude, is used for overcoupled resonators.

  2. Show from @eq:c-reflect that \(|r|=1\) for all \(\omega\) when \(\kappa_i = 0\).
    Solution

    With \(\kappa_i=0\), \(r = 1 - \kappa_e/(\ii\delta+\kappa_e/2)\) with \(\delta = \omega_0-\omega\). Combining, \(r = (\ii\delta - \kappa_e/2)/(\ii\delta + \kappa_e/2)\): numerator and denominator have the same modulus. A lossless resonator only delays the signal.

  3. Two oscillators with \(g/2\pi = 10\) MHz are detuned by \(\Delta/2\pi = 400\) MHz. What is the dispersive shift, and what fraction of energy can leak from one to the other?
    Solution

    \(g^2/\Delta = 100/400 = 0.25\) MHz. Maximum transfer \(4g^2/(\Delta^2+4g^2) = 400/(160000+400)\approx0.25\%\). If the detuning had been only 20 MHz the transfer would be 50%.

  4. Derive @eq:c-duffing by substituting \(x = A\cos\omega t\) into \(\ddot x + \omega_0^2x - \beta x^3 = 0\) and keeping only the \(\cos\omega t\) terms.
    Solution

    \(-\omega^2A\cos\omega t + \omega_0^2A\cos\omega t - \beta A^3(\tfrac34\cos\omega t + \tfrac14\cos3\omega t) = 0\). Matching \(\cos\omega t\): \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - \tfrac34\beta A^2\), so \(\omega\approx\omega_0 - 3\beta A^2/8\omega_0\).

  5. Find \(\beta\) for a pendulum (\(\ddot\theta + \omega_0^2\sin\theta = 0\)) and the fractional frequency drop at a 20° swing.
    Solution

    \(\sin\theta\approx\theta - \theta^3/6\), so \(\beta = \omega_0^2/6\) and \(\Delta\omega/\omega_0 = 3\beta A^2/8\omega_0^2 = A^2/16\). At \(A = 0.349\) rad, a drop of 0.76% (the exact pendulum formula agrees to within 0.01%).

  6. On resonance, how long does it take to transfer energy completely between two oscillators with \(g/2\pi = 5\) MHz?
    Solution

    \(\pi/2g = \pi/(2\times2\pi\times5\times10^6) = 50\) ns. Two-qubit "iSWAP"-type gates use exactly this exchange.
