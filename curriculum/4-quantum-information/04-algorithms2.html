Quantum algorithms II: Fourier transform, phase estimation, factoring and simulation

Goals

  • Build the quantum Fourier transform from Hadamards and controlled phase gates, and see why it is exponentially cheaper than the classical FFT (and why that alone is not useful).
  • Understand phase estimation, the subroutine behind most exponential speed-ups.
  • Follow Shor's factoring algorithm from number theory to circuit, and understand what a resource estimate is.
  • Understand Hamiltonian simulation by Trotterisation, and the place of variational (near-term) algorithms.

The most important quantum algorithms all rest on one idea: measuring a phase. If a unitary operation multiplies some state by \(\ee^{2\pi\ii\varphi}\), a quantum computer can learn \(\varphi\) to many digits far faster than any classical method could learn the corresponding quantity. Factoring large numbers (which would break widely used encryption) and computing the energies of molecules (chemistry and materials) both reduce to this "phase estimation". The tool that reads the phase out is the quantum Fourier transform. This chapter builds both, then shows how algorithm descriptions become hardware requirements.

Binary fractions: the notation we need

We will write numbers between 0 and 1 in binary: \(0.j_1j_2j_3\dots = j_1/2 + j_2/4 + j_3/8 + \cdots\), with each \(j_k\in\{0,1\}\). For example \(0.101 = 1/2 + 0/4 + 1/8 = 5/8\). An integer \(j\) with \(n\) bits, \(j = j_1j_2\dots j_n\) (most significant first), satisfies \(j/2^n = 0.j_1j_2\dots j_n\). Multiplying by 2 shifts the binary point: \(2\times0.101 = 1.01\), and since \(\ee^{2\pi\ii\times\text{integer}} = 1\), only the fractional part matters inside \(\ee^{2\pi\ii(\cdot)}\). This is the whole trick behind the efficient circuit below.

The quantum Fourier transform

The discrete Fourier transform of Book 1 (@b1:eq-m-dft), normalised to be unitary, acts on the \(N = 2^n\) basis states of \(n\) qubits as

$$ \mathrm{QFT}\ket j = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\ee^{2\pi\ii jk/N}\ket k = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\bigotimes_{l=1}^{n}\big(\ket0 + \ee^{2\pi\ii\,j/2^l}\ket1\big). $$

The second form (a product of single-qubit states) is not obvious, so here is why. Write \(k\) in binary, \(k = \sum_lk_l2^{n-l}\). Then \(\ee^{2\pi\ii jk/N} = \prod_l\ee^{2\pi\ii jk_l/2^l}\): the exponential of a sum is a product. The sum over all \(k\) becomes a product of independent sums over each bit \(k_l\in\{0,1\}\), and each gives \(\ket0 + \ee^{2\pi\ii j/2^l}\ket1\). Using the binary-fraction notation, the phase on output qubit \(l\) is \(\ee^{2\pi\ii\,0.j_{n-l+1}\dots j_n}\): each output qubit records a few binary digits of \(j\) in its phase.

That suggests the circuit. For the qubit holding \(j_1\): a Hadamard puts it into \(\ket0 + \ee^{2\pi\ii\,0.j_1}\ket1\) (since \(\ee^{\ii\pi j_1} = (-1)^{j_1}\)). Then a controlled rotation \(R_2 = \mathrm{diag}(1,\ee^{2\pi\ii/4})\) controlled by \(j_2\) adds the next digit, giving \(\ee^{2\pi\ii\,0.j_1j_2}\); \(R_3\) controlled by \(j_3\) adds the next, and so on. Repeat for each qubit, and reverse the order of the qubits at the end. The total is \(n\) Hadamards and \(n(n-1)/2\) controlled rotations, \(O(n^2)\) gates, compared with \(O(n2^n)\) operations for the classical FFT on a vector of \(2^n\) numbers. The rotations \(R_k\) for large \(k\) are tiny and can be dropped with negligible error (the approximate QFT).

An exponentially faster Fourier transform does not mean you can Fourier-transform your data exponentially faster. The QFT transforms amplitudes, and you cannot load \(2^n\) arbitrary numbers into amplitudes cheaply, nor read \(2^n\) amplitudes out (each measurement gives one sample). The QFT is useful only inside an algorithm whose output, after the QFT, is concentrated on a few outcomes that tell you what you want. Phase estimation is such an algorithm.

Phase estimation

Problem. We have a unitary \(U\) (as a circuit we can run, including controlled versions) and a state \(\ket u\) that is an eigenvector: \(U\ket u = \ee^{2\pi\ii\varphi}\ket u\). Find \(\varphi\in[0,1)\) to \(t\) binary digits.

Circuit. Use \(t\) "counting" qubits plus the register holding \(\ket u\).

  1. Put every counting qubit in \(\ket+\) with Hadamards.
  2. For \(k = 0, \dots, t-1\), apply \(U^{2^k}\) to the register, controlled by counting qubit \(k\). By phase kickback (Chapter 3), the register stays in \(\ket u\) while the control qubit picks up the phase: \(\ket0 + \ee^{2\pi\ii\,2^k\varphi}\ket1\).
  3. The counting register is now \(\bigotimes_k(\ket0 + \ee^{2\pi\ii 2^k\varphi}\ket1)\): exactly the QFT product form of @eq:i-qft with \(j/2^t\) replaced by \(\varphi\). Apply the inverse QFT; if \(\varphi = 0.\varphi_1\dots\varphi_t\) exactly, the register becomes \(\ket{\varphi_1\dots\varphi_t}\).
  4. Measure to read the binary digits of \(\varphi\). (If \(\varphi\) has more digits, the result is the nearest \(t\)-bit value with probability at least \(4/\pi^2\approx0.4\), boosted by adding a few extra qubits.)

Worked example: one counting qubit. Take \(U = Z\) and \(\ket u = \ket1\), so \(U\ket u = -\ket u = \ee^{2\pi\ii\cdot\frac12}\ket u\) and \(\varphi = 0.1\) in binary. With \(t = 1\): Hadamard gives \(\ket+\); controlled-\(Z\) kicks back \(-1\) onto the \(\ket1\) branch, giving \(\ket-\); the inverse QFT on one qubit is \(H\), giving \(\ket1\). We measure 1, i.e. \(\varphi = 0.1_2 = \tfrac12\). Correct. This one-qubit version is exactly the Hadamard–Z–Hadamard sandwich of Chapter 1.

Precision \(2^{-t}\) costs about \(2^t\) applications of \(U\) in total. The error falls as one over the total "time" spent, a scaling (called Heisenberg scaling) that beats the \(1/\sqrt{\text{shots}}\) of repeated independent measurement. Phase estimation is how a quantum computer would find energies (with \(U = \ee^{-\ii Ht}\), the phase is \(-Et/2\pi\hbar\)) and how it finds periods.

Shor's algorithm

Why factoring matters. RSA encryption, used throughout the internet, is secure because multiplying two large primes is easy but recovering them from their product \(N\) is believed to be classically hard. The best known classical method (the number field sieve) takes time roughly \(\exp(c\,n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})\) for an \(n\)-bit number: sub-exponential, but hopeless for 2048-bit keys. Shor's algorithm [@shor1997] takes polynomial time, about \(n^3\) gates with textbook arithmetic.

Step 1: reduce factoring to period finding (classical number theory). Pick a random \(a\) with \(1 \lt a \lt N\) and \(\gcd(a,N) = 1\). The sequence \(a^1, a^2, a^3,\dots \bmod N\) is periodic; its period, the order \(r\), is the smallest \(r\) with \(a^r\equiv1\pmod N\). If \(r\) is even, then \(a^r - 1 = (a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)\equiv0\pmod N\), so \(N\) divides that product, and with probability at least ½ over the choice of \(a\), \(\gcd(a^{r/2}\pm1, N)\) is a nontrivial factor.

Step 2: find the period with phase estimation (quantum). Define \(U\ket y = \ket{ay\bmod N}\) on a register of \(\lceil\log_2N\rceil\) qubits. Its eigenvalues are \(\ee^{2\pi\ii s/r}\) for \(s = 0,\dots,r-1\), and the easily prepared state \(\ket1\) is an equal superposition of the corresponding eigenvectors. So phase estimation on \(U\) starting from \(\ket1\) returns \(s/r\) for a random \(s\).

Step 3: extract \(r\) (classical). From the measured fraction \(\approx s/r\), the continued-fraction algorithm recovers \(r\) (or a divisor of it; repeat a few times). The expensive quantum part is the controlled \(U^{2^k}\), which is modular exponentiation \(a^x\bmod N\) implemented with reversible arithmetic circuits.

Worked example: \(N = 15\). Choose \(a = 7\). Powers: \(7^1 = 7\), \(7^2 = 49 \equiv 4\), \(7^3\equiv 28\equiv 13\), \(7^4\equiv 91\equiv 1 \pmod{15}\). So \(r = 4\). Then \(a^{r/2} = 7^2\equiv4\), and \(\gcd(4-1, 15) = 3\), \(\gcd(4+1, 15) = 5\). Indeed \(15 = 3\times5\). The quantum computer's only job was to find \(r = 4\): phase estimation would return \(s/4\in\{0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34\}\), i.e. the binary fractions \(0.00, 0.01, 0.10, 0.11\).

Resource estimates. Turning the algorithm into numbers of physical qubits and hours requires choosing an arithmetic circuit, an error-correcting code (Chapter 7), a physical error rate and a cycle time. An influential 2021 analysis estimated 20 million noisy qubits for 8 hours to factor a 2048-bit number, assuming \(10^{-3}\) physical error rates [@gidney2021rsa]; refinements since have pushed the count below a million [@gidney2025rsa]. Every input to such an estimate (error rate, cycle time, connectivity) is a hardware property, which is why the review connects chip manufacture all the way to cryptographic timelines.

Hamiltonian simulation

Simulating quantum systems on classical computers is hard because the state vector grows exponentially (@b1:sec-m-tensor-exp). Feynman's original proposal was to use a quantum system to simulate another. Most physical Hamiltonians are sums of simple local terms, \(H = \sum_jH_j\) (for example, interactions between neighbouring spins or electrons). Each \(\ee^{-\ii H_jt}\) is a small circuit. But \(\ee^{-\ii(H_1+H_2)t}\ne\ee^{-\ii H_1t}\ee^{-\ii H_2t}\) when the terms do not commute (@b1:eq-m-bch). The Trotter–Suzuki product formula fixes this by alternating many short steps [@lloyd1996]:

$$ \ee^{-\ii Ht} \approx \Big(\prod_j\ee^{-\ii H_jt/r}\Big)^r, \qquad \text{error } \approx \frac{t^2}{2r}\sum_{j\lt k}\big\|[H_j,H_k]\big\| . $$

The error comes only from non-commuting pairs and falls as \(1/r\) with the number of steps; higher-order formulas (symmetric splittings) do much better. Combined with phase estimation, this gives energies of molecules and materials. Credible targets (catalysts such as nitrogenase, battery materials, correlated electron systems) typically need hundreds to thousands of logical qubits and \(10^8\)–\(10^{12}\) \(T\) gates, which places them firmly in the fault-tolerant era.

Variational and near-term algorithms

Before error correction, circuits must be short, so algorithms of the previous sections are out of reach. Variational algorithms use a short parametrised circuit \(U(\boldsymbol\theta)\) to prepare a trial state, estimate a cost such as the energy \(\expval{H}\) by repeated measurement, and let a classical optimiser adjust \(\boldsymbol\theta\) [@peruzzo2014]. They are quantum versions of the variational principle (@b3:sec-q-pert-var). Their difficulties are instructive for engineers: estimating the cost to useful precision needs many measurements (shot noise falls only as \(1/\sqrt{\text{shots}}\)), noise biases the cost, and for many circuit families the gradients vanish exponentially with size ("barren plateaus"). By 2026 the industry consensus is that useful quantum advantage requires error correction, which the rest of this book develops.

Resource estimation closes the loop from application to hardware. A target algorithm fixes a logical error budget and a logical qubit count. The code and decoder then fix the physical error rate and qubit count required (@lf:eq-dreq). The physical error rate is set by the chip's design and fabrication. A quantum design flow can be judged by how well it predicts, before a chip is made, the inputs to that chain.

  • The QFT puts the binary digits of an integer into the phases of separate qubits; it costs \(O(n^2)\) gates but cannot by itself speed up classical Fourier analysis.
  • Phase estimation: controlled powers of \(U\) write the eigenphase into a register by kickback; the inverse QFT reads it out.
  • Shor: factoring → period finding → phase estimation on modular multiplication; exponentially faster than known classical methods.
  • Trotterised Hamiltonian simulation plus phase estimation gives energies; both need fault tolerance at useful sizes.
  • Resource estimates translate algorithms into hardware requirements; their inputs are chip properties.

Exercises

  1. Write the QFT on one qubit and on two qubits as matrices.
    Solution

    One qubit: \(H\). Two qubits (\(N = 4\), \(\omega = \ee^{2\pi\ii/4} = \ii\)), entries \(\omega^{jk}/2\): \(\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&1\\1&\ii&-1&-\ii\\1&-1&1&-1\\1&-\ii&-1&\ii\end{pmatrix}\).

  2. Write \(0.011\) (binary) as a fraction, and compute \(\ee^{2\pi\ii\times4\times0.011_2}\).
    Solution

    \(0.011_2 = 1/4 + 1/8 = 3/8\). \(4\times3/8 = 3/2\), and \(\ee^{2\pi\ii\cdot3/2} = \ee^{3\pi\ii} = -1\). Equivalently, multiplying by 4 shifts the point: \(1.1_2\), fractional part \(0.1_2 = \tfrac12\), phase \(\ee^{\ii\pi} = -1\).

  3. Find the order of \(a = 2\) modulo \(N = 21\) and use it to factor 21.
    Solution

    Powers of 2 mod 21: 2, 4, 8, 16, 11, 1. So \(r = 6\), \(a^{r/2} = 8\). \(\gcd(7, 21) = 7\) and \(\gcd(9, 21) = 3\). \(21 = 3\times7\).

  4. For two terms with \(\|[H_1,H_2]\| = 1\) (units with \(\hbar = 1\)) and \(t = 10\), how many first-order Trotter steps give error below \(10^{-3}\)?
    Solution

    Error \(\approx t^2/(2r) = 50/r \lt 10^{-3}\) requires \(r > 5\times10^4\). Higher-order formulas reduce this dramatically.

  5. Phase estimation with \(t\) counting qubits costs about \(2^t\) uses of \(U\). How many uses give \(\varphi\) to 10 decimal digits? Compare with estimating a probability to that precision by repeated measurement.
    Solution

    10 decimal digits ≈ 33 bits, so about \(2^{33}\approx 9\times10^9\) uses. Estimating a probability to \(10^{-10}\) by sampling needs about \((10^{10})^2 = 10^{20}\) shots. Phase estimation wins by the square root.
