Quantum algorithms I: interference, oracles and search

Goals

  • Understand quantum parallelism and why interference, not parallelism, gives speed-ups.
  • Work through Deutsch–Jozsa and Bernstein–Vazirani in the oracle model.
  • Derive Grover's search algorithm geometrically, and know its optimality.

Parallelism and interference

A classical function \(f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}\) can be implemented reversibly as a unitary oracle \(U_f\ket x\ket y = \ket x\ket{y\oplus f(x)}\). Applying it to the uniform superposition \(H^{\otimes n}\ket{0}^{\otimes n} = 2^{-n/2}\sum_x\ket x\) evaluates \(f\) on all \(2^n\) inputs "at once". But a measurement returns only one random \(x\) with its \(f(x)\): nothing gained. Speed-ups come from arranging the computation so that amplitudes for wrong answers interfere destructively and those for the right answer constructively, before measuring. Every quantum algorithm is a careful choreography of interference.

A useful trick: if the target qubit is prepared in \(\ket-\), then \(U_f\ket x\ket- = (-1)^{f(x)}\ket x\ket-\). The function value has been moved into a phase (phase kickback). Phases are what interfere.

Deutsch–Jozsa and Bernstein–Vazirani

Suppose \(f\) is promised to be either constant or balanced (0 on exactly half the inputs). Classically, a deterministic answer needs \(2^{n-1}+1\) queries in the worst case. The Deutsch–Jozsa circuit uses one: prepare \(H^{\otimes n}\ket0^{\otimes n}\), apply the phase oracle, apply \(H^{\otimes n}\) again, and measure. The amplitude of \(\ket{0\dots0}\) is

$$ \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)} = \begin{cases}\pm1 & f \text{ constant}\\ 0 & f\text{ balanced}\end{cases} $$

so outcome \(0\dots0\) occurs with certainty if \(f\) is constant and never if it is balanced. Bernstein–Vazirani uses the same circuit for \(f(x) = s\cdot x \bmod 2\) and recovers the hidden string \(s\) in one query (classically \(n\)), because \(H^{\otimes n}\) maps \(2^{-n/2}\sum_x(-1)^{s\cdot x}\ket x\) exactly to \(\ket s\). These are toy problems, but they show the structure of useful algorithms: Hadamards create superpositions, the oracle writes phases, and a Fourier-type transform (here \(H^{\otimes n}\), the Fourier transform over bit strings) concentrates amplitude on the answer.

Grover's search

Search \(N = 2^n\) items for the one marked by \(f(w) = 1\). Classically this needs about \(N/2\) queries on average. Grover's algorithm [@grover1996] needs about \(\tfrac\pi4\sqrt N\). Start in the uniform superposition \(\ket s\). Repeat two reflections: the oracle \(O = I - 2\ketbra ww\) (flip the sign of the marked item) and the diffusion \(D = 2\ketbra ss - I\) (reflect about the mean). Everything happens in the two-dimensional plane spanned by \(\ket w\) and the uniform superposition of unmarked items \(\ket{s'}\). The starting state makes a small angle \(\theta/2\) with \(\ket{s'}\), with \(\sin(\theta/2) = 1/\sqrt N\). Two reflections compose to a rotation by \(\theta\), so after \(k\) iterations

$$ \Pr(w) = \sin^2\!\Big(\frac{(2k+1)\theta}{2}\Big), \qquad k_{\mathrm{opt}}\approx\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N . $$

Iterating too long rotates past the target, and the success probability falls again. The quadratic speed-up is provably optimal for unstructured search. Its generalisation, amplitude amplification, boosts the success probability of any quantum subroutine quadratically faster than classical repetition, and appears as a component in many algorithms.

Why quadratic speed-ups are fragile. A fault-tolerant logical operation is roughly \(10^3\)–\(10^4\) times slower than a classical processor operation, and each oracle call may contain thousands of logical gates. A \(\sqrt N\) advantage pays off only for very large \(N\), and analyses suggest that quadratic speed-ups alone are unlikely to give practical advantage on early fault-tolerant machines. Exponential speed-ups (Chapter 4) are what drive the roadmaps.

Exercises

  1. For \(n = 1\) (Deutsch's problem), list the four functions \(f:\{0,1\}\to\{0,1\}\) and show the circuit distinguishes constant from balanced with one query.
    Solution

    \(f = 0\), \(f = 1\) (constant), \(f(x) = x\), \(f(x) = 1-x\) (balanced). After \(H\), phase oracle and \(H\), the amplitude of \(\ket0\) is \(\tfrac12[(-1)^{f(0)}+(-1)^{f(1)}]\): \(\pm1\) for constant, 0 for balanced.

  2. For \(N = 4\), how many Grover iterations give the marked item with certainty?
    Solution

    \(\sin(\theta/2) = 1/2\), so \(\theta = \pi/3\). After \(k = 1\), \(\sin^2(3\theta/2) = \sin^2(\pi/2) = 1\). One iteration suffices.

  3. For \(N = 2^{40}\), estimate the optimal number of iterations and compare with the classical average.
    Solution

    \(\tfrac\pi4\sqrt{2^{40}} = \tfrac\pi4\times2^{20}\approx8.2\times10^5\) versus \(2^{39}\approx5.5\times10^{11}\).
