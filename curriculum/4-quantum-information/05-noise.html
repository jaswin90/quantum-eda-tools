Noise, fidelity and benchmarking

Goals

  • Describe what goes wrong in a real gate, and model it as a quantum channel, especially a Pauli channel.
  • Define fidelity and error rate, and estimate the error a gate must have because of finite \(T_1\) and \(T_2\).
  • Understand why tomography does not scale and how randomised benchmarking measures gate errors robustly.
  • Recognise the errors that simple models miss: coherent errors, leakage, crosstalk, correlated and drifting errors.

Every real gate is slightly wrong. The qubit loses energy to its surroundings, its frequency jitters, the control pulse is a little too strong or too weak, and neighbouring qubits interfere. To build a useful machine we must answer three questions: how to describe these imperfections in a way that is simple enough to use, how to measure them on a real chip with many qubits, and which imperfections are dangerous for error correction. This chapter answers them. The single most important number it produces, the error rate per gate, is what error correction (Chapters 6–7) consumes and what chip design (Books 6–7) must deliver.

What goes wrong in a gate

Think of a single-qubit gate on a superconducting qubit: a 25 ns microwave pulse. Several things happen besides the intended rotation.

  • Relaxation (\(T_1\)): the qubit may decay from \(\ket1\) to \(\ket0\), giving its energy to the environment (Book 3, @b3:sec-q-open-lindblad).
  • Dephasing (\(T_\varphi\)): the qubit's frequency fluctuates, so the phase between \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) drifts randomly.
  • Control errors: the pulse rotates by \(\pi(1+\epsilon)\) instead of \(\pi\), or about a slightly tilted axis.
  • Leakage: the pulse excites the qubit's third level \(\ket2\), outside the qubit space.
  • Crosstalk: the pulse, or the qubit's coupling, also nudges a neighbour.

We need a mathematical object that captures all of these. That object is the quantum channel.

Gate errors as channels

A noisy process maps an input density matrix \(\rho\) (Book 3, Chapter 7) to an output density matrix. Any physically possible such map, a channel, can be written with Kraus operators as \(\mathcal E(\rho) = \sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger\) with \(\sum_kK_k^\dagger K_k = I\) (@b3:eq-q-kraus). Think of it as: "with some probability, operation \(K_k\) happens", although the \(K_k\) need not be unitary. A real gate is modelled as the ideal unitary \(U\) followed by an error channel: \(\tilde{\mathcal U} = \mathcal E\circ\mathcal U\).

A general channel on \(n\) qubits has \(16^n - 4^n\) real parameters (12 for one qubit, 240 for two). That is far too many to measure or reason about for a whole processor, so we simplify.

The Pauli channel. The most useful simplification says: after the gate, with some probability, an extra Pauli error (\(X\), \(Y\) or \(Z\) on some qubits) is applied:

$$ \mathcal E(\rho) = \sum_{P\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}} p_P\,P\rho P, \qquad \sum_Pp_P = 1 . $$

For one qubit there are just three numbers: \(p_X\) (bit flip), \(p_Z\) (phase flip) and \(p_Y\) (both). The depolarising channel is the special case \(p_X = p_Y = p_Z = p/3\): "with probability \(p\), a random Pauli error". Pauli channels are popular for two reasons. They are easy to simulate on large codes (Chapter 6 shows that Pauli errors on Clifford circuits are classically efficient to track). And any channel can be converted into a Pauli channel with the same average error by Pauli twirling: surround the gate with randomly chosen Paulis before and compensating Paulis after. On average, the off-diagonal "coherent" parts of the error cancel. Experiments use this deliberately ("randomised compiling").

Why Paulis? Because any single-qubit error operator can be expanded as \(e_0I + e_xX + e_yY + e_zZ\), and an error-correcting code that measures its syndrome forces the error to "choose" one of these terms (Chapter 6). So Paulis are the natural alphabet of errors, just as bit flips are for classical bits.

How much error does decoherence force?

Suppose a gate takes time \(t_g\), and during it the qubit relaxes and dephases. For an excited qubit, the probability of decaying in time \(t_g\) is about \(t_g/T_1\) (since \(\ee^{-t_g/T_1}\approx1 - t_g/T_1\) for short gates). Converting amplitude damping and dephasing into Pauli probabilities by twirling, and averaging over input states, gives

$$ p_X = p_Y \approx \frac{t_g}{4T_1}, \qquad p_Z\approx\frac{t_g}{2T_2} - \frac{t_g}{4T_1}, \qquad 1 - \bar F \approx \frac{t_g}{3}\Big(\frac{1}{T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\varphi}\Big) , $$

where \(1/T_2 = 1/2T_1 + 1/T_\varphi\) (@b3:eq-q-blocheq) and \(\bar F\) is the average gate fidelity defined below. Read the last formula as "error ≈ gate time divided by coherence time, times one third". It is the coherence limit: even a perfectly calibrated gate cannot do better.

Worked example: coherence limits. A qubit has \(T_1 = T_\varphi = 100\) µs. A 25 ns single-qubit gate: \(1-\bar F\approx\frac{25\times10^{-9}}{3}\times(10^4 + 10^4) = 1.7\times10^{-4}\). A 60 ns two-qubit gate acts on two such qubits; roughly doubling the single-qubit estimate at the longer duration gives \(2\times\frac{60\times10^{-9}}{3}\times2\times10^4 = 8\times10^{-4}\). The surface code's threshold is about \(10^{-2}\) (Chapter 7), so these qubits are a factor of 10–60 below threshold if control is perfect. Halving gate time or doubling coherence halves the error: this is why both fast gates and long coherence are pursued.

Measures of gate quality

How do we summarise "how close" a noisy gate is to the ideal one with one number?

  • The average gate fidelity \(\bar F\): prepare a random pure input state \(\ket\psi\), apply the noisy gate, and compute the overlap of the output with the ideal output \(U\ket\psi\); average over all \(\ket\psi\). It answers "on a typical input, how often does the gate do the right thing?".
  • The process (entanglement) fidelity \(F_e\): for a Pauli channel it is just \(p_I\), the probability that no error happened. It relates to the average fidelity by
$$ \bar F = \frac{dF_e + 1}{d+1}, \qquad d = 2^n . $$

The error rate \(r = 1 - \bar F\) is the number quoted in papers and press releases ("99.9% two-qubit fidelity" means \(r = 10^{-3}\)). For one qubit, \(r = \tfrac23(p_X+p_Y+p_Z)\): the factor \(\tfrac23\) appears because a random input state is unaffected by some errors (a \(Z\) error does nothing to \(\ket0\)).

Two gates with the same average fidelity can behave very differently in a long computation. A stochastic error (random Pauli with probability \(p\)) accumulates linearly: after \(N\) gates, error \(\approx Np\). A coherent error (the same small over-rotation \(\epsilon\) every time) adds up in amplitude: after \(N\) gates the rotation error is \(N\epsilon\) and the infidelity \(\approx(N\epsilon)^2/6\), growing as \(N^2\). The average fidelity of one gate, \(\approx\epsilon^2/6\), hides this. The worst-case measure, the "diamond-norm" distance, can be as large as \(\sqrt r\) for coherent errors. This is a major reason experiments randomise their gates (twirling), turning coherent errors into stochastic ones.

Tomography, and why it does not scale

State tomography reconstructs \(\rho\) by measuring many copies in several bases (for one qubit: \(X\), \(Y\) and \(Z\), giving the Bloch vector). Process tomography reconstructs a channel by doing state tomography on its outputs for a complete set of inputs. Two problems make it impractical for large systems. The number of settings grows exponentially (\(4^n\) for states, \(16^n\) for processes). And the result mixes the error of the gate with the errors of preparing inputs and measuring outputs (SPAM errors: state preparation and measurement), which are often larger than the gate error itself. Tomography is used on one or two qubits, mostly for diagnosis.

Randomised benchmarking

Randomised benchmarking (RB) [@magesan2011] measures a gate error that is immune to SPAM and scales to many qubits. The recipe:

  1. Choose a random sequence of \(m\) Clifford gates (Chapter 1: the gates generated by \(H\), \(S\), CNOT).
  2. Compute the single Clifford gate that undoes the whole sequence (easy classically, by Gottesman–Knill), and append it. Ideally, the qubit returns to its starting state.
  3. Run it, measure, and record whether the qubit came back ("survival probability").
  4. Repeat for many random sequences and several lengths \(m\); average.

Averaging over random Cliffords twirls whatever the noise is into a depolarising channel with some parameter \(p\) per gate. The survival probability therefore decays exponentially in \(m\):

$$ F(m) = A\,p^m + B, \qquad r = \frac{(d-1)(1-p)}{d} . $$

SPAM errors only change the constants \(A\) and \(B\), not the decay rate \(p\), so \(r\) is a clean error per Clifford (@lf:eq-rb). @fig:i-rb shows typical decays. Variants: interleaved RB inserts one specific gate after each random Clifford and compares decay rates to isolate that gate's error; simultaneous RB runs RB on many qubits at once to expose crosstalk; cross-entropy benchmarking uses random non-Clifford circuits and compares output statistics with simulation.

Worked example: reading an RB curve. A fit gives \(p = 0.998\) on one qubit. Then \(r = (2-1)(1-0.998)/2 = 10^{-3}\) per Clifford. On many machines a Clifford averages about 1.9 physical pulses, so the error per physical gate is about \(5\times10^{-4}\). If the coherence limit from @eq:i-coh is \(1.7\times10^{-4}\), then about two thirds of the error comes from something else: calibration, leakage or crosstalk. Comparing measured errors with coherence limits is the first diagnostic step on any new device.

Errors the simple models miss

  • Leakage. A transmon has levels above \(\ket1\). Population in \(\ket2\) is outside the qubit space; it is not a Pauli error, it persists until reset, and it spreads to neighbours through two-qubit gates. Error-correction schemes add "leakage reduction units" to pump it back.
  • Crosstalk. Driving one qubit disturbs another, through stray capacitance, shared control lines, or the always-on \(ZZ\) interaction between coupled qubits (@lf:eq-zz). Errors then depend on what the neighbours are doing.
  • Correlated errors. A cosmic ray or a radioactive decay in the chip's substrate creates a burst of quasiparticles that causes errors on many qubits at once for about a millisecond. Error correction assumes errors are independent and handles such bursts badly (@lf:sec-qec-dem).
  • Drift. Microscopic defects (two-level systems) move in frequency, so a qubit's \(T_1\) and frequency change over hours. A benchmark is a snapshot; a device has a distribution of error rates over time.

Benchmarks are the measured outputs that a predictive design flow must reproduce. The review's proposed "die record" (@lf:sec-prob-record) links each chip's layout and fabrication data with its measured RB errors, coherence and crosstalk, so that models can be trained and validated against real chips rather than against other simulations.

  • A noisy gate = ideal unitary followed by an error channel; Pauli channels (random \(X\), \(Y\), \(Z\) with given probabilities) are the workhorse model.
  • Coherence limit: error ≈ \(\tfrac{t_g}{3}(1/T_1 + 1/T_\varphi)\) per qubit; faster gates and longer coherence both help.
  • Error rate \(r = 1-\bar F\); coherent errors grow quadratically in circuit depth and are hidden by the average.
  • Randomised benchmarking fits \(Ap^m + B\) to random Clifford sequences and gives an error per gate that ignores SPAM errors.
  • Leakage, crosstalk, correlated bursts and drift are outside the simple model and matter most for error correction.

Exercises

  1. A single-qubit depolarising channel has \(p_X = p_Y = p_Z = 10^{-4}\). What are \(F_e\), \(\bar F\) and \(r\)?
    Solution

    \(F_e = p_I = 1 - 3\times10^{-4}\). \(\bar F = (2F_e+1)/3 = 1 - 2\times10^{-4}\). So \(r = 2\times10^{-4}\).

  2. Apply the bit-flip channel \(\rho\to(1-p)\rho + pX\rho X\) to \(\ket0\) and to \(\ket+\). Which state is unaffected, and why?
    Solution

    \(\ket0\): \((1-p)\ketbra00 + p\ketbra11\), a mixture. \(\ket+\): \(X\ket+ = \ket+\), so the output is \(\ketbra{+}{+}\), unchanged. \(\ket+\) is an eigenstate of \(X\), so an \(X\) error does nothing to it. That is why average fidelity is less than \(1-p\): some inputs are immune to some errors.

  3. Estimate the coherence-limited error of a 40 ns CZ gate between two qubits each with \(T_1 = 150\) µs and \(T_\varphi = 200\) µs.
    Solution

    Per qubit: \(\tfrac{40\,\mathrm{ns}}{3}(1/150 + 1/200)\,\mu\mathrm s^{-1} = 13.3\times10^{-3}\times0.01167\approx1.56\times10^{-4}\). Two qubits: about \(3.1\times10^{-4}\).

  4. An RB experiment fits \(p = 0.9985\) per Clifford on one qubit. What is \(r\)? If a Clifford averages 1.875 physical gates, what is the error per physical gate?
    Solution

    \(r = (1-p)/2 = 7.5\times10^{-4}\). Per physical gate \(\approx7.5\times10^{-4}/1.875 = 4\times10^{-4}\).

  5. A gate over-rotates by \(\epsilon = 0.01\) rad every time. What is its infidelity? After 100 repetitions in a row, compare the coherent result with what 100 independent stochastic errors of the same single-gate infidelity would give.
    Solution

    Single gate: \(\epsilon^2/6\approx1.7\times10^{-5}\). Coherent: total rotation error 1 rad, infidelity \(\approx(1)^2/6\approx0.17\) (the small-angle formula is rough here, but the order is right). Stochastic: \(100\times1.7\times10^{-5} = 1.7\times10^{-3}\). The coherent case is a hundred times worse.

  6. Why can RB not tell you which kind of error a gate has (for example, \(T_1\) versus over-rotation)? Name an experiment that could.
    Solution

    Twirling deliberately averages every error into the same depolarising form, so only its size survives. To diagnose the type, use experiments sensitive to specific errors: repeating the same gate many times without randomisation amplifies coherent over-rotations (they grow quadratically), comparing the RB error with the \(T_1\)/\(T_2\) coherence limit separates incoherent from other errors, and gate-set tomography reconstructs the full channel with SPAM separated.
