Entanglement as a resource: no-cloning, teleportation and networks

Goals

  • Prove the no-cloning theorem and understand its consequences for error correction.
  • Work through quantum teleportation and superdense coding.
  • Understand Bell measurements, entanglement swapping and why they matter for modular and photonic architectures.

No cloning

Classical error correction copies data. Quantum mechanics forbids it [@wootters1982]. Suppose a unitary \(U\) copied every state: \(U\ket\psi\ket0 = \ket\psi\ket\psi\) and \(U\ket\phi\ket0 = \ket\phi\ket\phi\). Taking the inner product of the two equations and using unitarity gives \(\braket\phi\psi = \braket\phi\psi^2\), so \(\braket\phi\psi\) is 0 or 1. Only orthogonal states (which are effectively classical bits) can be copied. The same conclusion holds for any quantum operation, not only unitaries. Two consequences matter here. Quantum error correction must protect information without copying it, by spreading it into entanglement (Chapter 6). And quantum information cannot be amplified like a classical signal, so loss on a photonic channel cannot be fixed by a repeater that copies (Book 5).

Teleportation

Alice has a qubit in an unknown state \(\ket\psi = \alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\) and shares a Bell pair \(\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB}\) with Bob. The joint state of Alice's two qubits and Bob's one can be rewritten exactly in the Bell basis of Alice's pair [@bennett1993]:

$$ \ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac12\Big[\ket{\Phi^+}\ket\psi + \ket{\Phi^-}Z\ket\psi + \ket{\Psi^+}X\ket\psi + \ket{\Psi^-}XZ\ket\psi\Big] . $$

Alice measures her two qubits in the Bell basis (a CNOT, a Hadamard and two \(Z\) measurements). Each of the four outcomes is equally likely and reveals nothing about \(\alpha,\beta\). She sends the two classical bits to Bob, who applies \(I\), \(Z\), \(X\) or \(XZ\) accordingly, and holds \(\ket\psi\). No cloning is violated (Alice's copy is destroyed by her measurement) and nothing travels faster than light (Bob needs the classical bits). Teleportation is the template for many hardware operations: moving logical qubits between modules, implementing gates by "gate teleportation" with prepared resource states (Chapter 7's magic states), and fusion-based photonic computing (Book 5).

Superdense coding

Run the protocol the other way. If Alice and Bob share \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), Alice can apply one of \(I, X, Z, XZ\) to her half, turning the pair into one of the four Bell states, and send her qubit to Bob. Bob's Bell measurement distinguishes them, so one transmitted qubit carries two classical bits. Teleportation (2 bits + 1 ebit → 1 qubit) and superdense coding (1 qubit + 1 ebit → 2 bits) show that entanglement is a consumable resource with an exchange rate.

Entanglement swapping and networks

If Alice–Bob share a Bell pair and Bob–Charlie share another, a Bell measurement on Bob's two qubits leaves Alice and Charlie entangled, though they never interacted: entanglement swapping. Chains of swaps, combined with purification, are the principle of quantum repeaters. In computing, the same idea links modules: two chips connected by optical or microwave links can share Bell pairs generated by sending photons and performing a Bell measurement in the middle, and then teleport gates between modules. Every modular roadmap in the industry (multi-chip superconducting systems, linked ion traps, networked neutral-atom arrays) depends on the rate and fidelity of such links, which become hardware specifications like any other.

Linear optics cannot do a complete Bell measurement. With only beam splitters, phase shifters and photon detectors, at most two of the four Bell states of two photons can be distinguished, so a linear-optical Bell measurement succeeds with probability at most 50%. This single fact shapes photonic quantum computing: computation must be organised around probabilistic "fusion" measurements and redundancy (Book 5, @b5:sec-p-qc).

Exercises

  1. Verify @eq:i-tele for the coefficient of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) on Alice's side.
    Solution

    Write \(\ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\alpha\ket{000}+\alpha\ket{011}+\beta\ket{100}+\beta\ket{111})\) (qubits: Alice's input, Alice's half, Bob). Project Alice's two qubits onto \(\bra{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\bra{00}+\bra{11})\): terms \(\ket{00}\ket0\) and \(\ket{11}\ket1\) survive, giving \(\tfrac12(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\).

  2. In superdense coding, which operation must Alice apply to send "11" if the code is \(00\to I\), \(01\to X\), \(10\to Z\), \(11\to XZ\), and which Bell state does Bob receive?
    Solution

    \((XZ\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(XZ\ket0\ket0 + XZ\ket1\ket1) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket1\ket0 - \ket0\ket1) = -\ket{\Psi^-}\).

  3. Show that no unitary can map \(\ket\psi\ket0\to\ket\psi\ket\psi\) for both \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\).
    Solution

    \(\braket{0}{+} = 1/\sqrt2\), but cloning would require \(\braket0+ = \braket0+^2 = 1/2\). Contradiction.
