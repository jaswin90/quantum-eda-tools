Where coherence is lost: loss and noise in superconducting circuits

Goals

  • Build a \(T_1\) budget: list every way a qubit can lose energy and add up the rates.
  • Compute dielectric loss from participation ratios and loss tangents, and see why thin surface layers dominate.
  • Understand two-level systems (TLS): what they are, why their loss saturates, and why they make \(T_1\) fluctuate.
  • Know the other relaxation channels (quasiparticles, radiation, Purcell, package modes) and the dephasing channels (flux noise, charge noise, photon shot noise).

A qubit's coherence times \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (Book 3, Chapter 8) decide how many gates it can perform before errors take over. Where do they come from? A qubit is an electromagnetic circuit, and anything that can absorb a little of its energy, or jiggle its frequency, shortens them. The main culprits are not exotic: thin layers of oxide and contamination on the surfaces of the metal and substrate, a few nanometres thick, which contain microscopic defects that absorb microwave energy. This chapter shows how to add up all the loss channels into a budget, and why both the layout (how much electric field touches those surfaces) and the process (how lossy the surfaces are) matter. That makes coherence, in part, something a designer can engineer.

The \(T_1\) budget

A qubit relaxes through every channel that can absorb energy at its frequency. Independent channels add their rates (@b1:sec-m-ode-first; @b2:eq-c-qsum), so the budget is written as a sum (@lf:eq-t1):

$$ \frac{1}{T_1} = \omega_q\sum_i p_i\tan\delta_i + \Gamma_P + \Gamma_{qp} + \Gamma_{\mathrm{rad}} + \Gamma_{\mathrm{lines}} + \dots, \qquad p_i = \frac{\int_{V_i}\varepsilon|\mathbf E|^2\dd V}{\int\varepsilon|\mathbf E|^2\dd V} . $$

Term by term:

  • Dielectric loss \(\omega_q\sum_ip_i\tan\delta_i\): the qubit's electric field stores a fraction \(p_i\) of its energy (the participation ratio) in each material region \(i\), and each region loses a fraction \(\tan\delta_i\) (its loss tangent, @b2:sec-c-max-dielectric) of that energy per radian of oscillation.
  • \(\Gamma_P\): Purcell decay through the readout resonator (Chapter 4).
  • \(\Gamma_{qp}\): quasiparticles tunnelling through the junction (Chapter 1).
  • \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{rad}}\): radiation into free space, package modes and spurious chip modes.
  • \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{lines}}\): energy leaking out through the drive and flux lines.

Because the rates add, the weakest link dominates: improving a channel that contributes 5% of the total gains almost nothing.

Participation ratios: where the field lives

The regions that matter for a coplanar transmon are:

  • the bulk substrate (crystalline silicon or sapphire), which holds most of the field (\(p\approx0.9\)) but is very clean (\(\tan\delta\lesssim10^{-7}\));
  • three thin interfaces, each only nanometres thick: metal–substrate (MS, between the metal film and the substrate), substrate–air (SA, the exposed substrate surface between metal patterns) and metal–air (MA, the oxidised top surface of the metal). Their participations are small, \(10^{-4}\)–\(10^{-3}\) each, but their loss tangents are large, about \(10^{-3}\) [@wang2015; @gambetta2017].

Why do nanometre-thin layers matter at all? Because the electric field of a thin metal film is concentrated at its edges (it diverges like \(r^{-1/2}\) near a sharp edge, @b2:sec-c-stat-cond). The interfaces right at the edges of the capacitor pads, where the field is strongest, sit exactly where the material is most damaged by fabrication. A thin, very lossy layer in a strong field can lose more energy than a thick, clean bulk in a moderate field.

Worked example: a participation budget. A 5 GHz transmon has \(p_{\mathrm{sub}} = 0.9\) with \(\tan\delta = 10^{-7}\), and \(p_{\mathrm{MS}} = 3\times10^{-4}\), \(p_{\mathrm{SA}} = 5\times10^{-4}\), \(p_{\mathrm{MA}} = 1\times10^{-4}\), all with \(\tan\delta = 2\times10^{-3}\).

Substrate: \(0.9\times10^{-7} = 9\times10^{-8}\). Interfaces: \((3+5+1)\times10^{-4}\times2\times10^{-3} = 1.8\times10^{-6}\). Total \(\sum p_i\tan\delta_i\approx1.89\times10^{-6}\), so the quality factor is \(Q = 1/1.89\times10^{-6}\approx5.3\times10^5\), and \(T_1 = Q/\omega_q = 5.3\times10^5/(2\pi\times5\times10^9)\approx17\) µs from dielectrics alone.

The interfaces cause 95% of the loss. Halving every interface participation (by geometry) or halving the interface loss tangent (by process) roughly doubles \(T_1\). Modern devices with \(T_1\) of 200–500 µs reach \(Q\) of several million by doing both.

So there are two levers. Geometry, the designer's lever: wider gaps between pads and ground, larger pads and rounded corners dilute the edge fields and lower \(p_{\mathrm{MS}}, p_{\mathrm{SA}}, p_{\mathrm{MA}}\), at the cost of chip area and stronger coupling to unwanted package modes. Materials and surfaces, the process engineer's lever: removing native oxides before deposition, using tantalum (whose oxide is less lossy) instead of niobium, cleaner interfaces, and etching trenches into the substrate between metal lines so that less field passes through the SA surface [@place2021; @deleon2021]. Computing participations accurately requires very fine simulation meshes at the metal edges, a key accuracy problem for design tools (@lf:sec-design-accuracy).

Two-level systems

What makes the amorphous surface layers lossy? Mostly microscopic two-level systems (TLS) [@muller2019]: an atom or small group of atoms in a disordered material that can sit in either of two nearly equivalent positions, tunnelling between them (@b3:sec-q-dyn-tunnel). If the configuration carries an electric dipole, the TLS couples to the qubit's field. A TLS whose energy splitting is near the qubit frequency absorbs the qubit's energy and then loses it to the lattice as heat.

Because each TLS can only hold one quantum of energy at a time, TLS loss saturates: at high microwave power (many photons) or high temperature (TLS already thermally excited), they are busy and absorb less. The standard model is

$$ \tan\delta_{\mathrm{TLS}}(\bar n, T) = \tan\delta_0\,\frac{\tanh(\hbar\omega/2k_BT)}{\sqrt{1 + \bar n/n_c}} . $$

This is why materials loss is measured with resonators at single-photon power and millikelvin temperature (@lf:eq-tls): high-power measurements underestimate the loss that qubits, which hold a single quantum, actually see.

\(T_1\) is not a fixed number. Individual TLS slowly drift in frequency, because they interact with other, slower fluctuators. When a TLS strongly coupled to a qubit drifts through the qubit frequency, \(T_1\) can drop by a factor of several for minutes to hours, then recover [@klimov2018]. A qubit's \(T_1\) is therefore a random process with a wide distribution. A design tool should predict a distribution of \(T_1\), and error-correction analyses should use it, not a single average.

Other relaxation channels

  • Quasiparticles (@sec:h-sc-qp): a quasiparticle tunnelling across the junction can absorb the qubit's energy. The rate is proportional to the quasiparticle density, reduced by infrared shielding, phonon traps and gap engineering.
  • Radiation and correlated events: a gamma ray or cosmic-ray muon deposits energy in the substrate; the resulting phonons create quasiparticles across the whole chip, suppressing \(T_1\) on many qubits for about a millisecond [@mcewen2022; @vepsalainen2020]. On a centimetre-scale chip such events occur roughly every ten seconds, and they produce correlated errors that error correction handles poorly (@b4:sec-i-qec2).
  • Purcell and line coupling (@sec:h-cq-purcell): computed from the real part of the admittance seen by the junction.
  • Package and box modes: the chip and its metal enclosure form a cavity whose modes fall lower in frequency as the chip gets bigger (@lf:eq-box). A mode near a qubit frequency drains it. Through-substrate vias, airbridges and carefully designed packages push these modes up or damp them (@lf:sec-pkg-modes).
  • Slotline and radiation modes: if the two ground planes on either side of a coplanar line are not tied together, an unwanted mode can propagate and radiate; airbridges across the line every few hundred micrometres suppress it.

Dephasing

Dephasing comes from slow fluctuations of the qubit's frequency, which randomise the phase of superpositions (@b3:sec-q-open-spectra). The main sources:

  • Flux noise: magnetic flux through SQUID loops fluctuates with a \(1/f\) spectrum, about 1–5 \(\mu\Phi_0/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}\) at 1 Hz, believed to come from unpaired electron spins on metal surfaces. It affects only flux-tunable qubits and couplers, through the slope \(\partial\omega/\partial\Phi\) (Chapter 2), which vanishes at the "sweet spot".
  • Charge noise: stray charges hopping in the substrate shift \(n_g\). Negligible for transmons because of their flat bands (Chapter 3), but important for charge qubits and spin qubits.
  • Photon shot noise: residual photons in the readout resonator shift the qubit frequency by \(2\chi\) each (Chapter 4), so random photon numbers dephase it at a rate \(\approx\bar n_{\mathrm{th}}\kappa\cdot4\chi^2/(\kappa^2+4\chi^2)\) (@lf:eq-dephase). This is why readout lines need heavy attenuation (@b2:sec-c-th-cryo).
  • Critical-current fluctuations of the junction: usually small, but a floor for the most coherent devices.

Every term in @eq:h-t1 depends on the layout (through participations, admittances and package modes) and on the process (through loss tangents and the quasiparticle environment). That makes coherence partly designable. The review's materials section argues for exactly this: participations computed with converged meshes at metal edges, combined with loss tangents measured on the same process, to predict \(T_1\) distributions before fabrication (@lf:sec-mat-gap).

  • \(1/T_1\) is a sum of independent channels; the largest dominates.
  • Dielectric loss = \(\omega\sum p_i\tan\delta_i\); thin interface layers dominate because the field is concentrated at metal edges.
  • Two levers: geometry lowers participation, process lowers loss tangent; both are needed for \(T_1\) of hundreds of µs.
  • Two-level systems cause saturable loss and make \(T_1\) fluctuate in time; predict distributions, not single numbers.
  • Quasiparticles, radiation bursts, Purcell decay and package modes are further channels; flux noise, charge noise and photon shot noise cause dephasing.

Exercises

  1. A resonator measured at high power shows \(Q_i = 2\times10^6\); at single-photon power, \(Q_i = 4\times10^5\). Assuming the difference is saturable TLS loss, what is the low-power TLS loss and what fraction of the total does it contribute?
    Solution

    \(1/Q_{\mathrm{low}} - 1/Q_{\mathrm{high}} = 2.5\times10^{-6} - 0.5\times10^{-6} = 2\times10^{-6}\): 80% of the low-power loss is TLS.

  2. Using the worked example, which single improvement gives the larger \(T_1\): eliminating substrate loss entirely, or reducing SA participation by 40%?
    Solution

    Substrate: removes \(9\times10^{-8}\) of \(1.89\times10^{-6}\), about 5%. SA: removes \(0.4\times5\times10^{-4}\times2\times10^{-3} = 4\times10^{-7}\), about 21%. Interfaces dominate.

  3. A qubit has \(1/T_1\) contributions (in units of \(10^3\) s\(^{-1}\)): dielectric 5.0, Purcell 1.0, quasiparticles 0.5, lines 0.3. What is \(T_1\)? What if a Purcell filter removes the Purcell term entirely?
    Solution

    Total \(6.8\times10^3\) s\(^{-1}\), \(T_1 = 147\) µs. Without Purcell: \(5.8\times10^3\) s\(^{-1}\), \(T_1 = 172\) µs, a 17% gain. Dielectric loss still dominates.

  4. A flux-tunable transmon at 0.25 \(\Phi_0\) from its sweet spot has \(\partial f/\partial\Phi\approx2\) GHz/\(\Phi_0\). With slow flux noise of \(5\,\mu\Phi_0\) rms, estimate the rms frequency jitter and \(T_2^*\).
    Solution

    \(\sigma_f = 2\times10^9\times5\times10^{-6} = 10\) kHz. Gaussian decay \(\ee^{-(2\pi\sigma_ft)^2/2}\) reaches \(1/\ee\) at \(t = \sqrt2/(2\pi\sigma_f)\approx22\) µs. At the sweet spot, first-order sensitivity vanishes and \(T_2^*\) is much longer.

  5. Using @eq:h-tls, by what factor does TLS loss fall between \(\bar n = 1\) and \(\bar n = 10^4\) photons if \(n_c = 10\)? What does this imply about measuring materials with a strong drive?
    Solution

    \(\sqrt{1+0.1} = 1.05\) versus \(\sqrt{1+1000} = 31.6\): a factor of about 30. A strong-drive measurement would report a loss 30 times smaller than what a single-photon qubit sees.
