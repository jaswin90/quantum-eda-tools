Other qubits: trapped ions, neutral atoms, spins and topological proposals

Goals

  • For each of trapped ions, neutral atoms and semiconductor spins: know what the qubit is, how it is held, how gates and readout work, and what limits it.
  • Know, for each, what is manufactured and therefore what varies from device to device.
  • Know what topological qubits promise and their current status.
  • Compare platforms on speed, accuracy, connectivity and manufacturing.

Superconducting circuits are one way to build qubits; nature offers others. Trapped ions and neutral atoms use real atoms, which are perfectly identical, held in place by electric fields or laser light. Spin qubits use single electrons trapped in silicon nanostructures, much like transistors. Topological qubits, if they work, would store information in a way that is protected by the mathematics of the material. Each platform answers the same questions differently: what is the qubit, how do you control it, what limits it, and what in it has to be manufactured. This chapter asks those questions of each, at the level needed to read the review's section on other modalities.

Trapped ions

The qubit. Two energy levels of a single atomic ion, for example ytterbium-171 or calcium-40 with one electron removed. Either two "hyperfine" ground-state levels split by a few GHz (extremely long coherence, seconds to minutes), or a ground level and a long-lived optical level. Every ion of a given isotope is identical by nature, so there is no qubit-to-qubit variation at all [@haffner2008].

The trap. You cannot hold a charged particle still with static electric fields alone: Laplace's equation (@b2:sec-c-stat-field) forbids a potential minimum in free space (Exercise 1). A Paul trap adds a radio-frequency field oscillating at \(\Omega/2\pi\approx10\)–50 MHz. The ion jiggles in the fast field, and averaged over that jiggling it feels an effective "pseudopotential" proportional to \(|\mathbf E_{\mathrm{rf}}|^2\), which does have a minimum where the RF field vanishes (@lf:eq-pseudo). Modern traps are surface-electrode chips: patterned metal electrodes on a substrate, with ions floating 50–100 µm above. Static voltages on tens to thousands of extra electrodes shape the potential and move ions between zones of the chip.

Gates. Ions in a common trap repel each other and form a chain whose collective vibrations (like masses on springs, @b1:sec-m-ode-systems) are shared by all ions. These vibrational modes act as a quantum bus. Laser beams (or microwave fields) tuned near a vibrational sideband couple an ion's internal state to the shared motion; the Mølmer–Sørensen gate [@molmer1999] uses a pair of tones to create an effective \(X\otimes X\) interaction between two ions via the motion, and works even if the motion is not perfectly cooled. Two-qubit errors below \(10^{-3}\) are routine.

Readout. A laser tuned to a transition that only one qubit state can scatter: that state glows (thousands of photons per millisecond, collected by a camera or photodetector), the other stays dark.

Limits. Gates are slow, 10–200 µs, a thousand times slower than superconducting gates. Electric-field noise from electrode surfaces heats the ion's motion, with a rate that rises steeply as the ion approaches the surface (about \(d^{-4}\)), which sets a minimum trap size (@lf:sec-mod-ion). Scaling to many ions needs ion transport between zones, junctions, and integrated optics to deliver laser beams.

What is manufactured. The trap chip: electrode geometry (whose potentials are computed with electrostatic solvers, the same mathematics as capacitance extraction, @b2:sec-c-statics), multilayer wiring for hundreds of electrodes, integrated waveguides and grating couplers for laser delivery, and, in microwave-gate designs, current-carrying structures. The qubits do not vary; the trap does.

Neutral atoms

The qubit. Two hyperfine or nuclear-spin states of a neutral atom (rubidium, caesium, strontium, ytterbium). Again, identical by nature.

The trap. Optical tweezers: tightly focused laser beams. An atom's electrons are polarised by the light's field, and the induced dipole is attracted to the region of highest intensity, the focus. Arrays of hundreds to thousands of tweezers are made with acousto-optic deflectors or spatial light modulators. Atoms load randomly (each tweezer catches an atom about half the time), and then moving tweezers rearrange them into a defect-free pattern.

Gates. Single-qubit gates use lasers or microwaves. Two-qubit gates use Rydberg states: highly excited levels (principal quantum number 50–100) in which the outer electron orbits far from the nucleus, giving a huge electric dipole. Two Rydberg atoms within several micrometres interact so strongly that the energy to excite the second one is shifted out of resonance: only one of them can be excited at a time (the Rydberg blockade). A sequence of laser pulses that exploits the blockade gives a CZ gate [@jaksch2000; @saffman2010]. Fidelities above 99.5% have been demonstrated [@evered2023].

Limits. Atoms are occasionally lost from their tweezers (an erasure, which codes handle well if detected); Rydberg states decay; laser intensity and phase noise limit fidelity; readout is slower than for superconducting qubits and often destructive.

Strength. Reconfigurability: atoms can be physically moved mid-computation, giving flexible connectivity that suits qLDPC codes (@b4:sec-i-qec2-ldpc).

What is manufactured. Not the qubits. The engineering is in optics (increasingly on photonic integrated circuits and spatial light modulators), lasers, vacuum systems and control (@lf:sec-mod-atom).

Semiconductor spin qubits

The qubit. The spin (up or down) of a single electron, or hole, confined in a quantum dot: a tiny region of a silicon or germanium chip, a few tens of nanometres across, defined by voltages on metal gate electrodes above it [@loss1998]. The dot is a particle in a box (@b3:sec-q-dyn-wave), with level spacings of meV. In a magnetic field \(B\), spin up and down differ in energy by \(hf = g\mu_BB\) (@b3:sec-q-spin-pauli), about 28 GHz per tesla. Isotopically purified silicon-28, which has no nuclear spins, gives long coherence.

Gates. Single-qubit gates by electron spin resonance (an oscillating magnetic field) or, more commonly, electric-dipole spin resonance (oscillating the electron back and forth in a magnetic-field gradient produced by a micromagnet, so it "sees" an oscillating field). Two-qubit gates use the exchange interaction between electrons in neighbouring dots, \(J\approx4t^2/U\) (@b3:sec-q-sec-tb), switched on by lowering the barrier between dots with a gate voltage, which increases the tunnelling \(t\).

Readout. Spin-to-charge conversion: only one spin state is allowed to tunnel to a neighbouring dot or reservoir (by Pauli exclusion or energy), and a nearby charge sensor detects whether an electron moved.

Limits and manufacturing. Spin qubits are made on CMOS-like production lines with transistor-like gate stacks [@zwerver2022], promising very dense integration and control electronics on the same chip. But every dot's operating voltages depend on local disorder: charge traps, interface roughness, and in silicon the "valley splitting" (a material property that varies atom by atom). So threshold voltages differ from dot to dot and must be tuned individually. Charge noise couples to qubits through \(\partial J/\partial V\) and the micromagnet gradient. Because \(J\) depends exponentially on gate voltage through \(t\), small voltage variations change gate speeds a lot. What most needs a manufacturing model is the variation of dot parameters across a wafer, which a quantum-dot compact model in a process design kit would capture (@lf:sec-mod-spin).

Topological qubits

Certain superconductor–semiconductor nanowires in a magnetic field are predicted to host Majorana zero modes at their ends: exotic states such that a pair of them, at opposite ends of a wire, can store one qubit non-locally. Local noise acting on one end cannot change the stored information, so the qubit would be protected by the physics of the material rather than by active error correction [@nayak2008], and some gates could be done by measuring joint parities. Microsoft has reported devices passing its "topological gap protocol" and single-shot parity measurements, but whether these devices really realise topological protection is disputed (@lf:sec-mod-topo). If they are validated, their manufacturing problems would resemble those of spin qubits: gate-defined nanostructures in heterostructures, where disorder is the main enemy.

Comparison

How to read it. Speed and accuracy combine: a platform with slower gates but longer coherence can have a similar ratio of coherence time to gate time. No platform dominates on every axis. For design automation the key row is the second last: where the qubits themselves vary in manufacture (superconducting, spin), Hamiltonian-level variability tools are central; where only the control hardware varies (ions, atoms), the problem moves to electrode, optical and photonic design.

The review applies one lens to every platform: what maps layout to Hamiltonian, what varies in manufacture, and what is missing from the tool chain (@lf:sec-mod). This chapter supplies the physics behind each answer; the review supplies the industrial context and the tool gaps.

  • Trapped ions: identical atomic qubits in RF traps on electrode chips; gates through shared motion; very accurate but slow; the trap is what is manufactured.
  • Neutral atoms: identical atoms in optical tweezers; Rydberg-blockade gates; reconfigurable connectivity; optics is what is engineered.
  • Spin qubits: electrons in gate-defined silicon dots; exchange gates; CMOS-compatible but every dot varies and needs tuning.
  • Topological qubits promise built-in protection but are not yet validated.
  • Where qubits vary (superconducting, spins), Hamiltonian-level variability tools matter most.

Exercises

  1. Why can an ion not be trapped by static electric fields alone?
    Solution

    In free space the electrostatic potential obeys Laplace's equation \(\nabla^2\phi = 0\): the second derivatives in the three directions sum to zero, so if the potential curves upward in one direction it must curve downward in another. There is always an escape direction. Oscillating fields evade this because the time-averaged pseudopotential is not a solution of Laplace's equation.

  2. An electron spin qubit has \(g = 2\). What field gives a 20 GHz qubit, and what is the thermal excited population at 100 mK?
    Solution

    \(B = hf/(g\mu_B) = 1.325\times10^{-23}/(1.855\times10^{-23})\approx0.71\) T. \(hf/k_BT = 20\times0.048/0.1 = 9.6\), population \(\approx\ee^{-9.6}\approx7\times10^{-5}\). Spin qubits can run warmer than transmons.

  3. A neutral-atom array loads each site with probability 0.55. Roughly how many sites must be loaded to have 99% probability of at least 200 atoms?
    Solution

    Binomial with mean \(0.55N\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.2475N}\). Need \(0.55N - 2.33\sqrt{0.2475N}\ge200\). At \(N = 405\): mean 222.8, sd 10.0, \(222.8 - 23.3 = 199.5\). So about 406 sites.

  4. An ion gate takes 100 µs and the coherence time is 10 s. A superconducting gate takes 50 ns with coherence 200 µs. Compare the number of gates each can perform within one coherence time.
    Solution

    Ion: \(10/10^{-4} = 10^5\). Superconducting: \(2\times10^{-4}/5\times10^{-8} = 4\times10^3\). The ion does more gates per coherence time, but each takes 2,000 times longer in wall-clock time, which matters for algorithms with billions of gates.

  5. In a spin qubit, \(J\propto t^2\) and \(t\propto\ee^{-V/V_0}\) with \(V_0 = 10\) mV. Two nominally identical pairs of dots differ in effective barrier voltage by 2 mV. By what factor do their exchange couplings differ?
    Solution

    \(J\propto\ee^{-2V/V_0}\): a factor \(\ee^{-0.4}\approx0.67\), a 33% difference that must be calibrated out for each pair.
