Gates on superconducting qubits

Goals

  • Understand how a microwave pulse rotates a transmon, why short pulses leak into the third level, and how the DRAG correction fixes it.
  • Understand why two coupled transmons have an unwanted always-on \(ZZ\) interaction, and compute it.
  • Understand the main two-qubit gates: tunable-coupler CZ, cross-resonance and parametric gates.
  • See how each gate scheme turns into frequency rules that the fabricated chip must satisfy: the origin of "frequency collisions".

Book 4 described gates as ideal matrices. On a superconducting chip, a single-qubit gate is a 20-nanosecond burst of microwaves on a control line, and a two-qubit gate is a carefully shaped pulse that briefly makes two qubits interact. Both are imperfect in ways that come straight from the physics of the transmon: its third energy level is close, and coupled qubits never fully stop interacting. Understanding these imperfections explains why chip designers obsess over the frequencies of qubits: each gate scheme works only if the qubits' frequencies obey certain rules, and fabrication spread (Chapter 2) can break those rules.

Single-qubit gates

A transmon is driven through a drive line: a transmission line ending near the qubit with a tiny coupling capacitance. A microwave voltage on it adds a term proportional to the qubit's charge. In the qubit's two-level subspace this is the driven qubit of Book 3 (@b3:eq-q-drive): in a frame rotating at the drive frequency, a resonant pulse rotates the Bloch vector about an axis in the \(xy\) plane at the Rabi frequency \(\Omega_R(t)\), proportional to the pulse amplitude. The rotation angle is the pulse area \(\int\Omega_R\dd t\), and the axis is set by the pulse's phase (@b3:sec-q-spin-gates). A typical \(\pi/2\) pulse lasts 20–30 ns with a smooth (Gaussian or cosine) envelope; smooth edges keep the pulse's spectrum narrow (@b1:sec-m-fourier-transform).

The third level. A transmon is not a two-level system. Its \(1\to2\) transition is only \(|\alpha|\approx200\)–300 MHz below the \(0\to1\) transition (Chapter 3), and the drive's matrix element for \(1\to2\) is \(\sqrt2\) times larger (the oscillator ladder, @b3:eq-q-fock). A pulse of duration \(t_g\) has a spectral width of about \(1/t_g\). If \(t_g\) is short enough that the pulse's spectrum reaches \(\omega_{12}\), part of the population leaks into \(\ket2\), outside the qubit space. Even without real leakage, the nearby \(1\to2\) transition shifts the qubit's energy during the pulse (an AC Stark shift), causing a phase error.

DRAG. The "derivative removal by adiabatic gate" scheme [@motzoi2009] adds a second, out-of-phase component to the pulse, proportional to the time derivative of the main envelope:

$$ \Omega_y(t) = -\beta\,\frac{\dot\Omega_x(t)}{\alpha} . $$

Why a derivative? In the frequency domain, taking a derivative multiplies the spectrum by \(\ii\omega\), which is zero at the centre and grows linearly away from it. Adding the derivative in quadrature reshapes the pulse's spectrum so that it has a zero exactly at the frequency of the unwanted \(1\to2\) transition, while leaving the wanted \(0\to1\) transition untouched. The constant \(\beta\approx1\) is fine-tuned in calibration.

With DRAG and a small detuning correction, 20–30 ns single-qubit gates reach errors near \(10^{-4}\), close to the coherence limit (@b4:eq-i-coh). Rotations about \(z\) are "virtual": they are done exactly and instantly by shifting the phase of all later pulses. One more hazard is drive crosstalk: part of one qubit's drive reaches its neighbours. If a neighbour's frequency is close, it gets rotated too. That is one more reason neighbouring qubits are given well-separated frequencies.

Coupled transmons and the \(ZZ\) problem

To make two-qubit gates, neighbouring qubits must be coupled, usually by a capacitor between their pads. The coupling is an exchange interaction \(g(a_1^\dagger a_2 + a_1a_2^\dagger)\) (@b2:sec-c-osc-coupled; quantum version @b3:sec-q-pert-deg). If the qubits are detuned (\(|\Delta|\gg g\)), they do not swap energy, but the coupling still shifts their levels in second-order perturbation theory (@b3:sec-q-pert-nd).

Here is the problem. Look at the four two-qubit levels \(\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11}\). If the qubits were independent, the energy of \(\ket{11}\) would equal the energy of \(\ket{01}\) plus that of \(\ket{10}\) (minus that of \(\ket{00}\)). But \(\ket{11}\) is coupled to \(\ket{20}\) and \(\ket{02}\) (one qubit doubly excited), which are nearby because of the small anharmonicity, and they push \(\ket{11}\) by an amount that \(\ket{01}\) and \(\ket{10}\) do not feel. The mismatch is the static \(ZZ\) interaction (@lf:eq-zz):

$$ \zeta = \omega_{11}-\omega_{10}-\omega_{01} = \frac{2g^2(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)}{(\Delta+\alpha_1)(\alpha_2-\Delta)} , \qquad \Delta = \omega_1-\omega_2 . $$

(Frequencies measured from \(\ket{00}\).) What it means: each qubit's frequency depends on whether its neighbour is in \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), by \(\zeta\). During idling and during other gates, this produces phase errors correlated between neighbours.

Worked example: how big is \(ZZ\)? \(g/2\pi = 4\) MHz, \(\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = 2\pi\times(-300)\) MHz, \(\Delta/2\pi = 100\) MHz. Numerator: \(2\times16\times(-600) = -19{,}200\). Denominator: \((100-300)(-300-100) = 80{,}000\). So \(\zeta/2\pi = -0.24\) MHz. Over a 1 µs error-correction cycle, the conditional phase is \(2\pi\times0.24\approx1.5\) radians: a huge error. Fixed-coupling architectures choose detunings where \(\zeta\) is smaller, but the real solution is a coupler that can switch the interaction off.

Tunable couplers and the CZ gate

A tunable coupler is a third circuit element, usually a flux-tunable transmon (with a SQUID, Chapter 2), placed between two qubits [@yan2018]. Each qubit couples to the coupler with strength \(g_1, g_2\), and to each other directly with a small \(g_{12}\). The coupler is detuned from both qubits, so it is never excited; instead it mediates a virtual exchange (second-order perturbation theory again):

\[ \tilde g\approx g_{12} + \frac{g_1g_2}{2}\Big(\frac1{\Delta_1}+\frac1{\Delta_2}\Big), \]

where \(\Delta_i\) are the qubit–coupler detunings. The virtual term can have the opposite sign to \(g_{12}\). At a particular coupler frequency they cancel: the qubits are effectively decoupled, and \(\zeta\) drops to a few kHz or less. Moving the coupler's frequency (by a flux pulse) turns the interaction on for a gate.

The CZ gate. A popular gate uses the avoided crossing (@b1:sec-m-op-eigen) between \(\ket{11}\) and \(\ket{20}\). Bring them close in energy by pulsing the coupler (or a qubit's frequency). The population of \(\ket{11}\) oscillates into \(\ket{20}\) and back, like the energy swap between coupled pendulums. Choose the pulse so that exactly one full cycle happens: \(\ket{11}\to\ket{20}\to\ket{11}\). A complete cycle of a two-level oscillation returns the state with a sign flip (a \(2\pi\) rotation of a spin-½ gives \(-1\)). The other three states are unaffected (no partner nearby). The result is \(\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,-1)\) up to single-qubit phases: a CZ (@b4:eq-i-cnot). Google's Sycamore and Willow processors use gates of this kind, 20–40 ns long, with errors around \(10^{-3}\). The main hazard is leakage into \(\ket{20}\) if the cycle is not exactly complete; shaping the flux pulse smoothly ("adiabatically") controls it. The same mechanism with the \(\ket{01}\leftrightarrow\ket{10}\) crossing gives iSWAP-type gates.

Cross-resonance

Some architectures (IBM's earlier processors, for example) use fixed-frequency transmons with fixed capacitive coupling. That avoids flux noise and saves control lines. The cross-resonance gate [@rigetti2010; @sheldon2016] drives the control qubit at the target qubit's frequency.

Through the static coupling, the control qubit's state slightly mixes with the target's, and the strength and sign of that mixing depend on whether the control is in \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\). A drive applied to the control at the target's frequency therefore reaches the target with an amplitude that depends on the control's state. The target rotates one way if the control is \(\ket0\) and the other way if it is \(\ket1\): an effective \(Z\otimes X\) interaction.

A \(ZX\) rotation by \(\pi/2\), plus single-qubit gates, is equivalent to a CNOT. The cross-resonance rate is proportional to the coupling times the drive strength, divided by the detuning, with a factor that involves the anharmonicity. It is fast only when the two qubits are detuned by less than about one anharmonicity (the "straddling regime"). That requirement conflicts with avoiding various unwanted transitions, which brings us to frequency collisions.

From gate scheme to frequency rules

Every gate scheme only works if the qubits' frequencies obey certain rules. For cross-resonance, for example, the detuning between neighbours must be inside the straddling window, and must avoid a list of forbidden values: near zero (the qubits would swap), near \(\pm\alpha/2\) and \(\pm\alpha\) (unwanted two-photon and \(1\to2\) transitions), and certain combinations involving a third "spectator" qubit. Landing in a forbidden window is called a frequency collision (@lf:sec-var-collisions).

Worked example: why fabrication spread breaks gates. A design places neighbouring fixed-frequency qubits 100 MHz apart, and one forbidden window is \(|\Delta| \lt 17\) MHz. If each qubit's fabricated frequency has a 1% spread (\(\sigma = 50\) MHz at 5 GHz), the detuning between two neighbours has spread \(\sqrt2\times50\approx71\) MHz. The chance that it lands inside \((-17, 17)\) MHz is roughly \(\Phi(-83/71) - \Phi(-117/71)\approx0.12 - 0.05 = 0.07\): about one coupled pair in fourteen collides. A chip with 100 couplings would almost certainly have several. This is why the review treats junction spread and frequency allocation as the central yield problem, and why tunable couplers (which widen the windows) and laser trimming (which narrows the spread) matter so much.

The review's frequency-allocation problem (Problem 1) is stated in terms of these gate schemes: choose target frequencies and couplings so that, under the fabrication distribution, the probability that no pair violates its collision windows is maximised. The windows come from this chapter's physics; the distribution comes from Chapter 2's junction statistics.

  • Single-qubit gates are shaped microwave pulses; the nearby \(1\to2\) transition causes leakage, cancelled by the DRAG derivative correction.
  • Coupled transmons have an always-on \(ZZ\) shift from the \(\ket{20}, \ket{02}\) levels; it can be large and causes correlated errors.
  • Tunable couplers cancel the direct coupling with a virtual one, switching the interaction off; CZ gates use a full cycle through the \(\ket{11}\)–\(\ket{20}\) avoided crossing.
  • Cross-resonance drives the control at the target's frequency to get a \(ZX\) interaction; it needs detunings inside the straddling window.
  • Each scheme defines forbidden frequency windows; fabrication spread makes collisions likely, the root of the yield problem.

Exercises

  1. Evaluate @eq:h-zz for \(g/2\pi = 4\) MHz, \(\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = 2\pi\times(-300)\) MHz and \(\Delta/2\pi = 500\) MHz. Compare with the worked example.
    Solution

    Numerator \(-19{,}200\); denominator \((500-300)(-300-500) = 200\times(-800) = -160{,}000\). \(\zeta/2\pi = +0.12\) MHz. Larger detuning halved \(\zeta\) here (and flipped its sign), but it also slows cross-resonance gates: the trade-off behind frequency planning.

  2. For a Gaussian pulse of width \(\sigma = 4\) ns and \(\alpha/2\pi = -250\) MHz, how many spectral standard deviations away is the \(1\to2\) transition? Why does DRAG still help?
    Solution

    Spectral width \(1/\sigma = 2.5\times10^8\) rad/s ≈ 40 MHz. 250 MHz is about 6σ away, a tiny Gaussian tail. But real pulses are truncated, which adds side lobes, and the \(\sqrt2\)-enhanced \(1\to2\) coupling causes AC Stark phase errors even without leakage. DRAG cancels the \(1\to2\) transition amplitude to first order regardless.

  3. A coupler gives \(g_1 = g_2 = 2\pi\times100\) MHz and qubit–coupler detunings \(\Delta_1 = \Delta_2 = -2\pi\times1\) GHz. What direct coupling cancels the interaction?
    Solution

    Virtual term \(\tfrac{g_1g_2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{2}{\Delta} = g^2/\Delta = 10^4/(-1000) = -10\) MHz (×2π). A direct coupling of \(+10\) MHz cancels it.

  4. In the collision worked example, what frequency spread \(\sigma\) would make the collision probability per pair below 0.1%?
    Solution

    The dominant term is \(\Phi((17-100)/(\sqrt2\sigma))\). Setting it to \(10^{-3}\) requires \(83/(\sqrt2\sigma) = 3.09\), so \(\sigma\approx19\) MHz, about 0.4% at 5 GHz. That is roughly what laser trimming achieves.

  5. Why does a full cycle \(\ket{11}\to\ket{20}\to\ket{11}\) give a minus sign?
    Solution

    Within the two-level subspace \(\{\ket{11},\ket{20}\}\), resonant coupling generates a rotation; one complete population cycle is a rotation by \(2\pi\) of that effective spin-½, and \(\ee^{-\ii2\pi\sigma_x/2} = \cos\pi\,I = -I\). The state returns with a sign of \(-1\), which is the CZ phase.
