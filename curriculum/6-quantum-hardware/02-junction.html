The Josephson junction

Goals

  • Derive the two Josephson relations and the junction's energy \(-E_J\cos\varphi\).
  • Treat the junction as a nonlinear inductor, and use the RCSJ model.
  • Relate \(E_J\) to the room-temperature resistance (Ambegaokar–Baratoff), and understand its exponential sensitivity to the barrier.
  • Use SQUIDs as tunable junctions, and know how junctions are fabricated.

The Josephson relations

Two superconductors separated by a barrier thin enough (1–2 nm of aluminium oxide) for Cooper pairs to tunnel form a Josephson junction [@josephson1962]. Each side has its own condensate phase, and the gauge-invariant phase difference \(\varphi\) across the barrier is the junction's single dynamical variable. Josephson predicted:

$$ I = I_c\sin\varphi, \qquad V = \frac{\hbar}{2e}\,\frac{\dd\varphi}{\dd t} = \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi}\,\dot\varphi . $$

A supercurrent up to the critical current \(I_c\) flows with zero voltage (the DC effect), and a constant voltage makes the phase wind and the current oscillate at \(2eV/h\approx483.6\) GHz per millivolt (the AC effect, now the basis of the volt standard). A simple derivation models the junction as a two-level tunnelling problem for the number of pairs transferred: coupling \(-\tfrac{E_J}{2}\sum_N(\ket{N}\bra{N+1} + \mathrm{h.c.})\) between states that differ by one tunnelled pair is diagonalised by phase states \(\ket\varphi = \sum_N\ee^{\ii N\varphi}\ket N\), giving energy \(-E_J\cos\varphi\); the current is \(2e\,\dot N = (2e/\hbar)\,\partial E/\partial\varphi\).

The second relation says the phase is the junction's flux in units of \(\Phi_0/2\pi\): \(\varphi = 2\pi\Phi/\Phi_0\) with \(\Phi = \int V\dd t\), the node flux of @b2:eq-c-flux. Integrating the power \(IV\) gives the stored energy

$$ U(\varphi) = -E_J\cos\varphi, \qquad E_J = \frac{\hbar I_c}{2e} = \frac{\Phi_0I_c}{2\pi} . $$

This is the potential energy of a pendulum in the junction's flux coordinate.

A nonlinear inductor

For small phase, \(I\approx I_c\varphi = (2\pi I_c/\Phi_0)\Phi\), which is an inductor with

$$ L_J = \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi I_c} = \Big(\frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi}\Big)^2\frac{1}{E_J}, \qquad L_J(\varphi) = \frac{L_J}{\cos\varphi} . $$

A junction with \(I_c = 30\) nA has \(L_J\approx11\) nH and \(E_J/h\approx15\) GHz. The inductance grows with current, so the junction is a nonlinear, dissipationless inductor: the only one available in electronics. Nonlinearity without loss is exactly what a qubit needs (@b3:sec-q-qho-anharm), and it is also what makes parametric amplifiers and frequency converters possible. A real junction also has a capacitance \(C_J\) (a parallel-plate capacitor across the thin oxide, about 50 fF per µm²) and, above the gap voltage, a resistance. The RCSJ (resistively and capacitively shunted junction) model puts these in parallel with the ideal element; its classical dynamics are a damped, driven pendulum, which explains the hysteretic current–voltage curves used to characterise junctions.

From room-temperature resistance to \(E_J\)

The critical current is tied to the normal-state tunnel resistance \(R_n\) by the Ambegaokar–Baratoff relation (@lf:eq-ab),

$$ I_cR_n = \frac{\pi\Delta}{2e}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_J = \frac{h\Delta}{8e^2R_n} = \frac{R_Q}{2R_n}\,\Delta , $$

where \(R_Q = h/(2e)^2\approx6.45\) kΩ is the resistance quantum for Cooper pairs. Since \(R_n\) can be measured at room temperature with a probe station in milliseconds, it is the main manufacturing metric for junctions: every junction on a wafer can be measured warm and its \(E_J\), and hence its qubit's frequency, predicted. The prediction is imperfect: the resistance changes on cooling and ageing, and the gap varies with film thickness, which is why the review proposes a statistical "warm-to-cold" model (@lf:sec-var-bayes). For aluminium, \(R_n\approx10\) kΩ gives \(E_J/h\approx14\) GHz.

The tunnel resistance depends exponentially on the barrier thickness (@b3:eq-q-tunnel) and inversely on area: \(R_n\propto\ee^{2\kappa d}/A\). With \(\kappa\approx5\) nm\(^{-1}\), a 0.01 nm change in average barrier thickness changes \(R_n\) by 10%. Junction areas are typically \(0.01\)–\(0.05\) µm², so a 5 nm error in a 150 nm linewidth changes the area by several percent. Both effects make junction-to-junction spreads of 1–3% hard to beat, and this, through @lf:eq-sens, is the root of frequency variability in superconducting processors.

SQUIDs: tunable junctions

Two junctions in parallel in a small loop form a DC SQUID. Flux quantisation (@eq:h-fluxq) constrains the two phases, \(\varphi_1 - \varphi_2 = 2\pi\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}/\Phi_0\), and for identical junctions the pair acts as one junction with

$$ E_J(\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}) = 2E_{J0}\,\Big|\cos\frac{\pi\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\Phi_0}\Big| , $$

tunable from \(2E_{J0}\) to zero by an external flux (with asymmetric junctions, to a nonzero minimum). Flux-tunable transmons and tunable couplers use SQUIDs, with a current line nearby supplying the flux. Tunability helps correct fabrication spread and enables fast two-qubit gates, but it exposes the qubit to flux noise (\(\partial\omega/\partial\Phi\ne0\) away from the "sweet spot" where the derivative vanishes) and requires a control line per tunable element.

How junctions are made

The standard process is double-angle shadow evaporation of aluminium through a suspended resist mask, either a bridge (the Dolan technique [@dolan1977]) or crossed trenches (the "Manhattan" style). Aluminium is evaporated from one angle, the surface is oxidised in a controlled oxygen atmosphere (pressure × time sets the barrier thickness), and a second aluminium layer is evaporated from another angle so that it overlaps the first only at the junction. The process is simple and gives excellent coherence, but it is sensitive to resist profiles, evaporation angles and oxidation conditions, and it is not native to CMOS-style fabs. Industrial lines (imec, MIT Lincoln Laboratory, foundries such as SkyWater and Rigetti's fab) have developed subtractive, overlap or 300 mm-compatible variants (@lf:sec-land-foundry). Post-fabrication laser annealing of junctions changes \(R_n\) by a few percent in a controlled way and is used to trim frequencies after measurement (@lf:sec-var-trim).

The junction is where process and design meet most sharply. A design specifies \(E_J\); the process delivers a distribution of \(R_n\) with a wafer-scale gradient and a local random part; trimming narrows the distribution at a cost in test time. The yield problem of the review's §4 is the propagation of this distribution through the frequency formula to collision constraints.

Exercises

  1. Compute \(L_J\) and \(E_J/h\) for \(I_c = 20\) nA.
    Solution

    \(L_J = 2.068\times10^{-15}/(2\pi\times2\times10^{-8}) = 16.5\) nH. \(E_J = \Phi_0I_c/2\pi = 6.58\times10^{-24}\) J, \(E_J/h\approx9.9\) GHz.

  2. An aluminium junction has \(R_n = 8\) kΩ. Using \(E_J = (R_Q/2R_n)\Delta\) with \(\Delta = 180\) µeV and \(R_Q = 6.45\) kΩ, find \(E_J/h\).
    Solution

    \(E_J = (6.45/16)\times180\) µeV \(= 72.6\) µeV; \(E_J/h = 72.6\times10^{-6}\times2.418\times10^{14}\approx17.6\) GHz.

  3. A symmetric SQUID has \(E_{J0}/h = 10\) GHz per junction. At what external flux is \(E_J\) half its maximum? What is \(\partial E_J/\partial\Phi\) there, in GHz per \(\Phi_0\)?
    Solution

    \(|\cos(\pi\Phi/\Phi_0)| = 1/2\) at \(\Phi = \Phi_0/3\). \(\partial E_J/\partial\Phi = -2E_{J0}(\pi/\Phi_0)\sin(\pi/3) = -2\times10\times\pi\times0.866\approx-54\) GHz per \(\Phi_0\). Flux noise of \(10^{-6}\Phi_0\) then moves \(E_J\) by 54 kHz, and the qubit frequency by roughly half that fraction.
