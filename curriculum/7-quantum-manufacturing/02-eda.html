Classical electronic design automation in one chapter

Goals

  • Understand what problem electronic design automation (EDA) solves, and the "fabless" business model it made possible.
  • Follow the classical chip design flow from specification to tape-out, knowing what each tool does.
  • Understand process design kits, parametrised cells, design-rule checks, layout-versus-schematic and parasitic extraction.
  • Understand compact models, corners, Monte Carlo and Pelgrom's mismatch law.
  • See which pieces carry over to quantum chips and which are missing.

A modern processor has tens of billions of transistors. No human draws them. They are placed, wired and checked by software, electronic design automation, written by companies such as Synopsys and Cadence. EDA's most important achievement is not speed but predictability: a design team that never visits a factory can send a file to a foundry and, with high confidence, receive working chips. That works because the foundry publishes a precise description of what it can make and how its devices behave, including their variation, and the design software checks every design against it. Quantum chips have no such contract yet. This chapter explains how the classical one works, so that the review's proposal to build a quantum equivalent makes sense.

Why EDA exists: the fabless model

Until the 1980s, chip companies designed and manufactured their own chips. Then the industry split: foundries (such as TSMC) manufacture for anyone, and fabless companies (such as Nvidia, Apple's chip group, Qualcomm) only design. This split works only if a design made far from the factory will work when manufactured. The bridge is a software contract: the foundry provides models and rules; the designer's EDA tools guarantee the design follows the rules and meets its performance targets under the modelled variation; and the foundry promises that designs that pass will yield. That contract is what quantum hardware currently lacks (@lf:sec-intro).

The design flow

The classical flow has seven stages [@lavagno2016; @weste2010]. Think of each as a transformation of a description of the chip into a more detailed one, with a check at the end.

  1. Specification and architecture. What must the chip do, how fast, at what power and cost? Block diagrams, performance models.
  2. Design entry. Analogue circuits are drawn as schematics (transistors, resistors, capacitors and their connections). Digital logic is written in a hardware description language (Verilog, VHDL) and compiled by logic synthesis into a network of standard cells: small pre-designed gates (NAND, flip-flop) from a library.
  3. Simulation. Analogue circuits are simulated with SPICE using compact device models (below); digital logic with timing and power models of each standard cell.
  4. Physical design. Turn the netlist into geometry: floorplanning (where big blocks go), placement (where each of millions of cells goes) and routing (how wires connect them across a dozen metal layers). These are huge combinatorial optimisations solved by graph partitioning, analytical placement and maze-routing algorithms [@kahng2011].
  5. Physical verification. DRC (design-rule checking): does every shape obey the foundry's geometric rules (minimum widths, spacings, overlaps, densities)? LVS (layout versus schematic): does the drawn geometry actually implement the intended circuit, with no shorts or opens?
  6. Parasitic extraction. Compute the unintended resistances, capacitances and inductances of the real wires and re-simulate with them included. A wire that was "ideal" in the schematic now slows a signal or couples noise into its neighbour.
  7. Sign-off and tape-out. Final checks of timing, power, noise, reliability and yield across the process corners and statistical variation, then the layout is written as a GDSII or OASIS file (a list of polygons per layer) and sent to the foundry.

Think of the flow as a funnel of increasingly expensive questions. Early checks are fast and approximate (a logic simulation); late checks are slow and exact (field-solver extraction of a critical net). Fast estimators guide the design; only the slow accurate checks are allowed to approve it. The review applies exactly this principle to machine-learning surrogates for quantum design: they may propose, but must not approve, unless their error is bounded (@lf:sec-design-inverse).

The process design kit

The process design kit (PDK) is the foundry's half of the contract. It contains:

  • the layer stack: which materials, how thick, with what electrical properties;
  • design rules: hundreds to thousands of geometric constraints, from "metal-1 lines at least 50 nm wide" to "no more than 70% metal density in any 100 µm window";
  • compact models of every device the process offers, fitted to measured silicon and including statistical variation;
  • parametrised cells (p-cells): small programs that generate a correct layout for a device from a few parameters (a transistor from its width and length, an inductor from its turns);
  • rule decks for extraction, DRC and LVS, read by the EDA tools.

A design that passes the PDK's checks and simulates correctly with its models will, with high probability, work when fabricated. That probability is what the foundry sells. Quantum chips have no equivalent yet: no compact models of qubits fitted to process data, no rule decks for coherence-relevant constraints, and no statistical models of junction variation shipped with the process. The review's §10 maps which pieces exist (@lf:sec-tools-gap).

Compact models, corners and Monte Carlo

A transistor's behaviour can be computed from first principles by solving semiconductor equations on a mesh, but that takes minutes per device. A compact model replaces this with a set of equations with fitted parameters that predicts the device's terminal currents and charges in microseconds, fast enough to simulate millions of devices. SPICE transistor models (the BSIM family) have hundreds of parameters, each extracted by fitting measurements of test structures [@nagel1975]. A quantum analogue would be a model that predicts a qubit's Hamiltonian parameters from its drawn geometry and the process state, fitted to measured devices.

Variation is represented in two complementary ways:

  • Corners: a few worst-case combinations of global process shifts, named for transistor speed ("fast-fast", "slow-slow", "fast-slow"...). A design must work at all corners. Corners are cheap (a handful of simulations) but can be over- or under-pessimistic.
  • Monte Carlo: sample the statistical model of global and local variation thousands of times and simulate each sample (@b1:sec-m-prob-mc). This gives a distribution of performance and an estimated yield.

Local, device-to-device mismatch follows Pelgrom's law [@pelgrom1989]:

$$ \sigma(\Delta P) = \frac{A_P}{\sqrt{WL}} , $$

the standard deviation of the difference of a parameter \(P\) between two nominally identical neighbouring devices falls with the square root of their area \(WL\).

Why the square root? A device's parameter is an average over many microscopic random fluctuations (atoms, grains, roughness) spread over its area. By the law of large numbers, the standard deviation of an average of \(N\) independent contributions falls as \(1/\sqrt N\), and \(N\) is proportional to area. Bigger devices average out more randomness.

Exactly this law applies to Josephson junctions: larger junctions are more reproducible, at the cost of more capacitance and less design freedom. Analogue designers routinely size devices by Pelgrom's law to meet matching requirements; quantum designers can do the same.

Worked example: sizing for matching. Suppose junction critical currents follow Pelgrom's law with \(A_P = 2\%\cdot\mu\mathrm m\). A small transmon junction of \(0.02\,\mu\mathrm m^2\) then has a relative mismatch of \(2\%/\sqrt{0.02} = 14\%\) (real processes do better, but the scaling holds), while a fluxonium array junction of \(1\,\mu\mathrm m^2\) has 2%, and an array of 100 such junctions in series averages further to 0.2%. That is why fluxonium's superinductor is reproducible while its single small junction is not.

What transfers to quantum design

Quantum design is not just classical design with different physics. The sign-off target is different in kind. Classical sign-off asks "does every signal arrive in time, with margin?", a deterministic check with a safety factor. Quantum sign-off must ask "does every qubit's Hamiltonian land inside windows of order \(10^{-3}\) relative, and does the processor's error model give the required logical error rate?", which is a probability statement over a high-dimensional manufactured distribution. That is why the review frames quantum EDA as a statistics problem as much as a simulation problem.

Read the review's §10 alongside this chapter: it surveys incumbent vendors (Synopsys/Ansys, Cadence, Keysight), equipment makers and quantum-native tools, and places each on a gap map. Knowing the classical flow lets you judge which gaps are engineering (porting an existing method) and which are research (no method exists yet).

  • EDA makes the fabless model possible: designs made far from the factory work because foundry models and rules are checked by software.
  • Flow: specification → design entry → simulation → physical design → DRC/LVS → extraction → sign-off and tape-out.
  • A PDK contains layer stack, design rules, compact models with variation, p-cells and rule decks; quantum PDKs mostly do not exist yet.
  • Variation is handled by corners and Monte Carlo; mismatch falls as \(1/\sqrt{\text{area}}\) (Pelgrom), which applies to junctions too.
  • Quantum sign-off is a probability statement about Hamiltonians and logical errors, not a deterministic margin check.

Exercises

  1. Junction critical currents follow Pelgrom's law with \(A_P = 2\%\cdot\mu\mathrm m\). What is the expected relative mismatch for junctions of \(0.02\) µm² and \(0.2\) µm²?
    Solution

    \(2\%/\sqrt{0.02} = 14\%\) and \(2\%/\sqrt{0.2} = 4.5\%\).

  2. List three rules you would add to a DRC deck for a superconducting qubit process that a classical deck would not contain.
    Solution

    Examples: minimum distance from a junction to any lossy dielectric such as a crossover's oxide; maximum interface participation for capacitor geometries (a physics check); airbridge spacing along CPW lines; maximum ground-plane area without flux-trapping holes; keep-out zones around qubits for unrelated lines; symmetry of SQUID loops.

  3. A design must work at 5 corners and in a 1,000-sample Monte Carlo. If each simulation takes 10 minutes, how long does verification take, and how might a surrogate model help?
    Solution

    1,005 simulations × 10 min ≈ 7 days. A fast surrogate (Chapter 6) trained on a few hundred simulations could evaluate the Monte Carlo samples in seconds; the accurate simulator is then run only on the worst cases the surrogate finds, to confirm.

  4. Explain in your own words what "layout versus Hamiltonian" would check, by analogy with LVS.
    Solution

    LVS checks that the drawn geometry implements the intended circuit. LVH would extract the Hamiltonian (frequencies, anharmonicities, couplings, dispersive shifts) from the drawn layout and check it against the designer's intended Hamiltonian within tolerances, flagging, for example, an unintended coupling between two qubits or a resonator that collides with a qubit.

  5. A classical chip team uses three corners (slow, typical, fast) for transistor speed. Propose an analogous set of "corners" for a transmon processor, naming the parameters you would vary and why a few corners might be misleading here.
    Solution

    Corners could combine junction resistance (low/nominal/high), pad capacitance (low/high, from linewidth and permittivity) and kinetic inductance (low/high). They are misleading for collision yield because failures come from local mismatch between neighbouring qubits, not global shifts: a global corner moves all qubits together and preserves their detunings. Statistical Monte Carlo of local variation is essential.
