Packaging, cryogenics and test

Goals

  • Understand what a package must do for a quantum chip, and the electromagnetic problems it creates (box modes, ground continuity, crosstalk, shielding).
  • Understand how a dilution refrigerator is organised, and budget heat and noise for its wiring.
  • Follow the test flow from room-temperature wafer probing to millikelvin characterisation, and understand why cold test is the bottleneck.
  • See how prediction and experimental design make scarce cold test more valuable.

A finished quantum chip is a few millimetres of silicon. To use it, it must be mounted in a package that connects dozens to hundreds of signal lines to it without spoiling its delicate microwave environment, cooled to about 10 millikelvin inside a refrigerator the size of a wardrobe, and then tested to find out what was actually made. Each of these steps limits scaling: the package can host unwanted microwave modes, every wire into the refrigerator carries heat and noise, and cold testing takes days per chip. This chapter explains each problem and connects it to the design questions of the review's §6–§7.

The package

A typical superconducting-chip package is a machined copper or aluminium enclosure. The chip sits in a pocket, surrounded by a printed circuit board that fans signal lines out to coaxial connectors; wirebonds (thin aluminium wires) or spring pins connect board to chip [@krinner2019]. The package must:

  • Thermalise the chip to the coldest stage of the refrigerator (heat must flow out through the metal and bonds).
  • Connect every control and readout line with low loss and low reflection (@b2:sec-c-tl-reflect).
  • Shield the chip from magnetic fields (mu-metal and superconducting shields), infrared radiation from warmer stages (light-tight enclosures, absorbing coatings), and increasingly ionising radiation.

And it must not introduce new problems:

  • No modes near qubit frequencies. The enclosure, and the chip's own substrate, form a cavity whose modes scale inversely with size (@b2:sec-c-max-waves). For a rectangular cavity filled with silicon, the lowest mode is roughly \(f\approx\frac{c}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\sqrt{(1/a)^2+(1/b)^2}\) (@lf:eq-box). Large dies push it into the 4–8 GHz qubit band, where it couples to qubits and drains them.
  • Continuous ground. The ground planes on chip and board must be tied together densely (many wirebonds, bump bonds, or through-silicon vias), otherwise separate ground regions support "slot" modes and radiate.
  • Low crosstalk between control lines in the board, connectors and bonds, so that driving one qubit does not drive its neighbour.

Worked example: when does a die get too big? For a square silicon die of side \(a\) in a tight cavity, \(f_{11}\approx\frac{c}{2\sqrt{11.45}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{a} = \frac{62.6\ \mathrm{GHz\,mm}}{a}\). At \(a = 5\) mm, 12.5 GHz: safely above the qubits. At 10 mm, 6.3 GHz: inside the band. At 20 mm, 3.1 GHz: several modes inside the band. Larger chips therefore need internal pillars or vias that split the cavity into smaller cells, or segmented packages (@lf:sec-pkg-modes).

Flip-chip and multi-chip modules move wiring off the qubit chip onto a second chip; superconducting through-silicon vias and bump bonds carry signals vertically. Each interface must be designed and simulated as part of the microwave circuit, not added afterwards.

The dilution refrigerator

A dilution refrigerator reaches 10–20 mK in stages [@pobell2007]. A pulse-tube cryocooler cools plates at about 50 K and 4 K. Below that, a mixture of helium-3 and helium-4 provides cooling: helium-3 "evaporates" from a concentrated phase into a dilute one in the mixing chamber, absorbing heat, much as evaporating sweat cools skin. Intermediate plates sit at the still (about 0.8 K) and the cold plate (about 100 mK). The chip is mounted on the mixing chamber plate.

The crucial engineering fact is that available cooling power falls steeply with temperature: about a watt at 4 K, tens of milliwatts at the still, about a milliwatt at 100 mK, and only tens of microwatts at the mixing chamber.

Heat and noise budgets for wiring

Every line from room temperature to the chip does two unwelcome things:

  • Carries heat by conduction down the cable. Stainless-steel and superconducting niobium-titanium coaxial cables conduct little heat; copper conducts a lot but has low electrical loss. Each cable is thermally anchored at every stage so most of its heat is dumped at warmer, more powerful stages.
  • Carries thermal noise from warmer stages (@b2:sec-c-th-cryo). A drive line must be heavily attenuated: about 20 dB at 4 K, 10 dB at the cold plate and 20–30 dB at the mixing chamber, so that the room-temperature noise of about 1,250 photons per mode at 5 GHz is reduced below about \(10^{-3}\) photons at the chip (@lf:eq-atten). Each attenuator absorbs signal power and turns it into heat at its stage.

Summing passive and active loads over all lines at each stage gives the review's constraint (@lf:eq-heat): the load at each stage must be below its cooling power. With two or three coaxial lines per qubit today, a large refrigerator supports on the order of a thousand qubits. Beyond that, multiplexing, cryogenic CMOS controllers, flexible printed cables and optical links are needed (@b4:sec-i-stack-elec, @lf:sec-pkg-wiring).

Worked example: a mixing-chamber budget. Suppose 20 µW of cooling at the mixing chamber. Each drive line conducts 2 nW of passive heat and dissipates 0.5 nW in its attenuator during operation; each flux line carries a DC current that dissipates 5 nW in resistive parts. For 1,000 qubits with one drive line and one flux line each: \(1000\times(2.5 + 2 + 5)\) nW ≈ 9.5 µW, within budget, but other stages (the 4 K plate, where amplifiers dissipate milliwatts, and the still) often bind first. Real designs keep large margins, because heat loads also raise the base temperature and with it qubit errors.

The test flow

Testing happens at several stages, each cheaper and less informative than the next:

  1. Room-temperature wafer probing. Junction resistances, line continuity, leakage. Seconds per die, fully automated, done on every die. Predicts \(E_J\) through Ambegaokar–Baratoff (@b6:sec-h-jj-ab), with a warm-to-cold uncertainty.
  2. Cryogenic probing at 4 K or below. Emerging cryogenic wafer probers measure resonator frequencies, critical currents and some loss at a few kelvin, on many dies per cooldown.
  3. Millikelvin characterisation. In a dilution refrigerator: spectroscopy (qubit and resonator frequencies), \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) (@b3:sec-q-open), gate calibration and benchmarking (@b4:sec-i-noise). One cooldown takes one to several days and holds a handful of chips; fully characterising a large processor takes days to weeks (@b4:sec-i-stack-cal).

It is tempting to think of testing as "checking" a chip. For quantum chips it is also measuring it: you cannot know a qubit's frequency, \(T_1\) or crosstalk without cooling it, and those measurements are the ground truth that every design model must be checked against. Cold test is where predictions meet reality, and it is also the slowest, most expensive step in the whole flow.

Making scarce cold test count

Because cold test is the scarce resource (@lf:sec-test-scarce), two things become valuable:

  • Prediction: models that infer cold behaviour from warm data and design data, with calibrated uncertainty, so that only promising chips are cooled. The hierarchical models of Chapter 6 do this.
  • Experimental design: choosing which measurements to make on a cooled chip, and in what order, to learn the most per hour of refrigerator time (@lf:eq-boed). For example, a spectroscopy sweep can be narrowed to where the model predicts the qubit to be, and adaptively refined.

Worked example: which chips to cool? Warm probing predicts each qubit's cold frequency with 60 MHz uncertainty. A chip is useful if all 20 qubits land within ±100 MHz of target. For each chip, compute the probability that all 20 do, using the warm predictions and their uncertainties (including correlations from the wafer model): if the predictions are centred, each qubit passes with probability \(2\Phi(100/60) - 1 = 0.90\), so a chip passes with \(0.90^{20}\approx12\%\); chips whose predictions are off-centre score lower. Cool the highest-probability chips first, and update the warm-to-cold model with every measured chip so later predictions improve. This is the decision problem the review's hierarchical model is built to serve (@lf:eq-hier).

The review's §6–§7 cover these topics in depth. Packaging is where chip design meets mechanical and thermal design, so a quantum design flow must extend beyond the die boundary. Test is where predictions meet data, so the flow must ingest measurements as well as produce layouts.

  • A package thermalises, connects and shields the chip, and must avoid box modes, broken grounds and crosstalk; box modes fall into the qubit band for dies above ~10 mm.
  • Dilution refrigerators offer watts at 4 K but only tens of µW at 10–20 mK; every wire costs heat and needs ~60 dB of attenuation against noise.
  • Wiring limits today's systems to roughly a thousand qubits per refrigerator without new technology.
  • Test: warm probing on every die, cryogenic probing on many, millikelvin characterisation on few.
  • Cold test is the bottleneck; prediction and experimental design make it count.

Exercises

  1. A silicon chip of 12 × 12 mm in a tightly fitting box: estimate its lowest box mode.
    Solution

    \(62.6\,\mathrm{GHz\,mm}/12\,\mathrm{mm}\approx5.2\) GHz, inside the qubit band. A 12 mm die needs mode suppression.

  2. A refrigerator offers 20 µW at the mixing chamber. Each drive line dissipates 0.5 nW in its attenuator and conducts 2 nW of passive heat. How many such lines can that stage support?
    Solution

    2.5 nW per line: 8,000 lines in principle. Other stages and flux-line currents usually bind first.

  3. Apply the attenuator formula \(\bar n_{\mathrm{out}} = \bar n_{\mathrm{in}}/A + (1 - 1/A)\bar n(T)\) for 20 dB at 4 K, 10 dB at 100 mK and 30 dB at 20 mK to a 5 GHz line starting at 300 K. What reaches the chip?
    Solution

    About \(2.9\times10^{-3}\) photons (worked in @b2:sec-c-thermal, exercise 4): close to, but above, \(10^{-3}\); more mixing-chamber attenuation or better thermalised attenuators are needed.

  4. In the "which chips to cool" example, what per-qubit uncertainty would raise the probability that a centred 20-qubit chip passes to 90%?
    Solution

    Need per-qubit pass probability \(0.9^{1/20} = 0.9947\), so \(2\Phi(100/\sigma) - 1 = 0.9947\), \(\Phi = 0.99737\), \(100/\sigma = 2.79\), \(\sigma\approx36\) MHz. Better warm-to-cold prediction (or trimming) directly raises the useful fraction of cooled chips.

  5. Why is a qubit chip with 1,000 qubits not simply "ten 100-qubit chips side by side" from a packaging point of view? Give two reasons.
    Solution

    (1) The die is larger, so its box modes fall into the qubit band and must be suppressed with vias, pillars or segmentation. (2) Many more lines must reach the chip edge or be brought in vertically, which needs flip-chip or multilayer wiring and raises crosstalk and heat-load challenges. (Splitting into separate chips instead needs low-loss chip-to-chip links, which is the modular alternative.)
