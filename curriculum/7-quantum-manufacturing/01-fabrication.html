How quantum chips are made

Goals

  • Know the basic steps of microfabrication (deposition, lithography, etching, lift-off, metrology) and what each can and cannot do.
  • Follow a superconducting-qubit chip from bare wafer to packaged die, step by step, including junctions, airbridges and 3D integration.
  • Understand the difference between a research cleanroom and a production line, and what statistical process control and \(C_{pk}\) mean.
  • See where variation enters at each step, which is what the rest of this book models.

A quantum chip is made the same way as any other microchip: by building up and carving away thin films on a flat disc of crystal called a wafer, using light or electron beams to define patterns. The process is a recipe of dozens of steps, each performed by a machine in a cleanroom. Every step has small, random imperfections, and quantum devices are unusually sensitive to them: a junction's oxide changing by a fraction of an atom's width shifts a qubit's frequency by tens of megahertz (Book 6). To design quantum chips that work reliably, you need to understand what each step does, where its variation comes from, and how factories keep that variation under control.

Wafers and cleanrooms

A wafer is a thin disc of single-crystal material, polished flat to within nanometres. Quantum chips use high-resistivity silicon (to avoid electrical loss in the substrate) or sapphire, in diameters of 100 mm in research labs up to 200 and 300 mm in industrial lines. One wafer holds many chips (dies), from a handful of large processors to hundreds of small test chips, which are cut apart at the end.

Fabrication happens in a cleanroom, where air is filtered so that a cubic metre holds at most a few hundred dust particles larger than 0.1 µm (ordinary air holds millions). A single particle landing on a 100 nm feature can ruin a device, which is the origin of defect-limited yield (Chapter 4).

The unit processes

Every chip is made by repeating a handful of basic operations [@plummer2000]:

1. Deposition: adding a film.

  • Evaporation: heat a metal source in high vacuum until atoms boil off and fly in straight lines to the wafer. Because the atoms travel in straight lines, evaporation is directional, which is exactly what shadow-evaporated junctions exploit (@b6:sec-h-jj-fab).
  • Sputtering: bombard a target with ions from a plasma, knocking atoms off it onto the wafer. Gives denser films that also coat sidewalls; standard for niobium, tantalum and titanium nitride.
  • Atomic layer deposition (ALD): grow a film one atomic layer at a time by alternating two chemical gases. Extremely precise thickness; used for oxides and nitrides.
  • Oxidation: expose a metal to oxygen to grow a thin oxide on its surface. The Josephson junction barrier is made this way; its thickness is set by oxygen pressure and time.

2. Lithography: defining a pattern. Spin a thin layer of light-sensitive polymer (resist) onto the wafer. Expose it, either through a patterned mask with ultraviolet light (optical lithography) or by writing the pattern point by point with a focused electron beam (e-beam lithography). A chemical developer then dissolves the exposed (or unexposed) resist, leaving a stencil.

How small can optical lithography print? Light cannot be focused to a spot much smaller than its wavelength, so the minimum feature size scales with the wavelength \(\lambda\) divided by the lens's numerical aperture NA (its light-gathering angle). The rule of thumb is \(R = k_1\lambda/\mathrm{NA}\), with \(k_1\approx0.3\)–0.6 depending on clever tricks [@mack2007]. With 365 nm "i-line" light, \(R\approx250\)–350 nm; with 193 nm deep-UV scanners, below 100 nm. Electron beams have tiny wavelengths and can write 10 nm features, but they write one point at a time, so they are slow. Research labs use e-beam for junctions; production lines prefer optical steppers that expose a whole die in a flash.

3. Etching: removing material. Where the resist does not protect the film, remove it. Wet etching uses liquid chemicals; it etches in all directions equally (isotropically), so it undercuts the resist. Dry etching (reactive-ion etching) uses a plasma whose ions strike the wafer vertically, giving straight sidewalls. Etching the silicon between metal lines ("trenching") moves the lossy substrate surface away from the strongest fields, improving coherence (@b6:sec-h-d-part).

4. Lift-off: the alternative to etching. Pattern the resist first, deposit metal over everything, then dissolve the resist: the metal on top of the resist washes away, leaving metal only where the resist had openings. Avoids exposing the film to etch chemistry, but edges depend on the resist's profile.

5. Metrology: measuring what was made. Film thickness (ellipsometry, profilometry), linewidths (scanning electron microscopy), sheet resistance (four-point probes), surface chemistry (X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy), and electrical test structures placed beside the real devices.

A superconducting-qubit process flow, step by step

  1. Substrate preparation. Clean the wafer; often strip the native silicon oxide with a hydrofluoric acid dip just before metal deposition, because the metal–substrate interface is one of the lossiest regions in the chip (@b6:sec-h-d-budget).
  2. Base metal. Sputter 100–200 nm of niobium, tantalum or aluminium over the whole wafer.
  3. Base pattern. Optical lithography and dry etch define ground planes, qubit capacitor pads, resonators, feedlines and control lines. Small holes etched into the ground planes trap magnetic vortices (@b6:sec-h-sc-psi).
  4. Junctions. E-beam (or advanced optical) lithography of a two-layer resist with an undercut; double-angle aluminium evaporation with a controlled oxidation in between; lift-off. An argon ion-milling step first removes native oxide where the junction leads must make electrical contact to the base metal.
  5. Airbridges. Arch-shaped resist forms and a further metal layer create small bridges that cross coplanar lines and tie the ground planes on either side together, suppressing unwanted modes (@b6:sec-h-d-other).
  6. Room-temperature test. A wafer prober touches needles to test pads and measures junction resistances across the wafer (Book 6, @b6:sec-h-jj-ab). Optionally, laser annealing raises selected junctions' resistances by a few percent to trim qubit frequencies towards target (@lf:sec-var-trim).
  7. Dicing and packaging. Coat with protective resist, saw the wafer into dies, clean them, and mount each in a package with wirebonds or bump bonds (Chapter 5).

Larger processors add 3D integration. A qubit chip can be flip-chip bonded face-down onto a second chip carrying readout resonators and control wiring, joined by small indium bumps; the bump height sets the gap between chips and hence capacitances. Through-silicon vias (metal-lined holes through the wafer) connect top and bottom surfaces and suppress substrate modes. Each addition brings new process steps, new interfaces, and new sources of variation.

Worked example: where a qubit's frequency error comes from. Follow one transmon through the flow. Its capacitance depends on the pad shapes (step 3: linewidth and etch bias errors of ~10 nm on 20 µm gaps, about 0.1–0.5% in capacitance) and on the substrate's permittivity and any trenching depth. Its \(E_J\) depends on the junction area (step 4: ~5 nm error on a ~150 nm junction, about 7% in area if both dimensions are off) and the oxide thickness (step 4: oxidation pressure, time and temperature, plus the time the wafer waits before the next step). Junction errors dominate: that is why step 6 measures and trims junctions, and why the review focuses on junction statistics (@lf:sec-var-junction).

Research cleanroom versus production line

A university cleanroom optimises flexibility: tools are shared among many projects, recipes change weekly, wafers are processed one at a time, and the record of what happened is a lab notebook. A production line optimises reproducibility: every tool is qualified and dedicated, every recipe frozen, every wafer tracked automatically through a manufacturing execution system (which tool, which recipe, when), and every critical parameter measured and plotted.

Statistical process control (SPC) is the discipline of watching those plots. Each critical parameter (say, the resistance of a standard test junction on every wafer) is plotted over time on a control chart with its target and control limits (typically ±3 standard deviations). Points drifting outside the limits, or runs of points on one side of the target, trigger investigation before product is ruined. How well a process can meet a specification \([L, U]\) is summarised by the process capability index

$$ C_{pk} = \min\Big(\frac{U - \mu}{3\sigma}, \frac{\mu - L}{3\sigma}\Big) , $$

where \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are the process mean and standard deviation. \(C_{pk} = 1\) means the nearest specification limit is three standard deviations from the mean (about 0.13% out of specification on that side); industry typically demands \(C_{pk}\ge1.33\) (four standard deviations, about 0.003%).

Worked example: \(C_{pk}\) for junctions. Specification \(10.0\pm0.3\) kΩ. Process: \(\mu = 10.1\) kΩ, \(\sigma = 0.08\) kΩ. Upper side \((10.3 - 10.1)/0.24 = 0.83\); lower side \((10.1 - 9.7)/0.24 = 1.67\). So \(C_{pk} = 0.83\): the process is off-centre, and about 0.6% of junctions exceed the upper limit. Re-centring to \(\mu = 10.0\) gives \(C_{pk} = 1.25\) and only 0.02% out. Often the cheapest improvement is to centre the process, not to tighten it.

Between 2024 and 2026, superconducting, ion-trap, photonic and spin-qubit programmes moved onto 200 and 300 mm production lines, and foundries opened to outside customers (@lf:sec-land-foundry). That move makes SPC data available at scale, and it is the precondition for the statistical design methods of Chapters 4 and 6.

Each process step is a source of variation with spatial structure: film thickness varies smoothly across a wafer, linewidths vary with local pattern density, oxidation varies from run to run. A design tool that predicts yield needs a model of this structure, built from metrology and test data. The review's "die record" (@lf:sec-prob-record) is the data structure that links each chip's process history to its measured performance, so such models can be built and checked.

  • Chips are built by repeating deposition, lithography, etching or lift-off, and metrology on a wafer in a cleanroom.
  • Optical lithography resolution is \(R = k_1\lambda/\mathrm{NA}\); e-beam is finer but slower.
  • A transmon flow: base metal, pattern, junctions by double-angle evaporation, airbridges, warm test and trim, dice and package; 3D integration adds bumps and vias.
  • Junction area and oxide thickness dominate qubit-frequency error.
  • Production lines use SPC and \(C_{pk}\) to keep parameters centred and tight; centring is often the cheapest fix.

Exercises

  1. An i-line stepper has NA = 0.57 and \(k_1 = 0.5\). What is its resolution? Can it pattern a 150 nm wide junction lead?
    Solution

    \(R = 0.5\times365/0.57\approx320\) nm. No: a 150 nm lead needs deep-UV (193 nm, NA 0.93 gives about 100 nm) or e-beam lithography, or a junction design (overlap junctions) that relaxes the requirement.

  2. Junction resistance has a specification of \(10.0\pm0.3\) kΩ. The process gives \(\mu = 10.1\) kΩ and \(\sigma = 0.08\) kΩ. Compute \(C_{pk}\) and the expected fraction out of specification.
    Solution

    \(C_{pk} = 0.83\). Upper tail \(1-\Phi(2.5) = 0.62\%\), lower tail \(1-\Phi(5)\approx3\times10^{-7}\): about 0.6%.

  3. A wafer of 200 mm diameter holds square dies of 10 mm side. Roughly how many whole dies fit? If particles land at 0.05 per cm² and any particle kills a die, what fraction survive?
    Solution

    Wafer area \(\pi\times10^2 = 314\) cm²; die area 1 cm²; allowing for edge loss, about 270–290 whole dies. Survival \(\ee^{-0.05\times1} = 95\%\) (Poisson model, Chapter 4).

  4. Why does the time between oxidation and the second aluminium evaporation matter for junction reproducibility?
    Solution

    The oxide keeps growing slowly and adsorbs contaminants while it waits, and the chamber's residual gases change. Variable waiting time means variable effective barrier thickness, and because tunnelling is exponential in thickness, even small differences change \(R_n\) noticeably. Production lines fix such waiting times as part of the recipe.
