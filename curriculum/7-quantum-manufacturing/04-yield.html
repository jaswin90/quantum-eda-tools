Variability and yield

Goals

  • Break manufacturing variation into its parts (lot, wafer, spatial gradient, local random) and know why the distinction matters.
  • Compute defect-limited yield with the Poisson and negative-binomial models.
  • Compute parametric yield by propagating variation through a design with its Jacobian.
  • Build the frequency-collision yield model for a qubit lattice step by step, and see why yield collapses over a narrow range of process spread.
  • Know the strategies for designing for yield: centring, robustness, tunability, trimming, chance constraints.

"Yield" is the fraction of manufactured chips that work. For classical chips it is the number that decides whether a product makes money. For quantum chips it is even more critical, because a processor needs every one of its hundreds of qubits to land inside narrow windows, and the probability that all of them do falls exponentially with their number (@b1:sec-m-prob-yield). This chapter builds the models that predict yield: first for random defects that kill chips outright, then for the gradual variation that pushes parameters out of specification, and finally for the frequency collisions that are the central yield problem of superconducting processors. These are the models behind the review's §4.

Where variation comes from, and its structure

Any manufactured parameter \(x\) (a junction resistance, a film thickness, a linewidth) can be written as a sum of contributions at different scales:

$$ x(\mathbf r, w, l) = x_0 + \delta_{\mathrm{lot}}(l) + \delta_{\mathrm{wafer}}(w) + g_w(\mathbf r) + \epsilon(\mathbf r) . $$

Read it from left to right:

  • \(x_0\): the target.
  • \(\delta_{\mathrm{lot}}\): a shift shared by a batch ("lot") of wafers processed together (same chemicals, same tool state).
  • \(\delta_{\mathrm{wafer}}\): a shift for each wafer.
  • \(g_w(\mathbf r)\): a smooth pattern across the wafer, often radial (deposition and oxidation are less uniform at the edge).
  • \(\epsilon(\mathbf r)\): local random variation, uncorrelated between devices more than a short distance apart (Pelgrom mismatch, @eq:f-pelgrom).

The parts behave differently, and each needs a different response. Global shifts (lot, wafer) move every device together and can be corrected by re-centring the process, or measured on a few devices and subtracted. Smooth gradients can be predicted from nearby test structures. Local randomness cannot be predicted at all, only reduced by design (larger devices) or corrected device by device (trimming). A nested variance model estimates each component's size from test data; it is the starting point of the hierarchical models of Chapter 6.

Worked example: decomposing variance. Junction resistances on 20 wafers have total standard deviation 3%. Averaging per wafer shows wafer means scattered by 2%; within each wafer, a radial fit explains 1.5%, leaving 1.5% local scatter. Check: \(\sqrt{2^2 + 1.5^2 + 1.5^2} = 2.9\%\). Implication: measuring a few junctions per wafer and correcting the wafer offset removes the 2%, and modelling the radial pattern removes another 1.5%, leaving only the 1.5% local part for trimming or design margins.

Defect-limited yield

Random defects (particles, resist residues, shorts, opens) kill a die if one lands in a sensitive region of area \(A\) (the critical area). If defects land independently at density \(D_0\) per unit area, the number in the critical area follows a Poisson distribution with mean \(AD_0\) (@b1:sec-m-prob-dists), and the probability of zero defects is

$$ Y_{\mathrm{Poisson}} = \ee^{-AD_0}, \qquad Y_{\mathrm{NB}} = \Big(1 + \frac{AD_0}{\alpha}\Big)^{-\alpha} . $$

Real defects cluster (a bad patch of resist ruins a whole area), which the negative binomial model captures with a clustering parameter \(\alpha\): small \(\alpha\) means strong clustering; \(\alpha\to\infty\) recovers Poisson [@stapper1983]. Clustering helps large dies: if defects bunch together, more dies escape them entirely (@fig:f-defect).

For quantum chips the "critical area" includes not only shorts and opens but anything that ruins a qubit: a junction that failed to form, a two-level defect strongly coupled to a qubit (@b6:sec-h-d-tls), a trapped vortex near a SQUID. Error correction can tolerate a few dead qubits by routing around them, at a cost in effective code distance (@lf:sec-qec-dem), which turns a pass/fail yield into a graded one.

Parametric yield

Even without defects, a die fails if its parameters land outside specification. Let \(\mathbf x\) be the process variables (junction resistances, film thicknesses...) with a probability distribution \(p(\mathbf x)\), and \(\boldsymbol\theta = f(\mathbf x)\) the device parameters (frequencies, couplings...) computed from them by the design (the "design map", Books 6 and this one). The parametric yield is

$$ Y = \Pr_{\mathbf x\sim p(\mathbf x)}\big[f(\mathbf x)\in\mathcal A\big] , $$

where \(\mathcal A\) is the acceptable region (all specifications met). When variations are small, linearise \(f\) around the nominal design with its Jacobian \(\mathbf J = \partial\boldsymbol\theta/\partial\mathbf x\): the parameter covariance is \(\mathbf J\Sigma_x\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}\) (@b1:eq-m-propagation). If the specifications are a box (each parameter within limits), the yield is a multivariate Gaussian probability, computed by Monte Carlo or with bounds.

Two standard design moves follow. Design centring moves the nominal point to the middle of the acceptable region, maximising margin in every direction. Robust design reduces \(\|\mathbf J\|\), the sensitivity, where physics allows: for example, larger junctions (Pelgrom), or operating flux-tunable qubits at a sweet spot where \(\partial\omega/\partial\Phi = 0\).

Frequency collisions, step by step

Now the model behind the review's @lf:fig-yield. Consider a square lattice of fixed-frequency transmons coupled for cross-resonance gates (@b6:sec-h-g-cr).

Step 1: the design. Neighbouring qubits are designed to differ in frequency by \(\mu\) (say 100 MHz), using a repeating pattern of a few frequencies.

Step 2: the variation. Each qubit's fabricated frequency is Gaussian with standard deviation \(\sigma\) around its target, independently (mostly from junction spread, @b6:sec-h-jj-sens). For a coupled pair, the detuning \(\Delta = f_i - f_j\) is then Gaussian with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(2\sigma^2\) (variances of independent variables add).

Step 3: the forbidden windows. A pair fails if \(\Delta\) lands in any forbidden window \((c_k - w_k, c_k + w_k)\): near zero (the qubits swap energy), near \(\pm\alpha/2\) (two-photon transitions), and outside the range where the gate is fast enough [@brink2018; @hertzberg2021].

Step 4: the per-pair failure probability. For each window, the probability that a Gaussian lands in an interval is a difference of cumulative normal distributions \(\Phi\) (@b1:sec-m-prob-dists):

$$ p_{\mathrm{edge}}(\sigma) = \sum_k\Big[\Phi\Big(\frac{c_k + w_k - \mu}{\sqrt2\sigma}\Big) - \Phi\Big(\frac{c_k - w_k - \mu}{\sqrt2\sigma}\Big)\Big] + \Pr\big(|\Delta| > \Delta_{\max}\big) . $$

Step 5: the chip. If edges failed independently, a chip with \(E\) couplings would be collision-free with probability \(Y\approx(1-p_{\mathrm{edge}})^E\approx\ee^{-Ep_{\mathrm{edge}}}\). (Edges sharing a qubit are actually correlated, and some conditions involve three qubits; Monte Carlo over the whole lattice gives the exact number for a given model.)

Worked example: how fast yield collapses. One forbidden window \(|\Delta| \lt 17\) MHz, \(\mu = 100\) MHz, 200 couplings.

At \(\sigma = 30\) MHz: \(\sqrt2\sigma = 42.4\); \(p_{\mathrm{edge}} = \Phi((17-100)/42.4) - \Phi((-17-100)/42.4) = \Phi(-1.96) - \Phi(-2.76) = 0.0250 - 0.0029 = 0.022\). Then \(Y = (1-0.022)^{200}\approx1.1\%\).

At \(\sigma = 18\) MHz: \(\sqrt2\sigma = 25.5\); \(p_{\mathrm{edge}}\approx\Phi(-3.26)\approx5.6\times10^{-4}\); \(Y\approx\ee^{-0.11}\approx90\%\).

Reducing \(\sigma\) by a factor of 1.7 takes yield from 1% to 90%. Because \(p_{\mathrm{edge}}\) comes from Gaussian tails, yield is a steep cliff in \(\sigma\), and because \(E\) grows with qubit count, the cliff moves to smaller \(\sigma\) as chips grow: holding yield requires \(p_{\mathrm{edge}}\propto1/E\). At \(\sigma\approx50\) MHz (about 2% junction spread), essentially no large fixed-frequency lattice is collision-free.

Designing for yield

  • Frequency allocation: choose the target pattern (how many distinct frequencies, which detunings) to maximise yield under the fabrication distribution, not to hit a nominal optimum. With uncertainty this is a chance-constrained optimisation: maximise performance subject to \(\Pr[\text{all constraints met}]\ge1-\epsilon\) [@morvan2022; @luedtke2008].
  • Tunability: flux-tunable qubits or couplers absorb fabrication error, at the cost of wiring and flux noise (@b6:sec-h-g-coupler).
  • Trimming: measure each junction warm, then laser-anneal it towards target. Demonstrated processes reduce frequency spread from about 2% to below 0.5% [@zhang2022], at the cost of test time, and only as well as cold frequencies can be predicted from warm resistance, which is itself statistical (@lf:sec-var-trim).
  • Architecture: tunable couplers widen collision windows; codes and compilers that can skip bad couplings convert a hard pass/fail yield into a graded one.

Optimising the nominal design and then "adding margin" is not the same as optimising yield. A nominal optimum often sits close to the edge of a constraint (because pushing a parameter improves performance until it hits a limit). Under variation, a design slightly back from that edge can have dramatically higher yield at almost the same nominal performance. Yield-aware design optimises the expected outcome over the distribution, which requires the statistical models of Chapter 6.

This chapter is a compressed version of the review's §4 (@lf:sec-var). Its open problems (statistical sign-off with calibrated uncertainty, chance-constrained allocation with correlated variation, warm-to-cold prediction for trimming) all start from these models, fed with data from real production lines.

  • Variation = lot + wafer + smooth gradient + local random; each part needs a different correction.
  • Defect yield: \(\ee^{-AD_0}\) (Poisson), softened by clustering (negative binomial).
  • Parametric yield = probability that the design map sends the process distribution into spec; linearise with the Jacobian.
  • Collision yield \(\approx(1-p_{\mathrm{edge}})^E\) with \(p_{\mathrm{edge}}\) from Gaussian tails: a steep cliff in \(\sigma\) that tightens as chips grow.
  • Design for yield with centring, robustness, tunability, trimming and chance-constrained allocation.

Exercises

  1. A 1 cm² die with \(D_0 = 0.1\) cm\(^{-2}\): compute Poisson yield and negative-binomial yield with \(\alpha = 0.5\). Repeat for 4 cm².
    Solution

    1 cm²: Poisson \(\ee^{-0.1} = 0.905\); NB \((1+0.2)^{-0.5} = 0.913\). 4 cm²: Poisson \(\ee^{-0.4} = 0.670\); NB \((1+0.8)^{-0.5} = 0.745\).

  2. With a single forbidden window \(|\Delta| \lt 17\) MHz, \(\mu = 100\) MHz and \(\sigma = 30\) MHz, compute \(p_{\mathrm{edge}}\) and the yield with 200 edges.
    Solution

    \(p_{\mathrm{edge}} = \Phi(-1.96) - \Phi(-2.76) = 0.0221\); \(Y = (1-0.0221)^{200}\approx1.1\%\).

  3. In the previous exercise, what \(\sigma\) gives 90% yield?
    Solution

    Need \(p_{\mathrm{edge}}\approx\ln(1/0.9)/200 = 5.3\times10^{-4}\). Dominated by the upper edge: \(\Phi((17-100)/\sqrt2\sigma) = 5.3\times10^{-4}\) gives \(83/(\sqrt2\sigma) = 3.27\), \(\sigma\approx18\) MHz.

  4. A qubit frequency depends on \(E_J\) and \(C\) as \(f\propto\sqrt{E_J/C}\). With independent relative spreads of 2% in \(E_J\) and 1% in \(C\), what is the frequency spread at 5 GHz? Which spread should the process team attack first?
    Solution

    Relative spread \(\tfrac12\sqrt{0.02^2+0.01^2} = 1.12\%\), i.e. 56 MHz. The \(E_J\) term contributes 80% of the variance, so junction control comes first.

  5. Why does doubling the number of qubits on a chip (at fixed \(\sigma\)) roughly square the collision yield, and what does that imply for scaling?
    Solution

    The number of couplings \(E\) roughly doubles, and \(Y\approx(1-p_{\mathrm{edge}})^E\), so \(Y_{2E} = Y_E^2\). A 90% chip becomes 81%, then 66%, 43%... with each doubling. Scaling requires \(p_{\mathrm{edge}}\) to fall in proportion to \(1/E\), i.e. tighter fabrication, wider windows (tunable couplers), or trimming.
