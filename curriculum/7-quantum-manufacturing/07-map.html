Reading the review: a map and seven projects

Goals

  • Know, for each section of From Laboratory to Foundry, which chapters of this series prepare for it.
  • Turn each of the review's seven open problems into a project you can start with the tools of this course.

Section-by-section preparation

The review assumes graduate quantum mechanics and fluency in statistics; this series supplies both and the engineering in between. Before each section of the review, re-read the chapters listed.

Seven projects

Each project below is a small, self-contained version of one of the review's open problems, sized for a few weeks with Python, NumPy/SciPy and open-source tools (scqubits, Stim, an open FEM solver such as Palace or Elmer). Each is a stepping stone, not a solution.

  1. Variation-aware frequency planning (Problem 1). Implement the collision model of @eq:f-pedge for a 5×5 square lattice with three or four frequency groups, including spectator conditions. Estimate yield by Monte Carlo versus \(\sigma\). Then optimise the target frequencies to maximise yield at \(\sigma = 20\) MHz, and compare with the nominal-optimal design. Uses: @sec:f-yield, @b1:sec-m-numerics, @b6:sec-h-gates.
  2. Extraction with an error bar (Problem 2). Simulate a transmon pad pair with an open electrostatic FEM solver on a sequence of meshes; extract \(C_\Sigma\) and the coupling capacitance; extrapolate in mesh size and report a convergence-based uncertainty. Propagate it to \(f_{01}\) and \(g\). Uses: @sec:f-em, @b2:sec-c-statics, @b6:sec-h-quant.
  3. Layout-versus-Hamiltonian check (Problem 3). Define a small netlist schema (nodes, capacitances, junctions, couplings) and write a checker that compares the Hamiltonian parameters derived from an extracted netlist with the designer's intended ones, flagging any parameter outside tolerance and any near-collision. Uses: @sec:f-eda, @b6:sec-h-quant, @b3:sec-q-pert.
  4. Warm-to-cold prediction (Problem 4). Generate synthetic wafer data from the hierarchical model of @eq:f-hier (with a radial gradient and a warm-to-cold shift), fit it with a probabilistic programming library, and use posterior predictions to decide which junctions to laser-trim and which dies to cool. Measure how many cold measurements per wafer are needed. Uses: @sec:f-stats, @b6:sec-h-jj, @sec:f-pkg.
  5. Adaptive characterisation (Problem 5). Simulate Ramsey experiments on a qubit with unknown detuning and \(T_2^*\), and implement Bayesian adaptive choice of delays. Compare the number of shots needed against a fixed linear sweep. Uses: @b3:sec-q-spin, @b3:sec-q-open, @sec:f-stats.
  6. From device parameters to \(\Lambda\) (Problem 6). Sample device parameters for a distance-5 surface-code patch from a variation model, convert them to per-gate error rates with @b4:eq-i-coh plus a \(ZZ\) term, build the noisy circuit in Stim, decode with matching, and estimate the distribution of logical error rates and \(\Lambda\) across fabricated chips. Uses: @b4:sec-i-qec2, @b4:sec-i-noise, @b6:sec-h-decoh.
  7. Photonic path-loss sign-off (Problem 7). Model a 16-mode Clements mesh with per-MZI loss and splitting-ratio distributions; compute by Monte Carlo the probability that every path meets a loss threshold and that the mesh can still be programmed to a target unitary. Uses: @b5:sec-p-linear, @b5:sec-p-integrated, @b5:sec-p-qc.

How to read a section of the review

The review is written for specialists and moves fast. Each of its technical sections follows the same pattern, and knowing the pattern makes it far easier to read:

  1. The physics. A few equations that state how a design or process quantity maps to a device parameter. For each one, find where this course derived it (the table above), and re-read that derivation first.
  2. The numbers. An estimate showing why the problem is hard, usually a sensitivity ("a 1% error moves the frequency by 25 MHz") or a scaling ("cost grows as \(N^2\)"). Redo each estimate yourself with a calculator. If your number differs, find out why.
  3. The tools. What existing commercial and open-source software does. Ask which step of the flow in @sec:f-eda each tool corresponds to.
  4. The gap. What is missing. Ask whether it is engineering (an existing method needs porting) or research (no method exists).

Worked example: reading the review's §3.2, "The parameter vector a designer must hit". The section writes a multi-qubit Hamiltonian (@lf:eq-multi). You know every term: qubit frequencies and Kerr terms from @b6:sec-h-q-transmon, exchange couplings from @b6:sec-h-g-zz, resonators and their couplings from @b6:sec-h-cqed. It then gives the dispersive shift (@lf:eq-chi, which you derived in @b3:sec-q-pert-sw and used in @b6:sec-h-cq-disp) and the \(ZZ\) formula (@lf:eq-zz, from @b6:sec-h-g-zz). Finally it derives the sensitivity \(\delta\omega/\omega\approx\tfrac12\delta E_J/E_J - \tfrac12\delta C/C\) (@lf:eq-sens). Check it: from \(\hbar\omega_{01}\approx\sqrt{8E_JE_C} - E_C\), differentiate with respect to \(E_J\) and \(C\) (\(E_C\propto1/C\)); the square root gives the factors of ½. Then redo its punchline: a 1% error in either gives about 25 MHz at 5 GHz. If you can do this for every section, you have understood the review.

Walking through an open problem: Problem 4

To show how the pieces fit, here is Problem 4 (warm-to-cold prediction, trim planning and die ranking) unpacked using only this course.

  1. What is measured warm? Every junction's resistance \(R_n\), by a wafer prober in seconds (@sec:f-fab-flow, @b6:sec-h-jj-ab).
  2. What do we want cold? Each qubit's frequency \(f_{01}\), which depends on \(E_J\) through \(\sqrt{8E_JE_C}\) (@b6:eq-h-transmon), and \(E_J\) on \(R_n\) through Ambegaokar–Baratoff (@b6:eq-h-ab).
  3. Why is the prediction uncertain? The ratio between warm and cold resistance varies by wafer and by position (oxide ageing, film thickness affecting the gap), and capacitances vary too. That is a nested variance structure (@eq:f-var).
  4. The model. A hierarchical regression (@eq:f-hier): physics gives the mean, random effects capture wafer and die offsets, and a few cold measurements per wafer update them.
  5. The decisions. (a) Trim planning: for each junction, choose a laser-anneal amount that moves its predicted frequency to target, accounting for the prediction's uncertainty (trimming can only increase resistance, so aim slightly high). (b) Die ranking: compute each die's probability of meeting its collision-free yield condition (@eq:f-pedge) and cool the best first (@sec:f-pkg-scarce).
  6. Validation. Hold out some wafers; check that the predicted 90% intervals for cold frequencies contain the measured values about 90% of the time (@sec:f-st-cal), and that ranking dies this way finds good chips faster than random order.

Each step is a few hundred lines of code with standard libraries. Doing it with real production data, and showing it improves yield and saves cold-test time, is a research contribution.

Where to go next

The review's reading guide (@lf:sec-reading) and the annotated reading list in the repository point to the primary literature, tiered by how deep you need to go. With this series behind you, the Tier 1 papers there should read as applications of ideas you have already met. The natural next step is to pick one project above, find the two or three papers the review cites for that problem, and reproduce one of their figures before trying to improve on it.

The thesis of the review is that quantum processors are now manufactured, and that what separates a research device from a product is the ability to predict, before fabrication, the distribution of what will be made. This series has built the physics and the mathematics behind that sentence. The review is where it is turned into a research agenda.
