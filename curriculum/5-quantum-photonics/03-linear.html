Linear optics and two-photon interference

Goals

  • Describe a beam splitter quantum mechanically, as a transformation of mode operators, and compute what it does to photons.
  • Derive the Hong–Ou–Mandel effect, and understand indistinguishability and how it is measured.
  • Understand how any multimode interferometer is built from beam splitters and phase shifters, and why boson sampling is hard.
  • Know how qubits are encoded in photons, and model loss as a beam splitter.

"Linear optics" means everything you can do to light with passive components: beam splitters, phase shifters, waveguides and mirrors, which mix light between paths but never create or destroy photons. Classically, linear optics is described by a matrix that mixes field amplitudes. The remarkable fact of this chapter is that the same matrix describes the quantum behaviour too, but acting on photon creation operators. With it we can predict a striking two-photon effect (Hong–Ou–Mandel), build any interferometer, encode qubits in photons, and model loss. Linear optics is the backbone of photonic quantum computers.

The beam splitter, classically and quantum mechanically

A beam splitter (in free space, a half-silvered mirror; on a chip, a directional coupler, @b2:sec-c-opt-dc) has two inputs and two outputs. Classically, the output field amplitudes are linear combinations of the input amplitudes, described by a \(2\times2\) matrix. Energy conservation (output power = input power, for a lossless device) forces this matrix to be unitary, as for any lossless S-matrix (@b2:sec-c-tl-s).

Quantum mechanically, the same matrix transforms the annihilation operators of the input modes \(a_1, a_2\) into those of the output modes \(b_1, b_2\):

$$ \begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}t & r'\\ r & t'\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{50:50:}\ \ b_1 = \frac{a_1 + \ii a_2}{\sqrt2},\ \ b_2 = \frac{\ii a_1 + a_2}{\sqrt2} . $$

(The factor \(\ii\) on reflection is a convention for a symmetric beam splitter; other conventions differ by phases and give the same physics.) Unitarity guarantees that the outputs satisfy the right commutation relations, \([b_j, b_k^\dagger] = \delta_{jk}\), so they are legitimate modes.

How to compute what happens to photons. Write the input state as creation operators acting on the vacuum, then express each input creation operator in terms of output ones. For a unitary \(U\), the inverse relation is \(a_k^\dagger = \sum_jU_{jk}b_j^\dagger\), so for the 50:50 beam splitter

\[ a_1^\dagger = \frac{b_1^\dagger + \ii b_2^\dagger}{\sqrt2}, \qquad a_2^\dagger = \frac{\ii b_1^\dagger + b_2^\dagger}{\sqrt2} . \]

Worked example: one photon. A photon enters port 1: \(a_1^\dagger\ket0\). Substitute: \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(b_1^\dagger + \ii b_2^\dagger)\ket0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{1,0} + \ii\ket{0,1})\), where \(\ket{m,n}\) means \(m\) photons in output 1 and \(n\) in output 2. The photon is in a superposition of the two outputs. A detector finds it in one or the other with probability ½ each, never split in half. This superposition of paths is a dual-rail qubit (below).

The Hong–Ou–Mandel effect

Now send one photon into each input at the same time, \(a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger\ket0\). Substitute both:

$$ a_1^\dagger a_2^\dagger\ket0 = \frac12\big(b_1^\dagger + \ii b_2^\dagger\big)\big(\ii b_1^\dagger + b_2^\dagger\big)\ket0 = \frac12\big(\ii\,b_1^{\dagger2} + b_1^\dagger b_2^\dagger - b_2^\dagger b_1^\dagger + \ii\,b_2^{\dagger2}\big)\ket0 = \frac{\ii}{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{2,0} + \ket{0,2}\big). $$

Look at the middle terms. \(b_1^\dagger b_2^\dagger\) comes from "both photons transmitted" and \(-b_2^\dagger b_1^\dagger\) from "both reflected" (each reflection contributes a factor \(\ii\), and \(\ii\cdot\ii = -1\)). Creation operators of different modes commute, so the two terms are equal and opposite, and they cancel exactly. The outcome "one photon in each output" never happens: the photons always leave together, both in output 1 or both in output 2 (using \(b^{\dagger2}\ket0 = \sqrt2\ket2\)). This is the Hong–Ou–Mandel (HOM) effect [@hom1987].

Classically, two independent particles at a 50:50 splitter end up in different outputs half the time. For photons, the two ways to get "one in each" (both transmit, both reflect) are indistinguishable alternatives, so their amplitudes add (@b3:sec-q-why-duality), and they are equal and opposite. It is interference between two-photon amplitudes, a consequence of photons being identical bosons (@b3:sec-q-sec-ident). There is no way to see it with classical waves or with distinguishable particles.

Distinguishability. The cancellation requires the two photons to be identical in every respect: frequency, polarisation, spatial mode, arrival time and temporal shape. If they are partly distinguishable, the two paths are partly distinguishable too, and the cancellation is incomplete. If one photon is delayed by \(\tau\), the probability of one photon in each output is

$$ P_c(\tau) = \frac12\Big(1 - V\,\big|\textstyle\int\xi_1^*(t)\,\xi_2(t-\tau)\,\dd t\big|^2\Big), $$

where \(\xi_{1,2}\) are the wavepackets and \(V\) the visibility. At \(\tau = 0\) with identical photons (\(V = 1\)), \(P_c = 0\); far from \(\tau = 0\) the wavepackets do not overlap and \(P_c = \tfrac12\), the classical value (@fig:p-hom). Scanning the delay and measuring this "HOM dip" is the standard measurement of photon indistinguishability. It must be close to perfect for photonic quantum computing, because every entangling operation relies on exactly this interference, and residual distinguishability acts as an error.

Multimode interferometers

With \(N\) waveguides, a linear optical circuit implements \(b_j = \sum_kU_{jk}a_k\) with an \(N\times N\) unitary \(U\). Which \(U\) can be built? All of them. Reck et al. showed that any \(U\) can be decomposed into \(N(N-1)/2\) two-mode beam splitters with adjustable splitting ratio and phase, arranged in a triangle [@reck1994]. In practice each adjustable beam splitter is a Mach–Zehnder interferometer (two 50:50 couplers with a phase shifter between them, @b2:eq-c-mzi) plus an external phase shifter. Clements et al. found a rectangular arrangement with the same count in which every path passes through the same number of elements, so losses are balanced, which matters a lot [@clements2016].

Worked example: size of a programmable mesh. A 12-mode universal interferometer needs \(12\times11/2 = 66\) MZIs, each with two phase shifters: 132 phase shifters to calibrate. In the Clements layout, the longest path crosses 12 MZIs. At 0.1 dB per MZI that is 1.2 dB, so a photon reaches the output with probability \(10^{-0.12} = 76\%\).

Real meshes are limited by fabrication: if a nominally 50:50 coupler is actually 47:53, the MZI built from it cannot reach a full 0 or 100% split, so some unitaries become unreachable unless the mesh has extra redundant elements. Heaters controlling phases also heat their neighbours (thermal crosstalk), so calibration must account for it.

Boson sampling. Send \(n\) single photons into \(n\) of the \(N\) input modes of a random interferometer and record where they come out. The probability of a given output pattern is the squared modulus of the permanent of an \(n\times n\) submatrix of \(U\). The permanent is like the determinant but with all plus signs, and, unlike the determinant, no efficient algorithm for it is known: computing it is #P-hard. Sampling from this distribution, boson sampling [@aaronson2011], is believed to be classically intractable once \(n\) reaches about 50. That made it a target for early demonstrations of quantum advantage. The Gaussian variant, with squeezed states instead of single photons [@hamilton2017], was run at large scale by Xanadu's Borealis machine (@lf:sec-mod-phot). Boson sampling is not a general-purpose computer, but it shows that even "just linear optics plus detectors" is hard to simulate.

Photonic qubits

  • Dual rail: one photon in one of two waveguides, \(\ket0_L = \ket{1,0}\) and \(\ket1_L = \ket{0,1}\). A single-qubit gate is an MZI (a programmable \(2\times2\) unitary on the two paths). This is the natural encoding on chips.
  • Polarisation: horizontal and vertical polarisation of one photon, manipulated with wave plates. Natural in free space and fibre.
  • Time bin: the photon arrives early or late. Robust over long fibres.

Single-qubit gates are easy and accurate in all of them. Two-qubit gates are the problem: linear optics never makes one photon affect another (a beam splitter acts on each photon's creation operator separately; interference like HOM is not an interaction). Chapter 7 shows how measurement and feed-forward supply the missing ingredient.

Loss as a beam splitter

Any loss with transmission \(\eta\) can be modelled as a beam splitter with transmission amplitude \(\sqrt\eta\) whose second input is the vacuum and whose second output is thrown away (traced out, @b3:sec-q-comp-reduced). Substituting \(a^\dagger\to\sqrt\eta\,b^\dagger + \sqrt{1-\eta}\,c^\dagger\), where \(c\) is the discarded mode, and tracing out \(c\), gives the results of @sec:p-st-loss: coherent states shrink, single photons become a mixture of one and zero photons, squeezing moves towards the vacuum. For a dual-rail qubit, loss takes the state out of the qubit space entirely (no photon in either rail). If the absence is detected, this is a heralded erasure: the code knows which qubit failed, which makes erasures far easier to correct than unknown errors.

The review notes that a photonic quantum circuit must satisfy \(\Pr(\min_{\mathcal P}\eta_{\mathcal P}\ge\eta_{\min})\ge Y_{\min}\) over all paths \(\mathcal P\) (@lf:sec-mod-phot), and the HOM visibility between sources sets a second, independent requirement. A Clements mesh with \(N\) modes has paths of \(N\) MZIs, so per-MZI loss and splitting-ratio statistics compound across the whole mesh.

  • Linear optics is described by a unitary matrix acting on mode operators; the classical S-matrix is also the quantum description.
  • To find output states, substitute \(a_k^\dagger = \sum_jU_{jk}b_j^\dagger\) and expand.
  • Hong–Ou–Mandel: two identical photons at a 50:50 splitter always exit together; the dip in coincidences measures indistinguishability.
  • Any \(N\)-mode unitary needs \(N(N-1)/2\) MZIs (Reck, Clements); boson sampling with such meshes is classically hard.
  • Dual-rail qubits are easy to rotate but hard to entangle; loss is a beam splitter to a discarded mode and makes heralded erasures.

Exercises

  1. Show that a single photon in input 2 of the 50:50 beam splitter leaves as \((\ii\ket{1,0} + \ket{0,1})/\sqrt2\).
    Solution

    From \(a_k^\dagger = \sum_jU_{jk}b_j^\dagger\) with \(U_{12} = \ii/\sqrt2\) and \(U_{22} = 1/\sqrt2\): \(a_2^\dagger\ket0 = (\ii b_1^\dagger + b_2^\dagger)\ket0/\sqrt2 = (\ii\ket{1,0} + \ket{0,1})/\sqrt2\).

  2. Two distinguishable particles (say, a photon and a differently coloured photon) enter the two ports. What is the probability they leave in different outputs?
    Solution

    Each independently transmits or reflects with probability ½. Different outputs happen if both transmit or both reflect: \(\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12\). That is the "classical" level on the HOM plot.

  3. Two photons with Gaussian wavepackets \(\xi(t)\propto\ee^{-t^2/4\sigma^2}\) are delayed by \(\tau\). Show that the overlap is \(\ee^{-\tau^2/8\sigma^2}\) and find the delay that gives \(P_c = 1/4\).
    Solution

    \(\int\xi(t)\xi(t-\tau)\dd t\propto\int\ee^{-(t^2 + (t-\tau)^2)/4\sigma^2}\dd t\); completing the square gives \(\ee^{-\tau^2/8\sigma^2}\). Then \(P_c = \tfrac12(1 - \ee^{-\tau^2/4\sigma^2})\); \(P_c = 1/4\) when \(\ee^{-\tau^2/4\sigma^2} = 1/2\), \(\tau = 2\sigma\sqrt{\ln2}\approx1.67\sigma\).

  4. How many MZIs does a Clements mesh on 20 modes need? If each loses 0.1 dB, what is the transmission of the longest path?
    Solution

    \(20\times19/2 = 190\) MZIs. Longest path 20 MZIs = 2 dB, transmission \(10^{-0.2} = 0.63\).

  5. Two photons in a HOM experiment have visibility \(V = 0.92\). What is \(P_c(0)\)? If they come from two different quantum dots whose frequencies differ, would you expect \(V\) to go up or down?
    Solution

    \(P_c(0) = \tfrac12(1 - 0.92) = 0.04\). Different frequencies make the photons distinguishable, reducing the overlap and hence \(V\) (Chapter 5).
