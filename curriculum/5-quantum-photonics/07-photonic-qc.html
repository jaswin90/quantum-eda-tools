Photonic quantum computing: from KLM to fusion and GKP

Goals

  • Understand why photons are hard to entangle, and how measurement plus feed-forward solves it (KLM).
  • Understand measurement-based computing with cluster states, and how fusion builds large entangled states from small ones.
  • Understand fusion-based quantum computing and how failures become correctable errors.
  • Understand continuous-variable computing with squeezed light and GKP qubits.
  • Build and read a loss budget, and see why the worst path, not the average, decides whether a machine works.

Photons make excellent qubits for storing and moving information, and single-photon gates are easy (a Mach–Zehnder interferometer). The problem is two-qubit gates: photons do not interact, so nothing in linear optics makes one photon's path depend on another's. The way out, discovered in 2001, is that measuring some photons can create entanglement between others, at the price of being probabilistic. Modern architectures turn that into a factory: make small entangled states over and over, stitch them together with measurements, and use error correction to tolerate the failures and the lost photons. In these machines the main enemy is not decoherence but loss, and the design problem is a loss budget across millions of optical paths.

Measurement as the missing interaction

Recall the Hong–Ou–Mandel effect (Chapter 3): two photons at a beam splitter can end up correlated (always in the same output) even though they never interacted. Interference plus detection can create correlations. Knill, Laflamme and Milburn (KLM) turned this into a universal recipe [@knill2001]: interfere the photons carrying the qubits with extra "ancilla" photons, detect some of the outputs, and keep the result only when the detectors show a particular pattern. Measurement is a nonlinear operation (it projects the state), so it supplies what linear optics lacks.

The catch is probability. The simplest heralded CZ gate works only 1 time in 9. KLM showed how to push the success probability towards one with gate teleportation (@b4:sec-i-ent-tele): prepare an entangled resource state offline, by trial and error, and then use it to teleport the gate onto the data. The cost was enormous in photons, but it proved that photons plus detectors plus fast switches are enough for a full quantum computer. Everything since has been about making this efficient.

Cluster states and measurement-based computing

A completely different way to compute, measurement-based quantum computing [@raussendorf2001], fits photons beautifully. First, prepare a large entangled resource called a cluster state: qubits arranged on a lattice, each started in \(\ket+\), with a CZ applied between every pair of neighbours. Then compute by measuring the qubits one at a time, each in a basis chosen depending on earlier results. Remarkably, the right sequence of single-qubit measurements on a large enough cluster implements any quantum circuit.

The cluster state is like a blank sheet of entanglement. The measurements "carve" a circuit into it: measuring a qubit in one basis removes it from the lattice; measuring it in another teleports the logical information to the next qubit while applying a rotation. Measuring left to right moves the computation through the lattice like a wave. All the hard work (entanglement) is done up front, and all the computation is easy single-qubit measurements, which photons are excellent at.

For photons, the difficult part is now building the cluster. Browne and Rudolph showed it can be stitched together from small pieces with fusion [@browne2005]: a two-photon measurement (a type of Bell measurement) on one photon from each of two small clusters, which merges them into one larger cluster. Linear-optical fusion succeeds only half the time (@b4:sec-i-ent-swap), but crucially, a failed fusion is heralded (the detectors show it failed) and leaves the photons in a known state, so failures can be planned around.

Fusion-based quantum computing

Fusion-based quantum computing (FBQC), the architecture behind PsiQuantum's roadmap [@bartolucci2023], works like this:

  1. Resource-state generators produce small entangled states of a few photons (for example six-photon states), one per clock cycle, at many locations on the chip. Each generator itself uses heralded sources, multiplexing and small linear-optical circuits.
  2. Fusions are performed between photons from neighbouring resource states. The pattern of fusions in space and time forms a three-dimensional lattice that implements a topological error-correcting code, a 3D relative of the surface code (@b4:sec-i-qec2).
  3. Fusion outcomes are the syndrome. Each fusion's measurement results play the role of the parity checks in the surface code. A failed fusion or a lost photon becomes a known or unknown error on the code, which the decoder handles.

Every imperfection becomes an error rate that the code must tolerate: fusion failure, photon loss, imperfect indistinguishability (Chapter 3), multi-photon contamination from sources (Chapter 5), detector inefficiency. Below threshold, the logical error rate falls exponentially with the code size, exactly as for the surface code. Published analyses give thresholds of a few percent to around ten percent for photon loss per fusion, depending on the scheme. That sounds generous until you count the components a photon passes through before fusing: sources, multiplexers, delay lines, switches, chip-to-chip links and detectors. Each must lose only a fraction of a percent.

Continuous variables and GKP qubits

A different family encodes information in the quadratures of light (Chapter 2) instead of in single photons. Its building blocks are deterministic: squeezed light is produced on every pulse (no heralding), and beam splitters entangle squeezed modes deterministically. Very large entangled "cluster states" of millions of modes have been made this way, using time-multiplexed fibre loops [@weedbrook2012]. However, Gaussian states and operations alone can be simulated classically, so a non-Gaussian ingredient is needed.

The leading choice is the Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) qubit [@gottesman2001]. Its wavefunction in \(x\) is a comb of narrow peaks spaced \(2\sqrt\pi\) apart; logical 0 lives on the even peaks and logical 1 on the odd peaks (shifted by \(\sqrt\pi\)).

Why a comb? Loss and noise act on a light mode mostly as small random shifts of \(x\) and \(p\). A shift smaller than \(\sqrt\pi/2\) moves a peak a little, but it is still clearly closest to its original position on the comb, so measuring \(x\) modulo \(\sqrt\pi\) reveals the shift and lets it be undone, like reading a ruler and snapping to the nearest mark. Only a shift larger than half the spacing confuses 0 with 1.

Real GKP states have peaks of finite width, quoted as an effective squeezing; fault-tolerant architectures need roughly 10 dB or more after all losses [@bourassa2021]. Because loss drags squeezing towards zero (@sec:p-st-loss), that sets the loss budget computed in the review (@lf:eq-sq): even a 15 dB source tolerates only about 7% total loss. Xanadu's Aurora system demonstrated the building blocks of this architecture at scale [@aghaeerad2025].

Loss budgets

Both architectures reduce to one engineering question: does every photon in the machine survive its path, and arrive indistinguishable from its partners, with probability above the architecture's threshold? A loss budget lists every element on the longest path with its loss in decibels, adds them (losses in dB add because transmissions multiply), and compares the total with the threshold.

Every line is about ten times better than typical classical photonics. And this is the mean. A machine has millions of paths, each built from components drawn from manufacturing distributions, so the tail of the distribution of path losses decides whether the threshold is met everywhere.

Worked example: the worst path. Suppose path losses are Gaussian with mean 0.36 dB and standard deviation 0.05 dB, and the threshold is 0.5 dB. A single path exceeds it when its loss is \((0.5-0.36)/0.05 = 2.8\) standard deviations above the mean, probability about \(2.6\times10^{-3}\). In a machine with \(10^6\) paths, about 2,600 paths fail. Either the mean must come down, the spread must narrow (better process control), or the architecture must detect and route around bad paths, the photonic version of working around dead qubits. Designing for the average would have missed this entirely.

The design target for a photonic quantum computer is a probability statement over a manufactured population: \(\Pr(\text{every path meets its loss and indistinguishability limits})\ge Y\). Classical photonic design kits give component means and corners; quantum design needs calibrated distributions, correlations between components on the same wafer, and a way to verify the condition over millions of paths. That gap, and how to fill it, is where this book hands over to Book 7 and to the review.

  • Photons do not interact; measurement plus feed-forward supplies the nonlinearity (KLM), at the price of probabilistic gates.
  • Measurement-based computing front-loads all entanglement into a cluster state and then computes by single-photon measurements.
  • Fusion-based computing builds the cluster from small resource states with fusions; fusion outcomes act as error-correction syndromes, and failures are heralded.
  • Continuous-variable machines use deterministic squeezing plus GKP qubits, whose comb structure turns small shifts into correctable errors; they need ≳10 dB of squeezing after loss.
  • Loss budgets add dB along each path; with millions of paths, the tail of the loss distribution, not the mean, decides success.

Exercises

  1. A fusion succeeds with probability ½. If a scheme needed 1,000 fusions to all succeed, what would the success probability be? Why do architectures make failures heralded and tolerable instead?
    Solution

    \(2^{-1000}\), effectively zero. Requiring every probabilistic step to succeed is hopeless; architectures treat failures as known erasures in a code that tolerates a fraction of them, so the success probability needs only to beat a threshold.

  2. Using \(V_{\mathrm{out}} = \eta V_{\mathrm{in}} + (1-\eta)\), what loss can a 15 dB squeezer tolerate while keeping 10 dB?
    Solution

    \(V_{\mathrm{in}} = 10^{-1.5} = 0.0316\), target \(V_{\mathrm{out}} = 0.1\). Solve \(0.0316\eta + 1 - \eta = 0.1\): \(\eta = 0.9/0.9684 = 0.929\), about 7% loss.

  3. A GKP qubit's peaks are spaced \(2\sqrt\pi\) apart and logical states differ by a shift of \(\sqrt\pi\). If noise shifts \(x\) by a Gaussian random amount with standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.3\), what is the probability of a logical error (shift larger than \(\sqrt\pi/2\) in either direction)?
    Solution

    \(\sqrt\pi/2 = 0.886\), i.e. \(0.886/0.3 = 2.95\) standard deviations. Two-sided tail \(2(1-\Phi(2.95))\approx3.2\times10^{-3}\). Smaller \(\sigma\) (better squeezing) reduces this rapidly.

  4. Add up the loss budget table and convert to a transmission. Which single line would you attack first, and why?
    Solution

    0.36 dB, transmission \(10^{-0.036} = 0.92\). The two 0.10 dB lines (source extraction and switching) dominate; halving either saves more than eliminating the waveguide loss entirely.

  5. In the worked example, by how much must the standard deviation fall (at the same mean) for the expected number of failing paths in \(10^6\) to drop below 1?
    Solution

    Need a tail probability below \(10^{-6}\), i.e. about 4.75 standard deviations: \(\sigma\le0.14/4.75\approx0.029\) dB. Narrowing the spread from 0.05 to 0.03 dB turns 2,600 failing paths into fewer than one.
