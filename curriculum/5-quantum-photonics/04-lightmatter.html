Light–matter interaction and cavity QED

Goals

  • Derive how a two-level emitter (atom, quantum dot, qubit) couples to one mode of light, and arrive at the Jaynes–Cummings model.
  • Solve the Jaynes–Cummings model: dressed states, vacuum Rabi oscillations and splitting, and the dispersive limit.
  • Understand spontaneous emission as an interaction with vacuum fluctuations, and how a cavity speeds it up or slows it down (Purcell effect).
  • Classify cavity-QED regimes by comparing three rates, and see how it all carries over to superconducting circuits.

Photons barely interact with each other, so to do anything nonlinear with light (make single photons, store them, entangle them) we need matter: an atom, a quantum dot, or a superconducting qubit. The simplest model of light meeting matter is one two-level "atom" coupled to one mode of a cavity. That model, Jaynes–Cummings, is solvable exactly with \(2\times2\) matrices, and it explains a remarkable range of phenomena: why excited atoms decay, how a mirror box can make them decay faster or not at all, how a single atom and a single photon can swap energy back and forth, and how superconducting qubits are read out. It is the bridge from this book to Book 6.

How light couples to an emitter

Take an emitter with two energy levels, ground \(\ket g\) and excited \(\ket e\), separated by \(\hbar\omega_a\) (@b3:sec-q-spin-pauli). Its electrons form a tiny electric dipole that can point in different directions depending on the state. An electric field \(\mathbf E\) has energy \(-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E\) with a dipole \(\mathbf d\). The dipole operator connects the two levels, \(\hat{\mathbf d} = \mathbf d(\sigma_+ + \sigma_-)\), where \(\sigma_+ = \ketbra eg\) raises the emitter and \(\sigma_- = \ketbra ge\) lowers it. Insert the single-mode field operator (@eq:p-efield), \(\hat E\propto\mathcal E(a - a^\dagger)\), and the interaction contains four terms:

  • \(a^\dagger\sigma_-\): the emitter drops down and a photon is created. Emission.
  • \(a\sigma_+\): a photon is destroyed and the emitter goes up. Absorption.
  • \(a\sigma_-\) and \(a^\dagger\sigma_+\): the emitter drops and a photon is destroyed, or both go up. These do not conserve energy; in the interaction picture they oscillate at \(\omega + \omega_a\), very fast.

Averaged over a few cycles, the fast terms contribute almost nothing, so we drop them: the rotating-wave approximation, valid when the coupling is much smaller than the frequencies (@b3:sec-q-spin-drive). The result is the Jaynes–Cummings Hamiltonian [@jaynes1963]:

$$ H_{\mathrm{JC}} = \hbar\omega_r\,a^\dagger a + \frac{\hbar\omega_a}{2}\sigma_z + \hbar g\big(a^\dagger\sigma_- + a\sigma_+\big), \qquad \hbar g = \mathbf d\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathcal E}(\mathbf r_a) . $$

Three terms: the cavity mode (frequency \(\omega_r\)), the emitter (frequency \(\omega_a\)) and their exchange of energy at rate \(g\). The coupling \(g\) is the dipole times the single-photon field at the emitter's position, which is why small mode volumes and well-placed emitters matter (Chapter 1).

Solving it: dressed states

The coupling only connects \(\ket{e, n}\) (emitter excited, \(n\) photons) with \(\ket{g, n+1}\) (emitter in ground state, one more photon): the total number of excitations is conserved. So the Hamiltonian splits into separate \(2\times2\) blocks, one per excitation number. Each block is

\[ \hbar\begin{pmatrix} \omega_rn + \omega_a/2 & g\sqrt{n+1}\\ g\sqrt{n+1} & \omega_r(n+1) - \omega_a/2\end{pmatrix}, \]

where \(\sqrt{n+1}\) is the matrix element of \(a^\dagger\) (@b3:eq-q-fock). This is exactly the coupled-mode matrix whose eigenvalues we found in Book 1 (@b1:sec-m-op-eigen), with coupling \(g\sqrt{n+1}\) and detuning \(\Delta = \omega_a - \omega_r\). The eigenvalues are

$$ E_{n,\pm} = \hbar\omega_r\Big(n+\frac12\Big) \pm \frac\hbar2\sqrt{\Delta^2 + 4g^2(n+1)} . $$

The eigenstates are superpositions of "excited emitter" and "one more photon", called dressed states: neither purely light nor purely matter.

On resonance (\(\Delta = 0\)) with one excitation, the two dressed states are \((\ket{e,0}\pm\ket{g,1})/\sqrt2\), split by \(2\hbar g\). If we start with an excited emitter and an empty cavity, the excitation swaps back and forth: emitter → photon → emitter, at frequency \(2g\). This is vacuum Rabi oscillation: the emitter gives its energy to the empty cavity and takes it back. In a spectrum, the single cavity line splits into two lines separated by \(2g\): vacuum Rabi splitting. Because the splitting grows as \(\sqrt{n+1}\), the ladder of levels is not evenly spaced. That anharmonicity is a direct signature that the light is quantised, and it is what lets a single emitter make a single photon behave nonlinearly.

Far from resonance (\(|\Delta|\gg g\sqrt n\)), the emitter and photon barely mix. Second-order perturbation theory gives the dispersive Hamiltonian of Book 3 (@b3:eq-q-dispersive), with \(\chi = g^2/\Delta\): the cavity frequency shifts by \(\pm\chi\) depending on the emitter's state, and the emitter's frequency shifts by \(2\chi\) per photon. This is how superconducting qubits are read out (Book 6).

Spontaneous emission and the Purcell effect

In empty space an excited atom decays in nanoseconds, emitting a photon in a random direction. In Jaynes–Cummings language: the atom couples to the vacuum fluctuations of every mode of free space, a continuum, and Fermi's golden rule (@b3:eq-q-golden) turns that into a decay rate:

$$ \Gamma_0 = \frac{\omega_a^3|\mathbf d|^2}{3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3} . $$

The \(\omega^3\) explains why optical transitions decay in nanoseconds, while a microwave transition in free space would take years: there are far more modes per unit frequency at high frequency, and each carries a stronger vacuum field.

The key insight is that spontaneous emission is not a property of the emitter alone; it depends on the electromagnetic environment, through the density of available modes. Put the emitter in a cavity resonant with it, of quality factor \(Q\) and mode volume \(V\), and the modes are concentrated into the cavity line. The emission rate is multiplied by the Purcell factor [@purcell1946]:

$$ F_P = \frac{3}{4\pi^2}\Big(\frac{\lambda}{n}\Big)^3\frac{Q}{V}, \qquad \Gamma_{\mathrm{cav}} = \frac{4g^2}{\kappa}\ \ (\text{on resonance, } g\ll\kappa). $$

High \(Q\) and small \(V\) give large enhancement. Off resonance the cavity has fewer modes than free space at the emitter's frequency, and emission is suppressed. Both effects are used:

  • Enhancement makes single-photon sources fast and sends their photons into one well-defined mode that can be collected into a fibre (Chapter 5).
  • Suppression protects superconducting qubits: a qubit detuned from its readout resonator decays through it only at the reduced rate \(\kappa(g/\Delta)^2\), and extra filters reduce this further (@lf:eq-chi, @b6:sec-h-cqed).

Worked example: Purcell enhancement of a quantum dot. A micropillar at 930 nm (\(n\approx3.5\) in GaAs) with \(Q = 2000\) and \(V = 2(\lambda/n)^3\). Then \(F_P = \frac{3}{4\pi^2}\times\frac{2000}{2} = 76\). A dot with free-space lifetime 1 ns would, on resonance, emit about 76 times faster, in about 13 ps, mostly into the cavity mode. Fast emission also helps indistinguishability, because the photon leaves before the dot's environment can disturb its phase.

Regimes of cavity QED

Three rates compete: the coupling \(g\) (emitter ↔ cavity), the cavity loss rate \(\kappa\) (photon escapes), and the emitter's loss rate \(\gamma\) into everything else (other modes, phonons, dephasing). Two derived quantities organise everything:

  • The cooperativity \(C = 4g^2/\kappa\gamma\): how much more likely the emitter is to interact with the cavity mode than with everything else. The fraction of emission that goes into the cavity mode is \(C/(C+1)\) in the bad-cavity regime.
  • The comparison of \(g\) with \(\kappa\) and \(\gamma\):
    • Weak coupling (\(g \lt \kappa\) or \(\gamma\)): the photon leaves before it can be reabsorbed. The cavity just changes emission rates (Purcell). With \(C\gg1\) this already gives efficient single-photon sources.
    • Strong coupling (\(g > \kappa, \gamma\)): emitter and photon swap energy many times before anything is lost; vacuum Rabi splitting is resolved. Atoms in high-finesse optical cavities, quantum dots in photonic-crystal cavities, and above all superconducting circuits reach this regime; circuit QED reaches \(g/\kappa\) of hundreds [@wallraff2004; @blais2004].

From cavity QED to circuit QED

Replace the atom by a transmon qubit and the optical cavity by a superconducting coplanar resonator: @eq:p-jc describes the device, with one correction (the transmon has a third level not far above, Book 6). The coupling is set by a capacitance: the transmon's charge couples to the resonator's voltage through the coupling capacitor \(C_g\), so

\[ \hbar g\approx\frac{C_g}{C_\Sigma}\,2e\,V_{\mathrm{zpf}}\,\bra1\hat n\ket0 , \]

with \(V_{\mathrm{zpf}}\) the resonator's zero-point voltage (@b3:eq-q-lc) and \(\bra1\hat n\ket0\) the transmon's charge matrix element. Every factor except the last is set by drawn geometry. The dispersive shift becomes the readout mechanism, the Purcell effect the readout's cost, and the resonator's \(\kappa\) the readout speed. Book 6 develops this; the review's @lf:eq-multi and @lf:eq-chi are this chapter's equations with the transmon's levels included.

Light–matter coupling is geometric: \(g\) depends on where the emitter sits in the mode and how the mode is shaped. For quantum-dot sources the dot's position is random relative to the lithographed cavity, so coupling becomes a yield problem (@lf:sec-mod-phot). For transmons, \(g\) is set by drawn capacitances and is predictable to a few percent, provided the electromagnetic extraction is accurate.

  • Dipole coupling plus the rotating-wave approximation gives the Jaynes–Cummings model: cavity + two-level emitter + exchange at rate \(g = d\mathcal E/\hbar\).
  • It splits into \(2\times2\) blocks: on resonance, energy swaps at \(2g\) (vacuum Rabi); far off resonance, dispersive shifts \(\pm g^2/\Delta\).
  • Spontaneous emission comes from vacuum fluctuations; a cavity enhances it on resonance (Purcell factor \(\propto Q/V\)) and suppresses it off resonance.
  • Cooperativity \(C = 4g^2/\kappa\gamma\) and the comparison of \(g\) with \(\kappa,\gamma\) define the regimes; circuit QED is deep in strong coupling.

Exercises

  1. Verify that \(H_{\mathrm{JC}}\) commutes with the excitation number \(N = a^\dagger a + \sigma_+\sigma_-\).
    Solution

    \([a^\dagger\sigma_-, N] = [a^\dagger,a^\dagger a]\sigma_- + a^\dagger[\sigma_-,\sigma_+\sigma_-] = -a^\dagger\sigma_- + a^\dagger\sigma_- = 0\), using \([a^\dagger, a^\dagger a] = -a^\dagger\) and \([\sigma_-,\sigma_+\sigma_-] = \sigma_-\). The Hermitian conjugate term commutes likewise.

  2. On resonance, an excited emitter starts with an empty cavity. Write the state at time \(t\) and find when the photon is certainly in the cavity.
    Solution

    In the one-excitation block, \(\ket{e,0}\to\cos(gt)\ket{e,0} - \ii\sin(gt)\ket{g,1}\). The photon is certainly in the cavity at \(gt = \pi/2\), i.e. \(t = \pi/2g\).

  3. A quantum dot in a micropillar has \(g/2\pi = 20\) GHz, \(\kappa/2\pi = 100\) GHz and \(\gamma/2\pi = 0.2\) GHz. Which regime is this, what is \(C\), and what fraction of emission goes into the cavity mode?
    Solution

    \(g \lt \kappa\): weak coupling (bad-cavity regime). \(C = 4\times400/(100\times0.2) = 80\). Fraction into the cavity \(\approx C/(C+1) = 0.99\). (Collection from the cavity into a fibre is a further efficiency.)

  4. For a transmon–resonator system with \(g/2\pi = 80\) MHz on resonance, how long does a single excitation take to swap from qubit to resonator?
    Solution

    \(\pi/2g = 1/(4\times80\,\mathrm{MHz}) = 3.1\) ns.

  5. By what factor does the free-space spontaneous emission rate change between a 1550 nm transition and a 5 GHz transition with the same dipole moment?
    Solution

    \(\Gamma_0\propto\omega^3\). Frequency ratio \(1.93\times10^{14}/5\times10^9 = 3.9\times10^4\); cubed, \(5.9\times10^{13}\). A microwave transition is fourteen orders of magnitude slower to decay into free space, which is why superconducting qubits lose energy mainly through materials and circuits, not by radiating.
