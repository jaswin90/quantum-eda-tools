Open quantum systems and decoherence

Goals

  • Describe noisy evolution with quantum channels and Kraus operators.
  • Write and solve the Lindblad master equation for relaxation and dephasing, and define \(T_1\), \(T_\varphi\), \(T_2\) and \(T_2^*\).
  • Relate decoherence rates to noise spectral densities, and see how spin echo and dynamical decoupling filter low-frequency noise.
  • Know when the simple (Markovian) picture fails.

Every qubit forgets. An excited qubit drops to its ground state after a while (energy relaxation, time \(T_1\)), and a superposition loses its phase even sooner (dephasing, time \(T_2\)). These two numbers are on every qubit's datasheet, and they set how many operations can be done before information is lost. This chapter explains what they mean mathematically (the density matrix shrinking toward a pole or the axis of the Bloch sphere), how to describe them (quantum channels and the Lindblad equation), where they come from physically (noise at particular frequencies), and how clever pulse sequences undo part of the damage. This is the language in which hardware quality is measured and in which error-correction models are built.

Quantum channels

A qubit is never truly isolated. It interacts with an environment (the substrate, the wiring, stray two-level defects), and if we follow the joint evolution and then trace out the environment (Chapter 7), the qubit's own state changes by a quantum channel: a linear map \(\rho\mapsto\mathcal E(\rho)\) that always produces a valid density matrix, even when the qubit is part of a larger entangled state (a property called complete positivity). Every such map can be written in Kraus form:

$$ \mathcal E(\rho) = \sum_k K_k\,\rho\,K_k^\dagger, \qquad \sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k = I . $$

One way to read it: "with some probability, the operation \(K_k\) happens". The condition \(\sum K_k^\dagger K_k = I\) keeps the trace equal to 1. A unitary gate is the special case of a single Kraus operator \(K_0 = U\).

Three channels describe most qubit noise:

  • Amplitude damping (energy loss with probability \(\gamma\)): \[ K_0 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad K_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt\gamma\\0&0\end{pmatrix}. \] \(K_1\) is the "jump": it takes \(\ket1\to\ket0\), emitting the energy into the environment. \(K_0\) is the "no jump" case, and it still shrinks the excited amplitude: not seeing a photon emitted makes the ground state slightly more likely. Repeated, it drives every state to \(\ket0\), the north pole.
  • Phase damping (loss of phase with probability \(p\)): \(K_0 = \sqrt{1-p}\,I\), \(K_1 = \sqrt p\,Z\). With probability \(p\) the qubit suffers a random \(Z\) (a phase flip). Populations are untouched; coherences are multiplied by \(1-2p\). The Bloch vector shrinks toward the \(z\) axis.
  • Depolarising noise: \(\rho\mapsto(1-p)\rho + p\,I/2\). With probability \(p\) the qubit is replaced by a completely random state. The Bloch vector shrinks uniformly toward the centre.

Book 4 builds error models for whole processors from these.

The Lindblad master equation

Channels describe a finite step. For continuous time we want a differential equation for \(\rho(t)\). When the environment is large, has a short memory compared with the qubit's dynamics (the Markov approximation: each moment's noise is independent of the past), and couples weakly, the evolution always takes the universal Lindblad form [@lindblad1976; @breuer2002]:

$$ \frac{\dd\rho}{\dd t} = -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}[H,\rho] + \sum_k\gamma_k\Big(L_k\rho L_k^\dagger - \frac12\big\{L_k^\dagger L_k, \rho\big\}\Big), $$

where \(\{A,B\} = AB+BA\). The first term is ordinary unitary evolution. Each jump operator \(L_k\), with rate \(\gamma_k\), describes one dissipative process: \(L_k\rho L_k^\dagger\) is the jump happening, and the anticommutator term is the "no-jump" back-action that keeps the trace at 1.

For a qubit, three processes cover nearly everything:

Solving the Lindblad equation for the matrix elements (exercise 3) gives the Bloch equations:

$$ \rho_{11}(t) = \rho_{11}(0)\,\ee^{-t/T_1}, \qquad \rho_{01}(t) = \rho_{01}(0)\,\ee^{-t/T_2}\,\ee^{\ii\omega_qt}, \qquad \frac1{T_2} = \frac1{2T_1} + \frac1{T_\varphi} $$

(at zero temperature, in the frame where \(\ket0\) is the ground state). In words:

  • Populations decay at rate \(1/T_1\): an excited qubit relaxes exponentially.
  • Coherences decay at rate \(1/T_2\) while precessing at \(\omega_q\).
  • Energy relaxation necessarily destroys coherence too: if the qubit jumps to \(\ket0\), its phase is gone. That contributes \(1/2T_1\) to the coherence decay (the \(\tfrac12\) because coherence is an amplitude and populations are amplitudes squared). Hence the fundamental bound \(T_2\le2T_1\).
  • Pure dephasing (\(T_\varphi\)) destroys coherence without exchanging energy: the qubit frequency jitters and the phase smears out.

Worked example: what the numbers mean. A transmon with \(T_1 = 100\) µs and \(T_2 = 120\) µs, running gates of 25 ns. During one gate the excited population decays by \(1-\ee^{-0.025/100}\approx2.5\times10^{-4}\), and coherence by \(1-\ee^{-0.025/120}\approx2.1\times10^{-4}\). So decoherence alone limits gate error to a few parts in \(10^4\), about \(10^{4}\) gates before information is mostly lost. Error correction (Book 4) needs physical errors well below 1%, which this satisfies with margin; larger computations need longer coherence or faster gates.

For a damped harmonic oscillator, the jump operator \(L = a\) at rate \(\kappa\) gives \(\dd\expval{a}/\dd t = -(\ii\omega+\kappa/2)\expval a\): the quantum version of the classical damped amplitude, consistent with Book 2's input–output equations (@b2:eq-c-inout).

Where the rates come from

The Lindblad rates are not fundamental constants: they are set by the noise spectrum of whatever couples to the qubit. Write the qubit–environment coupling as

\[ H_{\mathrm{int}} = \tfrac\hbar2\big(\lambda_\perp(t)X + \lambda_\parallel(t)Z\big), \]

with transverse noise \(\lambda_\perp\) (it can flip the qubit) and longitudinal noise \(\lambda_\parallel\) (it shifts the qubit's frequency). Then, using the golden rule of Chapter 6 (@eq:q-gamma1),

$$ \Gamma_1 = \tfrac14\big[S_{\perp}(\omega_q) + S_\perp(-\omega_q)\big], \qquad \Gamma_\varphi = \tfrac12 S_{\parallel}(\omega\to0) \quad\text{(white noise)} . $$

Relaxation samples noise at the qubit frequency; dephasing samples it near zero frequency. Different physical mechanisms dominate each:

  • A transmon's \(T_1\) is set by dielectric loss and two-level systems in surfaces, quasiparticles, and radiation into lines and package (@lf:eq-t1).
  • Its \(T_\varphi\) is set by flux noise (if it is tunable), residual thermal photons in the readout resonator (@lf:eq-dephase), and charge noise.

Low-frequency noise is usually not white but \(1/f\)-like (Book 2, Chapter 8), and then the decay is not exponential. Here is the clean way to think about it. If the qubit frequency fluctuates by \(\delta\omega(t)\), a superposition accumulates a random phase \(\varphi(\tau) = \int_0^\tau\delta\omega\,\dd t\). The coherence is the average \(\expval{\ee^{\ii\varphi}}\), which for Gaussian noise is \(\ee^{-\expval{\varphi^2}/2}\). In the frequency domain,

$$ \expval{\varphi^2} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int S_{\delta\omega}(\omega)\,\frac{|F(\omega\tau)|^2}{\omega^2}\,\dd\omega , $$

where \(F\) is the filter function of the pulse sequence: it says which noise frequencies the experiment is sensitive to.

  • For a plain Ramsey experiment, \(|F|^2 = 4\sin^2(\omega\tau/2)\), which passes low frequencies. Slow drifts dominate, and the decay time is the short \(T_2^*\). If the frequency offset is simply random from shot to shot with rms \(\sigma\) ("quasi-static" noise), the decay is Gaussian, \(\ee^{-\sigma^2\tau^2/2}\).
  • A spin echo (a \(\pi\) pulse halfway through) flips the qubit, so phase accumulated in the second half has the opposite sign and cancels the first half's. Static and slow fluctuations cancel: the filter function vanishes as \(\omega\to0\), and the decay time \(T_{2E}\) is longer.
  • Sequences of many \(\pi\) pulses (CPMG, dynamical decoupling) push the filter's passband to higher frequency. Varying the pulse spacing scans the passband across frequencies and maps out the noise spectrum: the qubit becomes a spectrometer.

The spread between \(T_2^*\), \(T_{2E}\) and \(2T_1\) tells an experimentalist what kind of noise limits a device (@fig:q-decoh).

A qubit has \(T_2^* = 20\) µs, \(T_{2E} = 150\) µs and \(T_1 = 80\) µs. What does this tell you?

Answer

\(T_{2E}\) is close to \(2T_1 = 160\) µs, so once slow noise is echoed away the qubit is nearly relaxation-limited. The big gap to \(T_2^*\) means slow frequency noise (drift, \(1/f\) flux or charge noise) dominates Ramsey dephasing. Improve the low-frequency noise environment, or use echoes and dynamical decoupling.

Beyond the Lindblad picture

The Markov approximation fails when the environment has a memory comparable to the dynamics. Examples: a strongly coupled two-level defect, \(1/f\) noise, a coupler mode close to a qubit, or a qubit near a structured filter. Non-Markovian effects show up as non-exponential decays, revivals (lost coherence coming back), and \(T_1\) that fluctuates from hour to hour as defects drift in frequency.

They matter for error correction because they create errors correlated in time and space, which the simple independent-error Pauli models of Book 4 do not capture. A cosmic-ray impact, for example, creates quasiparticles across a whole chip at once. The practical fix for most cases: treat the near environment (a resonator, a coupler, a strongly coupled defect) as part of the system, and only the far environment as a bath. That usually restores the Lindblad form for the enlarged system.

Try it in Python

Solve the Lindblad equation exactly (by turning it into a matrix acting on the stacked entries of \(\rho\)) and check the Bloch equations; then show how an echo cancels slow frequency noise that kills a Ramsey experiment.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm

T1, Tphi = 100.0, 300.0                    # microseconds
sm = np.array([[0, 1], [0, 0]], complex)    # sigma_minus = |0><1|
Z = np.diag([1.0, -1.0]).astype(complex)
I = np.eye(2)

def lindblad_superop(H, jumps):
    """Matrix L such that d vec(rho)/dt = L vec(rho) (column-stacking vec)."""
    L = -1j * (np.kron(I, H) - np.kron(H.T, I))
    for rate, J in jumps:
        JdJ = J.conj().T @ J
        L += rate * (np.kron(J.conj(), J) - 0.5 * np.kron(I, JdJ) - 0.5 * np.kron(JdJ.T, I))
    return L

Lsup = lindblad_superop(np.zeros((2, 2)), [(1 / T1, sm), (1 / (2 * Tphi), Z)])
vec = lambda r: r.reshape(-1, order="F")
unvec = lambda v: v.reshape(2, 2, order="F")

plus = np.full((2, 2), 0.5, complex)        # |+><+|
one = np.diag([0, 1]).astype(complex)        # |1><1|
for t in (0, 50, 100, 200):
    P = expm(Lsup * t)
    p1 = unvec(P @ vec(one))[1, 1].real
    coh = abs(unvec(P @ vec(plus))[0, 1])
    print(f"t = {t:3d} us: excited population {p1:.3f} (exp(-t/T1) = {np.exp(-t/T1):.3f}), "
          f"coherence {coh:.3f} (0.5 exp(-t/T2) = {0.5*np.exp(-t*(1/(2*T1)+1/Tphi)):.3f})")

# Quasi-static frequency noise: Ramsey versus echo, averaged over many shots
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
sigma = 2 * np.pi * 5e-3                    # 5 kHz rms, in rad/us
dw1 = rng.normal(0, sigma, 20000)           # frequency offset in each shot ...
dw2 = dw1 + rng.normal(0, 0.1 * sigma, 20000)  # ... drifting a little by the second half
for tau in (20, 45, 90):
    ramsey = abs(np.mean(np.exp(1j * (dw1 + dw2) * tau / 2)))
    echo = abs(np.mean(np.exp(1j * (dw1 - dw2) * tau / 2)))   # pi pulse flips the sign
    print(f"tau = {tau} us: Ramsey coherence {ramsey:.3f} (theory {np.exp(-(sigma*tau)**2/2):.3f}), echo {echo:.3f}")

The Lindblad solution reproduces \(\ee^{-t/T_1}\) and \(\ee^{-t/T_2}\) with \(1/T_2 = 1/2T_1 + 1/T_\varphi\). With 5 kHz of slow noise the Ramsey coherence falls to \(1/\ee\) at 45 µs (exercise 4), while the echo, which cancels everything except the small drift between its two halves, stays above 0.99.

Coherence is the end product that a design and fabrication process delivers. The chain from layout (participation ratios), materials (loss tangents) and circuit (Purcell and line coupling) through @eq:q-rates to \(T_1\) and \(T_2\), and from there to gate error and logical error (@lf:eq-eps), is the chain the review argues a quantum EDA system must automate.

  • Noisy evolution is a channel \(\sum K\rho K^\dagger\); amplitude damping, phase damping and depolarising cover most qubit noise.
  • Lindblad equation: Hamiltonian part plus jump operators with rates.
  • Populations decay at \(1/T_1\); coherences at \(1/T_2 = 1/2T_1 + 1/T_\varphi\); \(T_2\le2T_1\).
  • \(T_1\) samples noise at \(\omega_q\); dephasing samples low frequencies.
  • Filter functions: Ramsey sees slow noise (\(T_2^*\)); echoes and dynamical decoupling cancel it.
  • Non-Markovian and correlated noise falls outside the simple picture and matters for error correction.

Exercises

  1. Apply the amplitude-damping channel to \(\ket+\) and find the new Bloch vector.
    Solution

    \(\rho = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\). \(K_0\rho K_0^\dagger = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\\ \sqrt{1-\gamma}&1-\gamma\end{pmatrix}\), \(K_1\rho K_1^\dagger = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\). Sum: \(\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1+\gamma&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\\ \sqrt{1-\gamma}&1-\gamma\end{pmatrix}\). Bloch vector \((\sqrt{1-\gamma}, 0, \gamma)\): coherence shrinks as \(\sqrt{1-\gamma}\) while the vector moves toward \(\ket0\).

  2. A qubit has \(T_1 = 80\) µs and \(T_2 = 60\) µs (echo). What is \(T_\varphi\)?
    Solution

    \(1/T_\varphi = 1/T_2 - 1/2T_1 = 1/60 - 1/160 = 0.01042\) µs\(^{-1}\), so \(T_\varphi\approx96\) µs.

  3. Show from the Lindblad equation with \(L = \sigma_-\) that \(\dot\rho_{11} = -\Gamma_1\rho_{11}\).
    Solution

    \(\sigma_-\rho\sigma_+\) has \((0,0)\) element \(\rho_{11}\) and \((1,1)\) element 0. \(\sigma_+\sigma_- = \ketbra11\), so \(\{\sigma_+\sigma_-,\rho\}_{11} = 2\rho_{11}\). Hence \(\dot\rho_{11} = \Gamma_1(0 - \rho_{11})\).

  4. Quasi-static Gaussian frequency noise with rms \(\sigma\) (in rad/s) gives Ramsey coherence \(\ee^{-\sigma^2\tau^2/2}\). For \(\sigma/2\pi = 5\) kHz, what is the \(1/\ee\) time? Why does an echo remove this decay?
    Solution

    \(\sigma^2\tau^2/2 = 1\) gives \(\tau = \sqrt2/\sigma = \sqrt2/(2\pi\times5000)\approx45\) µs. In an echo the phase accumulated in the first half, \(\delta\omega\tau/2\), is cancelled by \(-\delta\omega\tau/2\) in the second half when \(\delta\omega\) is constant during the sequence.

  5. Apply the phase-damping channel with probability \(p\) to \(\ket+\). What is the new Bloch vector, and after how many applications with \(p = 0.01\) has the coherence halved?
    Solution

    Bloch vector \((1-2p, 0, 0)\). After \(n\) steps, \((1-2p)^n = 0.98^n = 0.5\) at \(n\approx34\).

  6. A qubit is measured to have \(T_2 = 250\) µs and \(T_1 = 100\) µs. What is wrong?
    Solution

    \(T_2\le2T_1 = 200\) µs is a hard bound. Either one of the fits is wrong, or \(T_1\) fluctuated between the two measurements (common when two-level defects drift), so they were not measured under the same conditions.
