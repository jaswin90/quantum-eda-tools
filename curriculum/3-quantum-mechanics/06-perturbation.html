Approximation methods: perturbation theory and effective Hamiltonians

Goals

  • Compute first- and second-order energy shifts, and handle degenerate levels.
  • Derive effective Hamiltonians by eliminating far-detuned states (the Schrieffer–Wolff idea), including the dispersive shift.
  • Use time-dependent perturbation theory and Fermi's golden rule to compute transition rates from a noise spectrum.
  • Know the variational principle and when numerical diagonalisation is better.

Non-degenerate perturbation theory

Few Hamiltonians can be diagonalised exactly. Perturbation theory treats \(H = H_0 + \lambda V\), where \(H_0\) has known eigenstates \(\ket{n}\) with energies \(E_n\), and \(\lambda V\) is small. Expanding eigenvalues and eigenvectors in powers of \(\lambda\) and matching orders gives, for a non-degenerate level,

$$ E_n' = E_n + \bra nV\ket n + \sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|\bra mV\ket n|^2}{E_n - E_m} + O(V^3), \qquad \ket{n'} = \ket n + \sum_{m\ne n}\frac{\bra mV\ket n}{E_n-E_m}\ket m + \dots $$

(setting \(\lambda=1\)). The first-order shift is the average of the perturbation in the unperturbed state. The second-order shift is a sum over virtual transitions to other levels, each weighted by its matrix element squared over the energy denominator. Two rules of thumb follow. Levels repel: a level is pushed down by everything above it and up by everything below it. And the expansion is valid only when \(|\bra mV\ket n|\ll|E_n-E_m|\); when two coupled levels approach each other, the second-order formula diverges and must be replaced by exact diagonalisation of the nearly degenerate pair. You saw exactly this structure in the avoided crossing of @b1:sec-m-op-eigen.

Degenerate levels

If several states share an energy, the denominators vanish. The fix is to first diagonalise \(V\) within the degenerate subspace: the "right" zeroth-order states are those in which \(V\) is diagonal, and the first-order shifts are its eigenvalues there. Two identical qubits coupled by \(g(\sigma_+\sigma_-' + \sigma_-\sigma_+')\) have degenerate states \(\ket{01}\) and \(\ket{10}\); the coupling splits them into \((\ket{01}\pm\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) at \(\pm g\). That splitting is what an iSWAP-type gate uses.

Effective Hamiltonians and the dispersive regime

Often we care only about a low-energy subspace (the two qubit levels, or the states with no excitation in a resonator), and other states are far away. We want an effective Hamiltonian acting only on the subspace that includes, to second order, the effect of virtual excursions outside it. The systematic method is the Schrieffer–Wolff transformation [@schrieffer1966; @bravyi2011sw]: find an anti-Hermitian \(S\) such that \(\ee^{S}H\ee^{-S}\) has no matrix elements between the subspace and the rest, to the desired order. Using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff expansion (@b1:eq-m-bch), if \(V\) couples the subspaces and \([S, H_0] = -V\), then

$$ H_{\mathrm{eff}} = H_0 + \tfrac12[S, V] + O(V^3) . $$

The result is the matrix version of the second-order formula in @eq:q-pt.

Worked example: the dispersive shift. A qubit (frequency \(\omega_q\)) couples to a resonator (frequency \(\omega_r\)) by the Jaynes–Cummings interaction \(g(a^\dagger\sigma_- + a\sigma_+)\), and they are far detuned, \(|\Delta| = |\omega_q-\omega_r|\gg g\). The state \(\ket{1_q, n}\) (qubit excited, \(n\) photons) couples to \(\ket{0_q, n+1}\) with matrix element \(g\sqrt{n+1}\) and energy difference \(\hbar\Delta\); the state \(\ket{0_q,n}\) couples to \(\ket{1_q, n-1}\) with \(g\sqrt n\) and difference \(-\hbar\Delta\). Second-order shifts are \(+\hbar g^2(n+1)/\Delta\) and \(-\hbar g^2n/\Delta\) respectively. Collecting terms,

$$ H_{\mathrm{eff}} = \hbar\Big(\omega_r + \chi\,\sigma_z\Big)a^\dagger a + \frac{\hbar}{2}\big(\omega_q + \chi\big)\sigma_z, \qquad \chi = \frac{g^2}{\Delta}, $$

with \(\sigma_z = +1\) for the excited qubit. The resonator frequency depends on the qubit state: \(\omega_r\pm\chi\). Probing the resonator therefore measures the qubit without exchanging energy with it, the basis of dispersive readout. The qubit frequency also depends on the photon number, \(\omega_q + 2\chi n\) (the AC Stark shift), so photons in the resonator dephase the qubit. For a transmon, whose third level is close, the same calculation including that level gives the review's @lf:eq-chi, with the factor \(\alpha/(\Delta+\alpha)\).

Time-dependent perturbation theory

Now let a weak perturbation \(V(t)\) act on a system initially in \(\ket i\). In the interaction picture, to first order, the amplitude to find the system in \(\ket f\) at time \(t\) is

$$ c_f(t) = -\frac{\ii}{\hbar}\int_0^t \bra fV(t')\ket i\,\ee^{\ii\omega_{fi}t'}\,\dd t', \qquad \omega_{fi} = \frac{E_f - E_i}{\hbar} . $$

The transition probability \(|c_f|^2\) is the squared Fourier transform of the perturbation at the transition frequency. A perturbation with no spectral content at \(\omega_{fi}\) cannot drive the transition, which is why pulse spectra matter (@b1:sec-m-fourier-transform).

Fermi's golden rule and noise-induced transitions

If the final states form a continuum (a lossy line, the phonons of a crystal, the modes of free space), the probability grows linearly in time and defines a rate. For a constant coupling to a continuum of density \(\rho(E_f)\):

$$ \Gamma_{i\to f} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|\bra fV\ket i|^2\,\rho(E_f) . $$

An equivalent form is more useful for engineering. Suppose a qubit couples to a fluctuating classical or quantum quantity \(\lambda(t)\) through \(V = \hbar\lambda(t)\,X/2\) (transverse noise). Averaging \(|c_f|^2\) over the noise, using the autocorrelation function and the Wiener–Khinchin theorem (@b1:eq-m-psd), gives

$$ \Gamma_{\downarrow} = \frac14\,S_\lambda(+\omega_q), \qquad \Gamma_{\uparrow} = \frac14\,S_\lambda(-\omega_q), $$

with \(S_\lambda\) the two-sided spectral density (quantum noise distinguishes positive and negative frequencies: the environment can absorb energy from the qubit or give energy to it). The qubit is a spectrum analyser: its relaxation rate measures the noise at its own frequency. For a quantum environment in equilibrium, \(S(-\omega)/S(\omega) = \ee^{-\hbar\omega/k_BT}\), which guarantees that the qubit relaxes to the Boltzmann distribution. Combined with the fluctuation–dissipation theorem (@b2:sec-c-th-johnson), @eq:q-gamma1 lets a classical circuit simulation predict \(T_1\): a qubit of capacitance \(C\) seeing an admittance \(Y(\omega)\) relaxes at \(\Gamma_1\approx\Re Y(\omega_q)/C\). Purcell decay through a readout resonator and loss through a drive line are both computed this way.

The variational method and numerics

For any normalised trial state, \(\bra\psi H\ket\psi\ge E_0\). Minimising over a family of trial states gives an upper bound on the ground-state energy that is often accurate. The variational principle underlies the variational quantum eigensolver of Book 4 and many computational chemistry methods. For small systems, though, the most reliable method is direct: represent \(H\) in a truncated basis (Fock states for oscillators, charge states for a transmon) and diagonalise numerically. This is what scqubits and similar packages do, and it is exact up to truncation error, which can be checked by enlarging the basis.

Effective Hamiltonians are the language in which device parameters are specified: \(\omega_q\), \(\alpha\), \(g\), \(\chi\) and \(\zeta\) are all coefficients of a Schrieffer–Wolff expansion. Their formulas (@lf:eq-chi, @lf:eq-zz) assume particular regimes; a design tool that applies them outside their validity (for example near a collision, where denominators are small) gives wrong answers silently. Checking against exact diagonalisation is part of sign-off.

Exercises

  1. For \(H = \begin{pmatrix}0 & g\\ g & \Delta\end{pmatrix}\) with \(g\ll\Delta\), compute the second-order shift of the lower level and compare with the exact eigenvalue.
    Solution

    Second order: \(g^2/(0-\Delta) = -g^2/\Delta\). Exact: \(\tfrac\Delta2 - \tfrac12\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}\approx -g^2/\Delta + g^4/\Delta^3\). They agree to leading order.

  2. Evaluate \(\chi = g^2/\Delta\) for \(g/2\pi = 100\) MHz and \(\Delta/2\pi = -1.5\) GHz, and the transmon-corrected value with \(\alpha/2\pi = -250\) MHz using \(\chi = \frac{g^2}{\Delta}\frac{\alpha}{\Delta+\alpha}\).
    Solution

    Two-level: \(10^4/(-1500) = -6.7\) MHz. Transmon: multiply by \(\alpha/(\Delta+\alpha) = -250/(-1750) = 0.143\), giving \(-0.95\) MHz. The third level reduces the dispersive shift sevenfold; using the two-level formula for a transmon is a common mistake.

  3. A constant perturbation \(V\) is switched on at \(t=0\). Using @eq:q-tdpt, show \(|c_f(t)|^2 = \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\frac{\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)}{\omega_{fi}^2}\).
    Solution

    \(c_f = -\frac{\ii V_{fi}}{\hbar}\frac{\ee^{\ii\omega_{fi}t}-1}{\ii\omega_{fi}}\), and \(|\ee^{\ii\omega t}-1|^2 = 4\sin^2(\omega t/2)\). For large \(t\), \(\sin^2(\omega t/2)/\omega^2\to\frac{\pi t}{2}\delta(\omega)\), which gives the golden rule.

  4. A transmon (\(C = 80\) fF) at 5 GHz sees a 50 Ω drive line through a coupling capacitor \(C_c = 0.05\) fF. With \(\Re Y\approx\omega^2C_c^2R\) for small \(C_c\), estimate the \(T_1\) limit from the drive line.
    Solution

    \(\omega = 3.14\times10^{10}\). \(\Re Y \approx (3.14\times10^{10})^2(5\times10^{-17})^2\times50 = 9.9\times10^{20}\times2.5\times10^{-33}\times50\approx1.2\times10^{-10}\) S. \(\Gamma_1 = \Re Y/C\approx1.5\times10^3\) s\(^{-1}\), so \(T_1\approx0.65\) ms. Drive lines must be coupled weakly, at the cost of needing more drive power.
