Linear operators, eigenvalues and the matrix exponential

Goals

  • See matrices as machines that transform vectors, and compute matrix elements, adjoints, traces and determinants.
  • Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand, and understand avoided crossings.
  • Understand why Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (the spectral theorem), and what unitary matrices preserve.
  • Use the Pauli matrices, compute functions of matrices including \(\ee^{-\ii Ht}\), and know the singular value decomposition.

If states are vectors, then everything you can do to a state (evolve it in time, rotate it, measure it) is an operator: a machine that takes a vector in and gives a vector out. With a basis, an operator becomes a matrix. Two questions about matrices dominate quantum engineering. First, which vectors does a matrix only stretch, and by how much? Those are its eigenvectors and eigenvalues, and they give a system's energy levels, a resonator's mode frequencies and a measurement's possible outcomes. Second, how do you compute the exponential of a matrix? That gives time evolution. This chapter answers both with examples small enough to do by hand.

Matrices are linear maps

A linear operator \(A\) maps vectors to vectors and respects linear combinations: \(A(a\ket u + b\ket v) = aA\ket u + bA\ket v\). Rotations, stretches, projections and reflections are all linear. Choose an orthonormal basis \(\{\ket j\}\). Inserting the resolution of the identity (@eq:m-resolution) on both sides of \(A\) gives

$$ A = \sum_{j,k} \ket j \bra j A \ket k \bra k = \sum_{j,k} A_{jk}\,\ketbra{j}{k}, \qquad A_{jk} = \bra j A\ket k . $$

So, once a basis is fixed, an operator is a matrix. Its element \(A_{jk}\) answers: "when \(A\) acts on basis vector \(\ket k\), how much of \(\ket j\) comes out?" Column \(k\) of the matrix is the image of \(\ket k\).

Applying one operator after another is matrix multiplication. Unlike numbers, matrices generally do not commute: \(AB\ne BA\). Rotate a book a quarter-turn about a vertical axis and then about a horizontal one, and compare with the other order: the book ends up in different positions. The commutator \([A,B] = AB - BA\) measures the difference. In quantum mechanics it decides whether two quantities can be known together (@b3:sec-q-post-uncert).

Changing to another orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{k'}\}\) is done by the matrix \(U_{jk} = \braket{j}{k'}\); the matrix of \(A\) in the new basis is \(U^\dagger A U\). Quantities that do not depend on the basis are particularly useful: the trace \(\Tr A = \sum_k A_{kk}\) (sum of the diagonal), which satisfies \(\Tr(AB) = \Tr(BA)\), and the determinant \(\det A\), which satisfies \(\det(AB) = \det A\det B\). For a \(2\times2\) matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\), \(\Tr = a + d\) and \(\det = ad - bc\).

The adjoint, Hermitian and unitary matrices

The adjoint (or Hermitian conjugate) \(A^\dagger\) is the conjugate transpose: swap rows and columns and conjugate every entry, \((A^\dagger)_{jk} = A_{kj}^*\). It reverses products, \((AB)^\dagger = B^\dagger A^\dagger\), and it is what turns a ket into a bra: \((A\ket v)^\dagger = \bra vA^\dagger\). Two families of operators matter above all others.

Definition. \(A\) is Hermitian (self-adjoint) if \(A^\dagger = A\). \(U\) is unitary if \(U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I\).

Hermitian matrices are the matrix version of real numbers: their diagonal entries are real and the entry below the diagonal is the conjugate of the one above, e.g. \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1-\ii\\ 1+\ii & 3\end{pmatrix}\). They represent measurable quantities: energy, position, spin.

Unitary matrices are the matrix version of numbers of modulus one (\(\ee^{\ii\theta}\)). They preserve every inner product, \(\braket{Uu}{Uv} = \bra u U^\dagger U\ket v = \braket uv\), and therefore every length and angle: they are generalised rotations. They represent reversible evolution and changes of basis.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Most vectors are both rotated and stretched by a matrix. Special vectors are only stretched: \(A\ket v = \lambda\ket v\). Such a nonzero \(\ket v\) is an eigenvector ("own vector") with eigenvalue \(\lambda\). To find them, rewrite as \((A - \lambda I)\ket v = 0\). A nonzero solution exists only if the matrix \(A-\lambda I\) squashes some direction to zero, i.e. is not invertible, which happens exactly when its determinant vanishes:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 . $$

This characteristic equation is a polynomial of degree \(n\) in \(\lambda\); its \(n\) roots (counted with multiplicity) are the eigenvalues. For a \(2\times2\) matrix it reads \(\lambda^2 - (\Tr A)\lambda + \det A = 0\), so

$$ \lambda_\pm = \frac{\Tr A}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Big(\frac{\Tr A}{2}\Big)^2 - \det A} . $$

For each eigenvalue, the eigenvector is found by solving \((A - \lambda I)\ket v = 0\), which for \(2\times2\) is one equation in two unknowns.

Worked example: a simple matrix. \(A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\). \(\Tr A = 4\), \(\det A = 3\), so \(\lambda = 2\pm\sqrt{4-3} = 3, 1\). For \(\lambda = 3\): \((A - 3I)\ket v = \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\ket v = 0\) gives \(v_1 = v_2\), so \(\ket v = (1,1)/\sqrt2\). For \(\lambda = 1\): \(v_1 = -v_2\), \(\ket v = (1,-1)/\sqrt2\). Check: \(A(1,1) = (3,3)\) ✓. Geometrically, \(A\) stretches the diagonal direction by 3 and the anti-diagonal by 1.

Worked example: two coupled modes and the avoided crossing. Two resonators of frequencies \(\omega_1, \omega_2\) coupled with strength \(g\) are described (Books 2 and 6) by \(H = \begin{pmatrix}\omega_1 & g\\ g & \omega_2\end{pmatrix}\). With mean \(\bar\omega = (\omega_1+\omega_2)/2\) and detuning \(\Delta = \omega_1-\omega_2\), @eq:m-2x2 gives

\[ \omega_\pm = \bar\omega \pm \tfrac12\sqrt{\Delta^2 + 4g^2}. \]

On resonance (\(\Delta = 0\)) the two modes split by \(2g\), and the eigenvectors are \((1,\pm1)/\sqrt2\): each normal mode is an equal mixture of both resonators. Far from resonance (\(|\Delta|\gg g\)), each mode is pushed away from the other by about \(g^2/|\Delta|\), and the eigenvectors are nearly the uncoupled ones. Plot \(\omega_\pm\) against \(\Delta\) and the two lines never cross: they "repel" with a minimum gap of \(2g\). This avoided crossing appears everywhere in quantum hardware, from the dispersive shift used to read out qubits to "frequency collisions" between neighbouring qubits (@lf:sec-var-collisions).

The spectral theorem

Spectral theorem (finite dimensions). If \(A\) is Hermitian, its eigenvalues are real, eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and there is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors \(\{\ket{a_k}\}\). Equivalently

$$ A = \sum_k a_k\,\ketbra{a_k}{a_k}, \qquad A = U D U^\dagger, $$

with \(D\) the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues and \(U\) the unitary whose columns are the eigenvectors.

Why the eigenvalues are real. If \(A\ket v = \lambda\ket v\), then \(\bra vA\ket v = \lambda\braket vv\). Take the complex conjugate of the left side: \(\bra vA\ket v^* = \bra vA^\dagger\ket v = \bra vA\ket v\), so it is real, and since \(\braket vv\) is real and positive, \(\lambda\) is real.

Why eigenvectors are orthogonal. If \(A\ket u = \mu\ket u\) and \(A\ket v = \lambda\ket v\) with \(\mu\ne\lambda\), compute \(\bra uA\ket v\) two ways: acting right gives \(\lambda\braket uv\); acting left (using \(A^\dagger = A\) and \(\mu\) real) gives \(\mu\braket uv\). So \((\lambda-\mu)\braket uv=0\), hence \(\braket uv=0\). (That a full basis exists needs an induction argument found in any linear algebra text [@axler2015].)

Physically: every measurable quantity has a set of "definite-value" states that are mutually exclusive (orthogonal) and together cover every possibility (a basis), with real values (real eigenvalues). That is exactly what quantum measurement needs (Book 3). The decomposition \(A = \sum_ka_k\ketbra{a_k}{a_k}\) says: a Hermitian matrix is a weighted sum of projectors onto its eigen-directions.

Unitary matrices also have orthonormal eigenbases, with eigenvalues of modulus one, \(\ee^{\ii\theta_k}\). More generally any normal matrix (\(AA^\dagger = A^\dagger A\)) can be diagonalised by a unitary.

The Pauli matrices

Three \(2\times2\) matrices appear on almost every page of Books 3–6:

$$ X = \sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad Y = \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-\ii\\ \ii&0\end{pmatrix}, \quad Z = \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}. $$

Each is Hermitian and unitary, has trace 0, and squares to the identity (so its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\)). \(X\) swaps the two basis vectors (a "bit flip"); \(Z\) flips the sign of the second (a "phase flip"); \(Y = \ii XZ\) does both. They multiply and commute according to

$$ \sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + \ii\sum_l \epsilon_{jkl}\,\sigma_l , \qquad [\sigma_j,\sigma_k] = 2\ii\sum_l\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l , $$

where \(\epsilon_{jkl}\) is \(+1\) for \((x,y,z)\) and its cyclic shifts, \(-1\) for the reversed orders, and 0 if an index repeats. In words: \(XY = \ii Z\), \(YZ = \ii X\), \(ZX = \ii Y\), and reversing the order flips the sign. Together with \(I\) they form a basis of all \(2\times2\) matrices, so any Hermitian \(2\times2\) matrix can be written \(a_0 I + a_xX + a_yY + a_zZ\) with real coefficients, and its eigenvalues are \(a_0\pm\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2}\). This is the basis of the Bloch-sphere picture of a qubit (@b3:eq-q-bloch).

Functions of matrices and the exponential

How do you compute \(\ee^{A}\) or \(\sqrt A\) for a matrix? If \(A = \sum_k a_k \ketbra{a_k}{a_k}\), apply the function to each eigenvalue:

$$ f(A) = \sum_k f(a_k)\,\ketbra{a_k}{a_k} = U f(D)\,U^\dagger . $$

This agrees with the power series (\(\ee^A = I + A + A^2/2! + \cdots\)) whenever both make sense: in the eigenbasis, \(A^n\) is just the diagonal of \(a_k^n\). The key case is the exponential. For Hermitian \(H\), \(U(t) = \ee^{-\ii Ht}\) has eigenvalues \(\ee^{-\ii h_kt}\) of modulus one, so it is unitary. It solves \(\dd\ket{\psi}/\dd t = -\ii H\ket\psi\), the Schrödinger equation in units with \(\hbar = 1\) (Chapter 5 shows why).

For Pauli matrices there is a closed form. Let \(\hat n\) be a unit vector and \(\sigma_n = \hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = n_xX + n_yY + n_zZ\). Because \(\sigma_n^2 = I\), the power series splits into even powers (which give \(I\)) and odd powers (which give \(\sigma_n\)), exactly as for Euler's formula:

$$ \ee^{-\ii\theta\,\sigma_n/2} = \cos\frac\theta2\,I - \ii\sin\frac\theta2\,\sigma_n . $$

This is a rotation of a qubit by angle \(\theta\) about the axis \(\hat n\): the single-qubit gate of Book 4. (The half-angle is a famous feature of spin-½: a full \(2\pi\) rotation gives \(-I\).)

Worked example: evolving a qubit. With \(H = \tfrac{\omega}{2}Z\) and starting state \(\ket+ = (\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\): \(\ee^{-\ii Ht} = \mathrm{diag}(\ee^{-\ii\omega t/2}, \ee^{\ii\omega t/2})\) (diagonal, because \(Z\) is), so \(\ket{\psi(t)} = (\ee^{-\ii\omega t/2}\ket0 + \ee^{\ii\omega t/2}\ket1)/\sqrt2\). The relative phase between the two components grows as \(\omega t\): the qubit "precesses" at frequency \(\omega\). After \(t = \pi/\omega\) the state is \(\propto\ket-\).

Two warnings. \(\ee^{A}\ee^{B} = \ee^{A+B}\) holds only if \([A,B]=0\). For non-commuting matrices, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formulas give the corrections; the first is used constantly to move into rotating frames and derive effective Hamiltonians (Book 3):

$$ \ee^{A}B\,\ee^{-A} = B + [A,B] + \tfrac{1}{2!}[A,[A,B]] + \cdots, \qquad \ee^{A}\ee^{B} = \ee^{A+B+\frac12[A,B]+\cdots}. $$

Singular value decomposition

Not every matrix can be diagonalised by one unitary (and non-square ones cannot at all), but every matrix can be written as

$$ M = V\,\Sigma\,W^\dagger , $$

with \(V, W\) unitary and \(\Sigma\) diagonal with non-negative singular values \(s_1\ge s_2\ge\dots\). Read it as: rotate (\(W^\dagger\)), stretch along the axes (\(\Sigma\)), rotate again (\(V\)). The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of \(M^\dagger M\). The SVD gives the best low-rank approximation of a matrix (keep the largest singular values), the condition number \(s_1/s_n\) that controls numerical error (Chapter 8), and, in Book 3, the Schmidt decomposition that measures entanglement.

A finite-element simulation of a chip produces a generalised eigenvalue problem \(\mathbf A\mathbf e = \lambda\mathbf B\mathbf e\) with millions of unknowns (@lf:sec-design-extract). Its eigenvalues are mode frequencies squared, and its eigenvectors are field patterns. Everything in this chapter, from the spectral theorem to avoided crossings, is what the solver does at enormous scale. The sensitivity of an eigenvalue to a design parameter (@lf:eq-adjoint) follows from the same argument used above to show eigenvalues are real.

  • With a basis, an operator is a matrix; \(A_{jk} = \bra jA\ket k\); matrices usually do not commute.
  • Eigenvalues solve \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\); for \(2\times2\), use trace and determinant.
  • Hermitian: real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenbasis (measurable quantities). Unitary: preserves lengths (evolution, basis changes).
  • Coupled modes repel: avoided crossing with gap \(2g\), shifts \(g^2/\Delta\) far away.
  • Functions of matrices act on eigenvalues; \(\ee^{-\ii\theta\sigma_n/2}\) is a qubit rotation; \(\ee^A\ee^B\ne\ee^{A+B}\) unless they commute.

Exercises

  1. Find the eigenvalues and normalised eigenvectors of \(X\) and of \(Y\).
    Solution

    Both have trace 0 and determinant \(-1\), so eigenvalues \(\pm1\). For \(X\): \(\ket\pm = (\ket0\pm\ket1)/\sqrt2\). For \(Y\): \(\ket{\pm\ii} = (\ket0\pm\ii\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

  2. Verify \(XY = \ii Z\) by matrix multiplication and deduce \([X,Y] = 2\ii Z\).
    Solution

    \(XY = \begin{pmatrix}\ii&0\\0&-\ii\end{pmatrix} = \ii Z\). Similarly \(YX = -\ii Z\), so \([X,Y] = 2\ii Z\).

  3. For the coupled-mode matrix with \(|\Delta|\gg g\), expand \(\omega_\pm\) to second order in \(g\). With \(\omega_1/2\pi = 5.0\) GHz, \(\omega_2/2\pi = 6.0\) GHz and \(g/2\pi = 50\) MHz, what is the shift of the lower mode?
    Solution

    \(\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2}\approx|\Delta|(1 + 2g^2/\Delta^2)\), so each mode moves away from the other by \(g^2/|\Delta|\) = \(2500/1000 = 2.5\) MHz.

  4. Use @eq:m-paulirot to show that \(\ee^{-\ii\pi X/2} = -\ii X\), and explain why the factor \(-\ii\) is unobservable.
    Solution

    With \(\theta = \pi\): \(\cos(\pi/2)=0\), \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\), giving \(-\ii X\). It differs from \(X\) by a global phase, which changes no probability (Chapter 1).

  5. Show that \(\Tr(\ketbra{u}{v}) = \braket vu\), and that the trace of a projector onto a unit vector is 1.
    Solution

    \(\Tr(\ketbra uv) = \sum_k\braket{k}{u}\braket{v}{k} = \braket vu\) using @eq:m-resolution. For \(u=v=e\), \(\Tr = 1\).

  6. Prove the first identity in @eq:m-bch to second order by expanding \(\ee^{\pm A} = I \pm A + A^2/2 + \dots\).
    Solution

    \((I + A + \tfrac12A^2)B(I - A + \tfrac12A^2) = B + [A,B] + \tfrac12(A^2B - 2ABA + BA^2) + \dots\), and \(\tfrac12(A^2B - 2ABA + BA^2) = \tfrac12[A,[A,B]]\).

  7. Diagonalise \(H = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}\) and write it as \(a_xX + a_zZ\). What are its eigenvalues?
    Solution

    \(H = X + Z\), so \(a_0 = 0\), \(|\mathbf a| = \sqrt2\), eigenvalues \(\pm\sqrt2\). (Also: trace 0, det \(-2\).) Note \(H/\sqrt2\) is the Hadamard gate of Book 4.
