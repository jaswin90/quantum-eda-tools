Numerical methods and optimisation

Goals

  • Understand how computers store numbers, where rounding error comes from, and what makes a problem ill-conditioned.
  • Know how large linear systems and eigenvalue problems are solved, including sparse and iterative methods.
  • Turn a differential equation into a matrix problem (finite differences, finite elements), and check whether the answer has converged.
  • Fit models to data (least squares), and solve optimisation problems with gradients, constraints and the adjoint method.

The equations of the previous chapters can be solved on paper only for simple shapes. A real chip has thousands of conductors of complicated shape, and its fields, modes and Hamiltonian must be computed numerically. Numerical methods turn continuous problems into large matrix problems, solve them, and (if done carefully) say how accurate the answer is. Optimisation then searches for the design that best meets a target. This chapter gives a working understanding of both: enough to read what a simulation or design tool is doing, to spot when its answer should not be trusted, and to follow the review's discussion of accuracy and scale.

Floating point and conditioning

Computers store real numbers in floating point: roughly 16 significant decimal digits in "double precision" (machine epsilon \(\epsilon\approx2.2\times10^{-16}\), the smallest relative gap between stored numbers). Every arithmetic operation may round its result by a relative amount of order \(\epsilon\). Usually this is harmless, but two situations amplify it enormously.

  • Cancellation. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers loses the digits they share. Computing a 1 MHz frequency shift as the difference of two 5 GHz eigenvalues, each accurate to 10 digits, leaves only about 6 good digits in the shift (Exercise 1).
  • Ill-conditioning. Some problems are intrinsically sensitive: tiny changes in the input produce large changes in the answer, whatever algorithm is used. For a linear system \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf b\), a relative change \(\delta\) in \(\mathbf b\) can change \(\mathbf x\) by up to \(\kappa(A)\,\delta\), where the condition number is the ratio of largest to smallest singular value (@eq:m-svd), \(\kappa(A) = s_{\max}/s_{\min}\). With \(\kappa = 10^{10}\), even a perfect algorithm loses 10 of its 16 digits.

Think of solving two nearly parallel lines for their intersection. Nudge one line slightly and the intersection point slides a long way along them. Nearly parallel lines are an ill-conditioned system: the equations barely distinguish between very different answers. Finite-element matrices for structures with very different length scales (100 nm junction leads and centimetre ground planes on one chip) behave like this.

A good habit: when a simulation gives a number, ask how many digits are physically meaningful, how many are numerically reliable, and whether those match the precision you need. The review's estimate that qubit frequencies must be predicted to a few parts in \(10^3\) (@lf:sec-design-accuracy) is demanding precisely because of these effects.

Solving linear systems and eigenproblems

Solving \(A\mathbf x = \mathbf b\) for a dense \(n\times n\) matrix is done by LU factorisation, Gaussian elimination organised as \(A = LU\) with triangular factors, in about \(\tfrac23n^3\) operations. That is fine for \(n = 10^4\) (seconds) but hopeless for the \(n = 10^7\) unknowns of a 3D electromagnetic simulation (centuries). Physical problems come to the rescue: each unknown interacts only with its neighbours, so the matrix is sparse (almost all entries zero). Two strategies exploit this:

  • Sparse direct solvers reorder the unknowns so that elimination creates few new nonzeros ("fill-in"). Robust, but memory grows faster than \(n\) in 3D.
  • Iterative (Krylov) solvers, such as conjugate gradients (for symmetric positive-definite matrices) and GMRES, build the answer from the sequence \(\mathbf b, A\mathbf b, A^2\mathbf b,\dots\), needing only matrix–vector products, which are cheap for sparse matrices. Their speed depends on the condition number, so they need a preconditioner: a rough, cheap approximate inverse that makes the system better conditioned.

For eigenvalues, dense matrices use the QR algorithm (repeated QR factorisations that converge to triangular form) in \(O(n^3)\). For large sparse problems we usually want only a few eigenvalues near a target, for example the modes near 5 GHz. Shift-invert iteration applies \((A - \sigma B)^{-1}\), whose largest eigenvalues correspond to the eigenvalues of \(A\mathbf e = \lambda B\mathbf e\) closest to the shift \(\sigma\). This is what an eigenmode solver does when you ask for "the first 10 modes above 3 GHz" [@trefethen1997].

Worked example: why sparsity matters. A 3D mesh with \(10^6\) unknowns, each coupled to about 30 neighbours, has \(3\times10^7\) nonzero matrix entries (about 0.5 GB). Stored dense, the matrix would have \(10^{12}\) entries (8 TB), and LU would take \(\sim10^{18}\) operations (weeks on a fast machine). A preconditioned iterative solver might need a few hundred matrix–vector products of \(3\times10^7\) operations each: about \(10^{10}\) operations, seconds.

Discretising differential equations

To solve \(-u''(x) = f(x)\) on \([0,1]\) with \(u(0)=u(1)=0\) by computer, place \(N\) grid points at spacing \(h\) and replace the second derivative by a finite difference. From Taylor expansion, \(u(x\pm h) = u \pm hu' + \tfrac{h^2}{2}u'' \pm\dots\); adding the two and rearranging gives

$$ -u''(x_k) \approx \frac{-u_{k-1} + 2u_k - u_{k+1}}{h^2}, \qquad \text{error } = O(h^2). $$

The differential equation becomes a tridiagonal linear system \(A\mathbf u = \mathbf f\). Halving \(h\) cuts the error by four ("second-order accuracy"), but doubles the unknowns in 1D and multiplies them by eight in 3D. The eigenvalues of \(A\) approximate the true ones \((n\pi)^2\) well for low modes and badly for modes whose wavelength approaches \(h\): a grid can only represent features larger than its spacing.

The finite-element method (FEM) generalises this to complicated shapes. The domain is cut into small elements (triangles in 2D, tetrahedra in 3D), the unknown is approximated by simple polynomials on each element, and the equation is imposed in an averaged "weak" form (multiply by test functions and integrate). This produces a sparse linear system or generalised eigenproblem. Its great advantage is flexibility: elements can be tiny near a junction or a sharp metal edge and large in empty space. Book 7 (@b7:sec-f-em) works through it, including a one-dimensional example you can run.

Convergence studies. A single simulation result is not an answer. A trustworthy result is a sequence of results on finer meshes that visibly converges, with an estimated limit and error. For a method of order \(p\), the result on mesh size \(h\) behaves like \(Q_h \approx Q + ch^p\), so three meshes determine \(Q\), \(c\) and \(p\) (Richardson extrapolation). The review notes that in quantum design practice "convergence is judged by eye"; automating this is part of what a sign-off tool must do.

Worked example: Richardson extrapolation. Three meshes, each half the size of the previous, give 5.212, 5.183 and 5.176 GHz. The differences, 29 and 7 MHz, shrink by about 4 each time, so \(p\approx2\). The remaining error on the finest mesh is about \(7/(2^2-1)\approx2.3\) MHz, so the extrapolated answer is about 5.174 GHz. Without the study you would not know whether the finest result was good to 0.1 MHz or 10 MHz.

Least squares and fitting

Fitting a model to data is the most common numerical task in a lab: extracting \(T_1\) from a decay curve, or a resonance frequency and \(Q\) from a sweep. For a model that is linear in its parameters, \(\mathbf y\approx X\boldsymbol\beta\) (each column of \(X\) a basis function evaluated at the data points), minimising the squared error \(\|\mathbf y - X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\) gives the normal equations \(X^{\mathsf T}X\boldsymbol\beta = X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y\). Geometrically, the best fit is the projection of the data onto the space spanned by the model's columns (Chapter 2). Numerically it is better to solve via QR or SVD, because forming \(X^{\mathsf T}X\) squares the condition number. Adding a penalty \(\lambda\|\boldsymbol\beta\|^2\) (ridge regularisation) stabilises ill-conditioned fits and corresponds to a Gaussian prior on \(\boldsymbol\beta\) (Chapter 7).

For models nonlinear in their parameters, such as \(A\ee^{-t/T_1}+B\) or a Lorentzian, iterative methods (Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt) linearise around the current guess, solve a linear least-squares step, and repeat. They need sensible starting values; with a bad initial guess they can converge to a wrong answer. That is why automated calibration software spends much of its effort on initial guesses.

Optimisation

Design is optimisation: choose parameters \(\mathbf x\) (pad sizes, gaps, frequencies) to minimise a cost \(f(\mathbf x)\) (gate error, crosstalk, area), subject to constraints. The gradient \(\nabla f\) points uphill, so gradient descent repeatedly steps \(\mathbf x\leftarrow\mathbf x - \eta\nabla f\) with a step size \(\eta\). Newton's method also uses second derivatives (the Hessian matrix \(H\)), stepping \(\mathbf x\leftarrow\mathbf x - H^{-1}\nabla f\); near a minimum it converges extremely fast. Quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS build an approximate Hessian from successive gradients [@nocedal2006].

A problem is convex if the cost and the allowed region are bowl-shaped (no separate valleys); then every local minimum is the global one, and reliable algorithms exist [@boyd2004]. Most device design problems are not convex, so practical design combines good initial guesses, gradient-based local search and global strategies such as multi-start or Bayesian optimisation (Book 7).

Constraints are handled with Lagrange multipliers. To minimise \(f(\mathbf x)\) subject to \(g(\mathbf x) = 0\), look for stationary points of the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L = f + \lambda g\):

$$ \nabla f + \lambda\nabla g = 0, \qquad g(\mathbf x) = 0 . $$

Geometrically: at the optimum you cannot improve \(f\) by sliding along the constraint surface, so \(\nabla f\) must be perpendicular to it, i.e. parallel to \(\nabla g\). The multiplier \(\lambda\) measures how much the optimal cost would improve if the constraint were loosened slightly: the "price" of a specification. Inequality constraints \(g\le0\) give the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions, in which a multiplier is nonzero only when its constraint is active (pressed against).

Gradients of simulations: the adjoint method

Gradient-based design needs the derivative of a simulation result with respect to every design parameter. Suppose 100 parameters \(\mathbf p\) enter a large linear system \(A(\mathbf p)\mathbf u = \mathbf b\), and the cost is \(f = \mathbf c^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u\). Finite differences (change each parameter a little and re-solve) need 101 solves. The adjoint method needs two. Differentiate \(A\mathbf u = \mathbf b\) with respect to \(p_k\): \(A\,\partial_k\mathbf u = -(\partial_kA)\mathbf u\). So

$$ \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_k} = \mathbf c^{\mathsf T}\partial_k\mathbf u = -\boldsymbol\lambda^{\mathsf T}(\partial_kA)\,\mathbf u, \qquad A^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\lambda = \mathbf c . $$

One forward solve for \(\mathbf u\), one "adjoint" solve for \(\boldsymbol\lambda\), and then each component of the gradient is a cheap matrix product, however many parameters there are. The same trick for eigenvalue problems gives the eigenvalue-sensitivity formula used in the review (@lf:eq-adjoint), and it is the same mathematics as back-propagation in neural networks. Adjoint gradients are what make "inverse design" of photonic and microwave components practical.

Electronic design automation is, in large part, applied numerical linear algebra and optimisation at scale: sparse solvers for extraction, model-order reduction to compress what the solver produced (@lf:sec-design-scale), and constrained optimisation for placement and frequency allocation. Quantum EDA inherits all of it, and adds tighter accuracy targets and statistics. This chapter is the minimum toolkit for reading those sections of the review critically.

  • Doubles carry ~16 digits; cancellation and ill-conditioning (\(\kappa = s_{\max}/s_{\min}\)) destroy them.
  • Large physical problems are sparse: use sparse direct or preconditioned iterative solvers; shift-invert for modes near a target.
  • Finite differences and finite elements turn PDEs into sparse matrices; always run a convergence study and extrapolate.
  • Least squares is projection; fit via QR/SVD; nonlinear fits need good starting values.
  • Gradients and Lagrange multipliers for constrained design; the adjoint method gives all gradients for the cost of two solves.

Exercises

  1. Two eigenvalues are computed as 5.000 000 123 GHz and 5.000 000 456 GHz, each with relative error \(10^{-9}\). What is the relative error of their difference?
    Solution

    Each has absolute error about 5 Hz. The difference is 333 Hz, with error up to about 10 Hz: about 3% relative, six orders of magnitude worse than the inputs. Compute small splittings from a model that gives them directly, not as differences of large numbers.

  2. For \(A = h^{-2}\,\mathrm{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)\) of size \(N\), the eigenvalues are \(\frac{4}{h^2}\sin^2\frac{n\pi h}{2}\) with \(h = 1/(N+1)\). Compare with the exact \((n\pi)^2\) for \(n=1\) and \(n=N\) when \(N = 99\).
    Solution

    \(h = 0.01\). \(n=1\): \(9.8688\) vs \(9.8696\) (0.008% error). \(n=99\): \(3.999\times10^4\) vs \(9.67\times10^4\), wrong by a factor 2.4. Only modes well resolved by the grid are trustworthy.

  3. Minimise \(f = x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(x + 2y = 5\) with a Lagrange multiplier.
    Solution

    \((2x + \lambda, 2y + 2\lambda) = 0\) gives \(x = -\lambda/2\), \(y = -\lambda\); the constraint gives \(\lambda = -2\), so \((1,2)\), \(f = 5\): the closest point on the line to the origin.

  4. A cost depends on 200 layout parameters through a simulation taking 10 minutes per solve. How long does one gradient take by finite differences, and by the adjoint method?
    Solution

    Finite differences: 201 solves ≈ 33 hours. Adjoint: 2 solves = 20 minutes.

  5. Show that minimising \(\sum_k(y_k - \beta x_k)^2\) gives \(\beta = \sum x_ky_k/\sum x_k^2\), and interpret it as a projection.
    Solution

    Setting the derivative \(-2\sum x_k(y_k - \beta x_k)\) to zero gives the result: \(\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle/\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x\rangle\), the coefficient of the projection of \(\mathbf y\) onto \(\mathbf x\).

  6. Run gradient descent by hand on \(f(x) = (x-3)^2\) from \(x = 0\) with step \(\eta = 0.25\). What happens with \(\eta = 1.1\)?
    Solution

    \(f' = 2(x-3)\). With \(\eta = 0.25\): \(x\to x - 0.5(x-3)\): 0, 1.5, 2.25, 2.625, ... halving the distance to 3 each step. With \(\eta = 1.1\): \(x\to x - 2.2(x-3)\), so the distance to 3 is multiplied by \(-1.2\) each step: the iterates overshoot and diverge. Step size matters.
