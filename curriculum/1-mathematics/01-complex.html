Complex numbers, the exponential and phasors

Goals

  • Do arithmetic with complex numbers in Cartesian and polar form, and picture it in the plane.
  • Derive Euler's formula \(\ee^{\ii\theta} = \cos\theta + \ii\sin\theta\) and use it to replace trigonometry with algebra.
  • Represent oscillating signals as phasors, and understand why physicists and engineers write \(\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\) and \(\ee^{+j\omega t}\) respectively.
  • See why quantum amplitudes must be complex numbers.

Arithmetic in the plane

A complex number is a pair of real numbers \(z = x + \ii y\), where \(\ii\) is a symbol with the single rule \(\ii^2 = -1\). We call \(x = \Re z\) the real part and \(y = \Im z\) the imaginary part. Addition and multiplication follow ordinary algebra together with that rule:

$$ (a+\ii b)(c+\ii d) = (ac - bd) + \ii\,(ad + bc). $$

The complex conjugate flips the sign of the imaginary part, \(z^* = x - \ii y\), and the modulus is the distance from the origin, \(|z| = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). The two are related by the identity that does most of the work in quantum mechanics:

$$ z z^* = (x+\ii y)(x-\ii y) = x^2 + y^2 = |z|^2 . $$

Division uses the same trick: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator to make the denominator real, \(1/z = z^*/|z|^2\).

It helps to draw \(z\) as the point \((x,y)\) in the complex plane. Addition is then vector addition. Multiplication is less obvious in Cartesian form, which is why we now change coordinates.

Polar form and Euler's formula

Any point in the plane can be given by its distance \(r=|z|\) from the origin and its angle \(\theta\) from the positive real axis, so \(z = r(\cos\theta + \ii\sin\theta)\). The angle is called the argument or phase, \(\theta = \arg z\). Now multiply two numbers in this form and use the angle-addition formulas from trigonometry:

\[ (\cos\alpha + \ii\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta+\ii\sin\beta) = (\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta) + \ii(\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta) = \cos(\alpha+\beta) + \ii\sin(\alpha+\beta). \]

Multiplying adds the angles. The function \(f(\theta) = \cos\theta + \ii\sin\theta\) therefore satisfies \(f(\alpha)f(\beta) = f(\alpha+\beta)\), which is the defining property of an exponential. To find which exponential, differentiate:

\[ \frac{\dd f}{\dd\theta} = -\sin\theta + \ii\cos\theta = \ii(\cos\theta + \ii\sin\theta) = \ii f(\theta), \qquad f(0)=1 . \]

The unique solution of \(f' = \ii f\) with \(f(0)=1\) is \(f(\theta) = \ee^{\ii\theta}\). This is Euler's formula:

$$ \ee^{\ii\theta} = \cos\theta + \ii\sin\theta, \qquad z = r\,\ee^{\ii\theta}. $$

A second way to see it is from the power series \(\ee^x = \sum_n x^n/n!\). Substituting \(x = \ii\theta\) and grouping even and odd powers using \(\ii^2=-1\), \(\ii^3=-\ii\), \(\ii^4=1\) reproduces the series for cosine and sine. Both arguments matter: the first shows why the exponential appears (it turns rotation into multiplication), the second shows that the definition is consistent.

In polar form, multiplication is simple: \(r_1\ee^{\ii\theta_1}\cdot r_2\ee^{\ii\theta_2} = r_1r_2\,\ee^{\ii(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\). Moduli multiply and phases add. Multiplying by \(\ee^{\ii\phi}\) rotates a point by \(\phi\) without changing its length. Inverting Euler's formula gives the two identities you will use most often:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{\ee^{\ii\theta}+\ee^{-\ii\theta}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{\ee^{\ii\theta}-\ee^{-\ii\theta}}{2\ii}. $$

Worked example: trigonometry without memorisation. Find \(\cos^2\theta\) in terms of \(\cos 2\theta\).

Using @eq:m-cossin, \(\cos^2\theta = \tfrac14(\ee^{\ii\theta}+\ee^{-\ii\theta})^2 = \tfrac14(\ee^{2\ii\theta} + 2 + \ee^{-2\ii\theta}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos2\theta\). The same method gives every product-to-sum formula. In Book 3 the identical algebra produces the "rotating-wave approximation", where a product of a drive \(\cos\omega t\) and a qubit oscillation \(\ee^{-\ii\omega_q t}\) splits into a slowly varying part and a fast part that averages away.

Oscillations and phasors

A sinusoidal signal of amplitude \(A\), angular frequency \(\omega\) and phase \(\phi\) is \(v(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\). Using Euler's formula,

$$ v(t) = \Re\!\big[\,\tilde V\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}\big], \qquad \tilde V = A\,\ee^{\ii\phi}. $$

The complex number \(\tilde V\) is the phasor. It carries the amplitude and phase; the factor \(\ee^{\ii\omega t}\) carries the time dependence, which is the same for every signal in a linear circuit driven at frequency \(\omega\). The payoff is that differentiation becomes multiplication: \(\dd/\dd t\) acting on \(\ee^{\ii\omega t}\) gives \(\ii\omega\). A capacitor obeys \(i = C\,\dd v/\dd t\), so in phasor form \(\tilde I = \ii\omega C\,\tilde V\), and its impedance is \(Z_C = \tilde V/\tilde I = 1/(\ii\omega C)\). An inductor, \(v = L\,\dd i/\dd t\), has \(Z_L = \ii\omega L\). Circuit analysis at a single frequency becomes algebra with complex numbers. Book 2 builds microwave engineering on exactly this.

Sign conventions. Electrical engineers write \(\ee^{+j\omega t}\) with \(j=\sqrt{-1}\), so an inductor is \(j\omega L\). Physicists write \(\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\), because the Schrödinger equation makes a state of energy \(E\) evolve as \(\ee^{-\ii Et/\hbar}\); with that convention an inductor is \(-\ii\omega L\). Nothing physical changes, since the two are complex conjugates, but formulas copied between an engineering text and a physics paper often disagree by a conjugate. When a sign looks wrong, check the convention first. This series uses \(\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\) in quantum chapters and says so explicitly when it switches to the engineering convention for circuits.

Two phasors at the same frequency add like vectors. This is interference: \(A\ee^{\ii\phi_1} + A\ee^{\ii\phi_2}\) has modulus \(2A|\cos((\phi_1-\phi_2)/2)|\), which is \(2A\) when the phases agree and zero when they differ by \(\pi\). The power, proportional to the modulus squared, is \(|\tilde V_1+\tilde V_2|^2 = |\tilde V_1|^2 + |\tilde V_2|^2 + 2\Re(\tilde V_1\tilde V_2^*)\). The last term, the cross term, is what makes interferometers work, from a Mach–Zehnder modulator to the quantum interference of photons in Book 5.

Rotating frames

Suppose a signal oscillates near a carrier frequency \(\omega_0\): \(s(t) = a(t)\,\ee^{-\ii\omega_0 t}\), where the envelope \(a(t)\) changes slowly. Multiplying by \(\ee^{+\ii\omega_0 t}\) removes the carrier and leaves \(a(t)\). We say we have moved to a frame rotating at \(\omega_0\). A component that oscillates at \(\omega\) in the lab frame oscillates at \(\omega - \omega_0\), the detuning, in the rotating frame. This is what a heterodyne receiver does in hardware (it mixes the signal with a local oscillator), and it is what a quantum engineer does on paper to make qubit dynamics look slow and simple. You will meet it again in the Rabi problem (@b3:sec-q-spin), in dispersive readout and in pulse shaping.

Why quantum amplitudes are complex

In quantum mechanics, a system that can reach an outcome along two indistinguishable paths is described by assigning each path an amplitude, a complex number \(c_k\). The probability of the outcome is the modulus squared of the sum, \(P = |c_1 + c_2|^2\). By @eq:m-modsq that contains the cross term \(2\Re(c_1c_2^*)\), so probabilities can cancel. Real amplitudes with signs would allow some cancellation, but complex numbers are needed for two further reasons that will become clear in Book 3: time evolution is a rotation of phases, \(\ee^{-\ii Et/\hbar}\), which needs a continuous family of phases rather than just \(\pm1\); and the description of spin requires three mutually "perpendicular" directions in a two-dimensional space, which real numbers cannot supply. For now the key fact is: only relative phases between amplitudes are observable. Multiplying every amplitude by the same \(\ee^{\ii\chi}\) leaves every \(|c_1+c_2|^2\) unchanged.

Every simulated quantity in a superconducting-chip design flow, from an impedance \(Z(\omega)\) to a scattering parameter \(S_{21}\), is a complex number at each frequency. Its modulus tells you how much signal passes; its phase tells you the delay and, near a resonance, the frequency and linewidth. Reading a resonator's response as a circle in the complex plane, rather than as a dip in a magnitude plot, is how loss is extracted from measurements (Book 2, @b2:sec-c-tline).

Exercises

  1. Write \(z = 1 + \ii\sqrt3\) in polar form and compute \(z^6\).
    Solution

    \(|z| = \sqrt{1+3}=2\) and \(\arg z = \arctan\sqrt3 = \pi/3\), so \(z = 2\ee^{\ii\pi/3}\). Then \(z^6 = 2^6\ee^{2\pi\ii} = 64\). In Cartesian form this would take five multiplications.

  2. Show that \(|z_1z_2| = |z_1||z_2|\) and that \((z_1z_2)^* = z_1^*z_2^*\).
    Solution

    In polar form \(z_1z_2 = r_1r_2\ee^{\ii(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\), whose modulus is \(r_1r_2\). Conjugation flips the sign of every phase, \((r\ee^{\ii\theta})^* = r\ee^{-\ii\theta}\), so \((z_1z_2)^* = r_1r_2\ee^{-\ii(\theta_1+\theta_2)} = z_1^*z_2^*\).

  3. A resistor \(R\) is in series with a capacitor \(C\). Find the impedance and the frequency at which the current leads the voltage by \(45^\circ\) (engineering convention \(\ee^{j\omega t}\)).
    Solution

    \(Z = R + 1/(j\omega C) = R - j/(\omega C)\). The current is \(\tilde I = \tilde V/Z\), so its phase relative to the voltage is \(-\arg Z = \arctan(1/(\omega RC))\). This equals \(45^\circ\) when \(\omega RC = 1\), i.e. \(\omega = 1/RC\), the familiar corner frequency of an RC filter.

  4. Use @eq:m-cossin to show \(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]\). If \(A = \omega_d t\) is a drive and \(B = \omega_q t\) a qubit oscillation with \(\omega_d \approx \omega_q \approx 2\pi\times 5\) GHz, what are the two frequencies that appear, and which would an averaging over 10 ns keep?
    Solution

    Expanding both cosines in exponentials and collecting terms gives \(\tfrac14(\ee^{\ii(A+B)}+\ee^{-\ii(A+B)}+\ee^{\ii(A-B)}+\ee^{-\ii(A-B)})\), which is the stated result. The frequencies are the difference \(\omega_d-\omega_q\) (near zero) and the sum \(\approx 2\pi\times 10\) GHz. Over 10 ns the sum term completes about 100 cycles and averages to nearly zero; the difference term survives. Dropping the sum term is the rotating-wave approximation.

  5. Show that multiplying all amplitudes by a common phase \(\ee^{\ii\chi}\) leaves \(|c_1+c_2|^2\) unchanged, but changing only \(c_2 \to c_2\ee^{\ii\chi}\) does not.
    Solution

    \(|\ee^{\ii\chi}(c_1+c_2)|^2 = |\ee^{\ii\chi}|^2|c_1+c_2|^2 = |c_1+c_2|^2\). With only \(c_2\) changed, the cross term becomes \(2\Re(c_1c_2^*\ee^{-\ii\chi})\), which depends on \(\chi\). Global phases are invisible; relative phases are observable.
