Fourier analysis, linear systems and spectra

Goals

  • Understand the idea of a spectrum: any signal is a sum of pure tones.
  • Compute Fourier series and transforms of simple signals, and use their rules (derivative, shift, modulation, Parseval).
  • Understand the time–frequency trade-off and why it limits how fast a qubit can be driven.
  • Analyse linear systems with impulse responses, convolution and transfer functions; know sampling, aliasing and the FFT.
  • Describe noise by its power spectral density.

A musical chord is several notes at once; your ear separates them. Fourier analysis is the mathematics of that separation: any signal, however complicated, is a sum of pure sine waves of different frequencies, and the list of how much of each frequency is present is its spectrum. Engineers think in spectra because linear systems (filters, cables, amplifiers, resonators) treat each frequency independently, so a complicated problem in time becomes a simple one in frequency. Quantum engineers need it for three things in particular: designing control pulses that do not disturb neighbouring transitions, understanding how signals are distorted on their way to a chip, and describing noise.

Fourier series: periodic signals

A signal that repeats every period \(T\) can be written as a sum of complex exponentials at multiples of the fundamental frequency \(\omega_1 = 2\pi/T\):

$$ f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\,\ee^{\ii n\omega_1t}, \qquad c_n = \frac1T\int_0^T f(t)\,\ee^{-\ii n\omega_1t}\,\dd t . $$

Where does the coefficient formula come from? From Chapter 2: the functions \(\ee^{\ii n\omega_1t}/\sqrt T\) form an orthonormal basis for functions on \([0, T]\) (check: \(\int_0^T\ee^{\ii(m-n)\omega_1t}\dd t\) is \(T\) if \(m = n\) and zero otherwise, because a whole number of oscillations integrates to zero). So \(c_n\) is just "the component of \(f\) along the \(n\)-th basis function", an inner product. Each \(c_n\) is a complex number: its modulus is the strength of harmonic \(n\), its argument the phase.

Worked example: a square wave. \(f = +1\) for the first half of each period and \(-1\) for the second. Doing the integral gives \(c_n = 2/(\ii\pi n)\) for odd \(n\) and 0 for even \(n\). Combining \(\pm n\) terms: \(f(t) = \frac4\pi\big(\sin\omega_1t + \tfrac13\sin3\omega_1t + \tfrac15\sin5\omega_1t + \cdots\big)\). The harmonics fall off only as \(1/n\). That slow fall-off is caused by the sharp jumps: a smooth signal has coefficients that fall off much faster. Rule of thumb: sharp features in time need many high frequencies.

The Fourier transform: any signal

A signal that does not repeat can be treated as one with an infinitely long period. The allowed frequencies \(n\omega_1\) then crowd together into a continuum, and the sum becomes an integral. We use the convention

$$ \tilde f(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}\,\dd t, \qquad f(t) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde f(\omega)\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}\,\dd\omega . $$

\(\tilde f(\omega)\) is the spectrum: how much of frequency \(\omega\) the signal contains. (Engineering texts often put the minus sign in the forward transform; as with phasors, the conventions are complex conjugates.) A small set of rules turns the transform into a calculating machine:

  • Derivative: \(\dot f \to -\ii\omega\tilde f\). Differential equations become algebra.
  • Shift: \(f(t - t_0)\to \ee^{\ii\omega t_0}\tilde f\). A delay changes only the phases, by an amount growing linearly with frequency.
  • Modulation: \(f(t)\ee^{-\ii\omega_0t} \to \tilde f(\omega-\omega_0)\). Multiplying by a carrier shifts the whole spectrum by \(\omega_0\). This is how a slow pulse envelope is moved up to a 5 GHz qubit frequency.
  • Parseval: \(\int|f|^2\dd t = \frac1{2\pi}\int|\tilde f|^2\dd\omega\). The total energy is the same counted in time or in frequency.

Two transform pairs are worth memorising:

  • Gaussian ↔ Gaussian. \(\ee^{-t^2/2\sigma^2} \to \sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\,\ee^{-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}\). A pulse of width \(\sigma\) in time has width \(1/\sigma\) in angular frequency: short pulse, broad spectrum.
  • Decay ↔ Lorentzian. A one-sided decay \(f(t) = \ee^{-\gamma t/2}\) for \(t>0\) gives \(\tilde f(\omega) = 1/(\gamma/2 - \ii\omega)\), whose modulus squared is the Lorentzian \(1/(\omega^2+\gamma^2/4)\), of full width \(\gamma\). This is the precise statement behind Chapter 5's "linewidth equals decay rate" (@fig:m-lorentz).

Time–frequency uncertainty. For any pulse, the spreads in time and angular frequency (standard deviations of \(|f|^2\) and \(|\tilde f|^2\)) satisfy \(\Delta t\,\Delta\omega \ge \tfrac12\), with equality for a Gaussian.

This is a purely mathematical fact about Fourier pairs: you cannot have a signal that is both very short and very pure in frequency. Its quantum version, with \(E = \hbar\omega\), is the energy–time uncertainty relation. Its practical consequence is a speed limit on qubit control. A transmon's \(1\to2\) transition sits only 200–300 MHz from the \(0\to1\) transition we want to drive. A pulse much shorter than a few nanoseconds has a spectrum broad enough to reach the unwanted transition and excite it: leakage. Shaped pulses such as DRAG (Book 6) are designed in the frequency domain to put a spectral zero right at the unwanted transition.

Worked example: how short can a pulse be? A Gaussian pulse with \(\sigma = 5\) ns has spectral width \(1/\sigma = 2\times10^8\) rad/s, i.e. \(\Delta f = 32\) MHz. A transition 250 MHz away is about 8 spectral standard deviations from the carrier, where a Gaussian is negligible: safe. With \(\sigma = 1\) ns the width is 160 MHz and the unwanted transition is only 1.6 standard deviations away: the pulse would leak badly. That is why single-qubit gates on transmons take 20–40 ns (several \(\sigma\) for a smooth pulse) rather than 1 ns.

The delta function

The Dirac delta \(\delta(t)\) is an idealised impulse: zero everywhere except at \(t = 0\), so concentrated that its area is 1, \(\int\delta(t)\,\dd t = 1\). Its defining property is "sifting": \(\int f(t)\delta(t-t_0)\dd t = f(t_0)\). It is not an ordinary function but the limit of ever narrower, taller pulses (a Gaussian with \(\sigma\to0\)). Its Fourier transform is the constant 1: an infinitely short impulse contains all frequencies equally (the extreme case of "short pulse, broad spectrum"). Conversely, \(\int\ee^{\ii\omega t}\dd t = 2\pi\delta(\omega)\): a pure, eternal tone has all its power at one frequency.

Linear time-invariant systems and convolution

A system is linear if the response to a sum of inputs is the sum of responses, and time-invariant if delaying the input just delays the output. Cables, filters, amplifiers (at small signals) and resonators are all LTI. Every LTI system is completely described by its impulse response \(h(t)\): the output when the input is a delta function. Why? Any input can be written as a sum of shifted, scaled impulses, \(x(t) = \int x(s)\delta(t-s)\dd s\); by linearity and time invariance, the output is the same sum of shifted, scaled impulse responses:

$$ y(t) = \int h(t-s)\,x(s)\,\dd s = (h * x)(t), \qquad \tilde y(\omega) = \tilde h(\omega)\,\tilde x(\omega) . $$

The first expression is a convolution, the "weighted memory of past inputs" of @eq:m-firstorder (there, \(h(t) = \ee^{-\gamma t}\)). The second is the convolution theorem: convolution in time is plain multiplication in frequency. The function \(\tilde h(\omega)\) is the transfer function. This is why engineers live in the frequency domain: a chain of filters, cables and amplifiers is just a product of transfer functions.

Worked example: pre-distorting a pulse. The control line to a qubit (cables, filters, attenuators) has a transfer function \(\tilde h(\omega)\) that slightly smears and delays pulses: what arrives at the chip is \(h * x\), not the intended \(x\). If \(\tilde h\) is measured, the electronics can send \(x_{\mathrm{pre}}\) with \(\tilde x_{\mathrm{pre}} = \tilde x_{\mathrm{target}}/\tilde h\), so that the chip receives exactly the target pulse. This "pre-distortion" is standard for the fast flux pulses used in two-qubit gates.

The Laplace transform, \(F(s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)\ee^{-st}\dd t\), is the same idea for signals that start at \(t=0\), with a complex variable \(s = \sigma + \ii\omega\). Transfer functions of circuits are ratios of polynomials in \(s\), and their poles (values of \(s\) where they blow up) are the system's resonances: a pole at \(s_k = -\gamma_k/2 + \ii\omega_k\) is a mode with frequency \(\omega_k\) and loss rate \(\gamma_k\), exactly the eigenvalues of Chapter 5. A physical, stable system has all its poles in the left half of the complex plane (decaying, not growing).

Sampling and the discrete Fourier transform

Computers, digitisers and waveform generators work with samples \(f_k = f(k\Delta t)\), \(k = 0,\dots,N-1\). The discrete Fourier transform is

$$ F_n = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} f_k\,\ee^{-2\pi\ii\,kn/N}, \qquad f_k = \frac1N\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}F_n\,\ee^{2\pi\ii\,kn/N}, $$

computed in \(O(N\log N)\) steps by the fast Fourier transform (FFT), one of the most important algorithms ever written. Three facts about sampling:

  • Nyquist frequency. Sampling at rate \(f_s = 1/\Delta t\) can represent frequencies only up to \(f_s/2\).
  • Aliasing. Higher frequencies do not disappear; they masquerade as lower ones (a 1.6 GHz tone sampled at 2.4 GS/s looks like 0.8 GHz). Anti-aliasing filters remove them before sampling.
  • Resolution. The frequency resolution is \(1/(N\Delta t)\), the inverse of the total record length: to resolve two lines 1 MHz apart, record for at least 1 µs.

The DFT, divided by \(\sqrt N\), is a unitary matrix (Chapter 3). Its quantum version, the quantum Fourier transform, is the engine of Shor's factoring algorithm (Book 4).

Noise and spectral densities

A noisy signal \(x(t)\) is unpredictable in detail, so we describe it statistically. If its statistics do not change over time (stationary), its autocorrelation \(C(\tau) = \expval{x(t+\tau)x(t)}\) depends only on the time lag \(\tau\): it says how similar the noise is to itself a time \(\tau\) later. The power spectral density is the Fourier transform of the autocorrelation (the Wiener–Khinchin theorem):

$$ S_x(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty C(\tau)\,\ee^{\ii\omega\tau}\,\dd\tau, \qquad \expval{x^2} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int S_x(\omega)\,\dd\omega . $$

It tells you how the noise power is spread over frequency. White noise has a flat spectrum (no memory: \(C(\tau)\) is a spike at zero); resistor noise is white over a huge range (Book 2). \(1/f\) noise, with \(S\propto1/|\omega|\), has power at all time scales, growing towards slow fluctuations; it causes the slow wandering of qubit frequencies. Book 3 shows that a qubit acts as a spectrum analyser: its energy relaxation samples the noise at its own frequency, while its dephasing samples noise near zero frequency. Specifying noise as a spectral density is therefore how the hardware books connect physical noise sources to error rates.

One-sided and two-sided spectra. Physicists often use the two-sided density above, defined for positive and negative \(\omega\). Engineers often quote a one-sided density over positive frequencies only, which is twice as large, and per hertz rather than per radian per second. Factors of 2 and \(2\pi\) in noise formulas almost always come from this.

Try it in Python

The FFT turns a sampled signal into its spectrum. Here a decaying 50 MHz tone is sampled at 1 GS/s; its spectrum shows a Lorentzian peak whose width matches the decay rate.

import numpy as np
fs, N = 1e9, 2**16                              # 1 GS/s, 65.5 us record
t = np.arange(N) / fs
gamma = 2*np.pi*1e6                             # energy decay rate (1 MHz linewidth)
x = np.exp(-gamma*t/2) * np.cos(2*np.pi*50e6*t)
X = np.fft.rfft(x)
f = np.fft.rfftfreq(N, 1/fs)
P = abs(X)**2
k = P.argmax()
half = f[P >= P[k]/2]
print(f[k], half.max() - half.min())           # peak at 50 MHz, width about 1 MHz

Halve gamma and the measured width halves: linewidth equals decay rate. Shorten the record (smaller N) until the width you measure is set by the record length rather than by the physics: that is the resolution limit \(1/(N\Delta t)\).

Electromagnetic solvers work either in time (launch an impulse, record the ringing, Fourier transform) or in frequency (one solve per frequency). The pole–residue fits used to compress a simulated response into a small model (vector fitting, @lf:sec-design-scale) are rational transfer functions whose poles are the circuit's resonances. Passivity, the condition that a circuit cannot create energy, becomes a condition on the transfer function, and checking it is part of sign-off.

  • Any signal is a sum of pure tones; Fourier coefficients are inner products with complex exponentials.
  • Derivative ↔ \(\times(-\ii\omega)\), delay ↔ linear phase, modulation ↔ frequency shift, energy conserved.
  • Short pulses have broad spectra (\(\Delta t\Delta\omega\ge\tfrac12\)); this sets the speed limit of qubit gates.
  • LTI systems: output = impulse response convolved with input = transfer function times input spectrum; poles are resonances.
  • Sample above twice the highest frequency; resolution is 1/record length.
  • Noise is described by its power spectral density, the Fourier transform of its autocorrelation.

Exercises

  1. Compute the Fourier transform of a rectangular pulse of height 1 and duration \(T\) centred on \(t=0\). Where is its first zero?
    Solution

    \(\tilde f(\omega) = \int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\ee^{\ii\omega t}\dd t = \frac{2\sin(\omega T/2)}{\omega}\). The first zero is at \(\omega = 2\pi/T\). A 20 ns square pulse has its first spectral zero 50 MHz from the carrier, with slowly decaying side lobes: a poor choice near other transitions.

  2. A Gaussian pulse has \(\sigma = 5\) ns. What is its spectral standard deviation in MHz? Compare with an anharmonicity of 250 MHz.
    Solution

    \(\Delta f = 1/(2\pi\sigma)\approx32\) MHz; 250 MHz is about 8 standard deviations away.

  3. Show that the convolution of two Lorentzians of widths \(\gamma_1, \gamma_2\) is a Lorentzian of width \(\gamma_1+\gamma_2\).
    Solution

    A Lorentzian of width \(\gamma\) is the transform of \(\ee^{-\gamma|t|/2}\). Convolution in frequency is multiplication in time: \(\ee^{-\gamma_1|t|/2}\ee^{-\gamma_2|t|/2} = \ee^{-(\gamma_1+\gamma_2)|t|/2}\). Decay rates from independent mechanisms add: \(1/Q = \sum_i1/Q_i\).

  4. An AWG samples at 2.4 GS/s. What is the highest frequency it can synthesise directly, and what frequency appears if you program a 1.6 GHz tone?
    Solution

    Nyquist is 1.2 GHz. A 1.6 GHz tone aliases to \(2.4 - 1.6 = 0.8\) GHz.

  5. White noise has \(C(\tau) = \sigma^2\tau_c\,\delta(\tau)\). What is its spectral density, and what happens to \(\expval{x^2}\)? Why does this show white noise is an idealisation?
    Solution

    \(S = \sigma^2\tau_c\), flat. Then \(\expval{x^2}\) (integral of a constant over all frequencies) diverges. Real noise is white only over a finite bandwidth, and measured noise power is the density times the bandwidth.

  6. You record a qubit's Ramsey signal for 20 µs, sampled every 50 ns. What frequency range can you see, and with what resolution?
    Solution

    Sampling at 20 MHz gives a Nyquist frequency of 10 MHz; resolution \(1/20\,\mu\mathrm s = 50\) kHz.
