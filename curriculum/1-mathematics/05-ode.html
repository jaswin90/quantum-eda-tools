Differential equations and linear dynamics

Goals

  • Understand what a differential equation says, and solve the first-order decay equation with and without a source.
  • Solve the harmonic oscillator, the damped oscillator and the driven oscillator, and define the quality factor \(Q\).
  • Write any linear system as \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x\) and solve it with eigenvectors or the matrix exponential, including normal modes of coupled oscillators.
  • Know the partial differential equations of the series and the method of separation of variables.

Physics states its laws as differential equations: rules for how something changes from moment to moment. "The current through a capacitor is proportional to how fast its voltage changes", "the acceleration of a mass is proportional to the force on it", "the rate of change of a quantum state is the Hamiltonian acting on it". Solving such an equation means finding the whole history from the rule plus a starting point. Almost every system in quantum engineering is, to a good approximation, linear, and linear differential equations can be solved with the tools of the previous chapters: exponentials, complex numbers and eigenvalues. This chapter builds the solutions you will use repeatedly: decay, oscillation, resonance and coupled modes.

First-order equations: decay

The simplest law says a quantity shrinks at a rate proportional to its size: \(\dot x = -\gamma x\) (the dot means \(\dd/\dd t\)). Try \(x = \ee^{st}\): then \(\dot x = s\ee^{st}\), so \(s = -\gamma\), and with the starting value \(x(0)\),

\[ x(t) = x(0)\,\ee^{-\gamma t}, \qquad \tau = 1/\gamma . \]

After one time constant \(\tau\), \(x\) has fallen to \(1/\ee\approx37\%\); after \(5\tau\), below 1%. Examples everywhere: a capacitor discharging through a resistor (\(\tau = RC\)), photons leaking out of a cavity, heat flowing to a cold plate, and, central to this series, a qubit's excited-state population relaxing to the ground state, whose time constant is the famous \(T_1\).

With a source term, \(\dot x = -\gamma x + f(t)\): multiply both sides by \(\ee^{\gamma t}\) (the "integrating factor"). The left side becomes \(\frac{\dd}{\dd t}(\ee^{\gamma t}x)\) by the product rule, so integrating from 0 to \(t\) gives

$$ x(t) = x(0)\,\ee^{-\gamma t} + \int_0^t \ee^{-\gamma(t-s)}\,f(s)\,\dd s . $$

Read it: the initial value decays away, and the response at time \(t\) is a weighted sum of all past inputs \(f(s)\), with weight \(\ee^{-\gamma(t-s)}\) that forgets inputs older than about \(\tau\). This "weighted memory of the past" is a convolution, the central idea of Chapter 6.

Worked example: charging a capacitor. An RC circuit with \(\tau = 1\) µs, initially uncharged, connected at \(t = 0\) to a constant source so that \(f(t) = \gamma V_0\). Then \(x(t) = \int_0^t\ee^{-\gamma(t-s)}\gamma V_0\,\dd s = V_0(1 - \ee^{-t/\tau})\). After 1 µs the voltage is 63% of \(V_0\), after 5 µs 99%. The same curve describes a qubit's excited population rising towards its thermal value, or a resonator filling with photons when a drive is switched on.

The harmonic oscillator

A mass on a spring feels a restoring force proportional to its displacement, \(F = -kx\), so Newton's law \(m\ddot x = -kx\) gives

$$ \ddot x + \omega_0^2\,x = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} . $$

Try \(x = \ee^{st}\) again: \(s^2 + \omega_0^2 = 0\), so \(s = \pm\ii\omega_0\). Imaginary exponents mean oscillation (Chapter 1). Combining the two solutions into a real one: \(x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)\). The frequency \(\omega_0\) is fixed by the system; the amplitude \(A\) and phase \(\phi\) by how it was started. Energy sloshes between kinetic (\(\tfrac12m\dot x^2\)) and potential (\(\tfrac12kx^2\)) while the total stays constant.

The same equation describes a pendulum at small angles, an LC circuit (with \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\), Book 2), every mode of a cavity, and the small motions of almost any system near equilibrium (because any smooth potential looks like a parabola near its minimum). Its quantum version (Book 3) describes resonators and photons, and a transmon qubit is, to first approximation, one too.

Damping and the quality factor

Add friction proportional to velocity, \(-m\gamma\dot x\):

$$ \ddot x + \gamma\,\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0, \qquad s = -\frac\gamma2 \pm \ii\sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \frac{\gamma^2}{4}} . $$

(From \(s^2 + \gamma s + \omega_0^2 = 0\) and the quadratic formula.) The real part of \(s\) gives decay, the imaginary part oscillation. Three regimes:

  • Underdamped (\(\gamma \lt 2\omega_0\)): oscillation inside a decaying envelope, \(x\approx A\ee^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_0t+\phi)\) when \(\gamma\ll\omega_0\). The amplitude decays at \(\gamma/2\), the energy (proportional to amplitude squared) at \(\gamma\).
  • Critically damped (\(\gamma = 2\omega_0\)): the fastest return to rest without overshooting (how car suspensions are tuned).
  • Overdamped (\(\gamma > 2\omega_0\)): slow creeping back with no oscillation.

For high-quality oscillators, the useful measure is the quality factor:

$$ Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma} = 2\pi\times\frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy lost per cycle}} . $$

Roughly, \(Q\) is how many radians of oscillation happen before the energy is gone (\(Q/2\pi\) cycles). A guitar string has \(Q\sim10^3\); superconducting microwave resonators reach \(Q\sim10^6\); at 5 GHz that means energy lasts \(Q/\omega_0\approx30\) µs. Loss in quantum hardware is almost always quoted as a \(Q\) or as its inverse, a loss tangent (Book 2).

Driven oscillators and resonance

Push the oscillator with a periodic force \(F\cos\omega t\). After the start-up transient has decayed, it oscillates at the drive frequency \(\omega\). Use a phasor (Chapter 1), \(x = \Re[\tilde X\ee^{\ii\omega t}]\), so each time derivative becomes \(\ii\omega\): \((-\omega^2 + \ii\gamma\omega + \omega_0^2)\tilde X = F\), hence

$$ \tilde X = \frac{F}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + \ii\gamma\omega}, \qquad |\tilde X|^2 \approx \frac{F^2/4\omega_0^2}{(\omega-\omega_0)^2 + (\gamma/2)^2}\quad(\omega\approx\omega_0). $$

(The approximation uses \(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 = (\omega_0-\omega)(\omega_0+\omega)\approx2\omega_0(\omega_0-\omega)\) near resonance.) The response peaks sharply at \(\omega = \omega_0\): resonance. The peak shape is a Lorentzian whose full width at half maximum is \(\gamma\). So the width of a resonance in frequency equals the decay rate in time: a narrow line means a long-lived oscillator, and \(Q = \omega_0/\gamma\) can be read from a frequency sweep. The phase of \(\tilde X\) swings by \(\pi\) as \(\omega\) passes through \(\omega_0\) (the oscillator lags the drive by 90° exactly on resonance), which is why a resonance traces a circle in the complex plane.

Worked example: reading \(Q\) from a measurement. A resonator at 6 GHz shows a resonance 60 kHz wide (full width at half the peak power). Then \(Q = 6\times10^9/6\times10^4 = 10^5\), and the energy lifetime is \(1/\gamma = 1/(2\pi\times60\,\mathrm{kHz}) = 2.65\) µs. A network analyser sweep of a few seconds measures what would otherwise need nanosecond-resolved time measurements.

Systems of linear equations: \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x\)

Any linear differential equation can be rewritten as a set of first-order equations. For the damped oscillator, use \(\mathbf x = (x, \dot x)\):

\[ \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\begin{pmatrix}x\\ \dot x\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-\omega_0^2 & -\gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\ \dot x\end{pmatrix}. \]

(The first row just says "the derivative of \(x\) is \(\dot x\)"; the second is the equation of motion.) The general system \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x\) has the solution

$$ \mathbf x(t) = \ee^{At}\,\mathbf x(0) = \sum_k c_k\,\ee^{\lambda_kt}\,\mathbf v_k , $$

where \(\lambda_k, \mathbf v_k\) are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(A\) (Chapter 3), and \(c_k\) are the components of \(\mathbf x(0)\) along the eigenvectors. Why: along an eigenvector, \(A\) acts as multiplication by \(\lambda_k\), so each component obeys its own simple equation \(\dot c = \lambda_kc\) and grows or decays as \(\ee^{\lambda_kt}\). The real parts of the eigenvalues decide stability (negative means decay), and the imaginary parts give oscillation frequencies.

The Schrödinger equation \(\ii\hbar\,\dd\ket\psi/\dd t = H\ket\psi\) is exactly of this form, with \(A = -\ii H/\hbar\). Because \(H\) is Hermitian, its eigenvalues \(E_k\) are real, so the eigenvalues of \(A\) are purely imaginary, \(-\ii E_k/\hbar\): pure oscillation, no decay. Closed quantum systems never lose anything; every state is a sum of energy eigenstates each spinning at its own frequency. Decay appears only when the system is coupled to an environment (Book 3, Chapter 8).

Worked example: normal modes of two coupled oscillators. Two identical LC circuits coupled by a small capacitor obey \(\ddot x_1 = -\omega_0^2x_1 + \kappa x_2\) and \(\ddot x_2 = -\omega_0^2x_2 + \kappa x_1\), i.e. \(\ddot{\mathbf x} = -K\mathbf x\) with \(K = \begin{pmatrix}\omega_0^2 & -\kappa\\ -\kappa & \omega_0^2\end{pmatrix}\). Try \(\mathbf x = \mathbf v\,\ee^{\ii\omega t}\): \(K\mathbf v = \omega^2\mathbf v\), an eigenproblem. The eigenvectors are \((1,1)\) (both oscillating in step) and \((1,-1)\) (in opposition), with \(\omega^2 = \omega_0^2\mp\kappa\). These are the normal modes: patterns that oscillate at a single frequency. If you excite only oscillator 1, that is half of each normal mode; their slightly different frequencies make them drift in and out of phase, and the energy beats back and forth between the two oscillators with period about \(2\pi\omega_0/\kappa\). The quantum version of this energy swap is the iSWAP gate between two qubits (Book 4).

Partial differential equations

Fields (voltage along a transmission line, electric field in a chip, the wavefunction of an electron) depend on position as well as time and obey partial differential equations (PDEs), involving derivatives in several variables. Four appear repeatedly in the series:

(\(\nabla^2 = \partial_x^2 + \partial_y^2 + \partial_z^2\) adds up the curvature in each direction.) The classic analytic method is separation of variables. For the wave equation on a string of length \(L\) with fixed ends, try \(u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)\). Substituting and dividing by \(XT\) gives \(X''/X = T''/(c^2T)\). The left side depends only on \(x\), the right only on \(t\), so both must equal the same constant, call it \(-k^2\). Then \(X = \sin kx\), and the fixed ends require \(kL = n\pi\); and \(T = \cos(ckt + \phi)\). The allowed frequencies \(\omega_n = n\pi c/L\) are the modes of the string, of a half-wave transmission-line resonator and of an optical cavity. Any motion is a sum of modes, each behaving like an independent harmonic oscillator. That observation, made quantum, is how light is quantised in Book 5.

Taylor series and linearisation

Almost every equation in the physical world is nonlinear, and almost every one is solved, at first, by pretending it is linear. The tool that justifies this is the Taylor series: near a point \(x_0\), a smooth function is a polynomial,

\[ f(x_0+h) = f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,h + \tfrac12f''(x_0)\,h^2 + \tfrac16f'''(x_0)\,h^3 + \cdots \]

For small \(h\), each term is much smaller than the one before, so a few terms are enough. Expansions used throughout the series:

\[ \ee^x\approx1+x+\tfrac{x^2}2, \quad \cos x\approx1-\tfrac{x^2}2+\tfrac{x^4}{24}, \quad \sin x\approx x-\tfrac{x^3}6, \quad \sqrt{1+x}\approx1+\tfrac x2, \quad \frac1{1+x}\approx1-x . \]

Linearisation: why everything oscillates. A particle of mass \(m\) in any potential \(V(x)\) with a minimum at \(x_0\) has \(V'(x_0) = 0\). Taylor-expanding, \(V(x)\approx V(x_0) + \tfrac12V''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2\): near the bottom, every valley is a parabola. So small motions are harmonic with \(\omega = \sqrt{V''(x_0)/m}\). For a pendulum, \(V = -mg\ell\cos\theta\) gives \(V''(0) = mg\ell\), and with "mass" \(m\ell^2\) (moment of inertia), \(\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}\). The same argument makes every circuit near its operating point an LC oscillator, and every crystal a set of vibrating springs. If \(V''(x_0)\lt0\) the point is a maximum: unstable, and small displacements grow exponentially instead of oscillating.

The next terms are the nonlinearity. For the pendulum, \(\cos\theta\approx1-\theta^2/2+\theta^4/24\), and the \(\theta^4\) term is what makes a transmon an anharmonic oscillator and hence a qubit (Book 2, Chapter 3; Book 3, Chapter 4).

Sensitivity: small changes, first order. A resonator has \(f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})\). If \(C\) changes by a small fraction \(\epsilon\), then \(f\propto(1+\epsilon)^{-1/2}\approx1-\epsilon/2\). A 1% error in a simulated capacitance moves the frequency by 0.5%, about 25 MHz at 5 GHz. Rules of this kind ("the fractional change in \(f\) is minus half the fractional change in \(C\)") are first-order Taylor expansions, and they are how fabrication tolerances are turned into frequency spreads (@lf:eq-sens).

Estimate \(\sqrt{1.02}\) without a calculator.

Answer

\(1 + 0.02/2 = 1.01\). The exact value is 1.00995.

Try it in Python

The solution \(\mathbf x(t) = \ee^{At}\mathbf x(0)\) can be computed directly, or a numerical integrator can step the equation forward. Both should agree.

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

w0, gamma = 2*np.pi*1.0, 0.2                      # 1 Hz oscillator, light damping
A = np.array([[0, 1], [-w0**2, -gamma]])
x0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
print(np.linalg.eigvals(A))                       # -0.1 +/- 6.28j: decay and oscillation
for t in (0.5, 1.0, 5.0):
    print(t, expm(A*t).dot(x0)[0])                # exact solution at time t

sol = solve_ivp(lambda t, x: A.dot(x), (0, 5), x0, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12)
print(sol.y[0, -1])                               # agrees with the exact value at t = 5

Change gamma to 4*np.pi (critical damping) and to 20 (overdamped) and watch the eigenvalues become real.

When a design tool reports "mode 3 at 7.21 GHz with \(Q = 2.4\times10^5\)", it has solved a huge version of \(\dot{\mathbf x}=A\mathbf x\) and read off an eigenvalue \(\lambda = -\gamma/2 + \ii\omega\): the imaginary part is the frequency and the real part the loss, so \(Q = \omega/\gamma = -\Im\lambda/(2\Re\lambda)\). Unwanted package modes (@lf:sec-pkg-modes) are simply eigenvalues that landed too close to a qubit frequency.

  • Linear equations are solved by trying \(\ee^{st}\): real \(s\) gives decay, imaginary \(s\) oscillation, complex \(s\) both.
  • Decay with time constant \(\tau\); with a source, the response is a convolution of past inputs.
  • Oscillator \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}\); damping \(\gamma\); quality factor \(Q = \omega_0/\gamma\); resonance linewidth = decay rate.
  • \(\dot{\mathbf x} = A\mathbf x\) is solved by eigenvectors; coupled oscillators have normal modes and beat.
  • Separation of variables turns wave equations into modes, each an oscillator.

Exercises

  1. A qubit has \(T_1 = 100\,\mu\)s. What fraction of an excited population survives a 40 ns gate? A 1 µs readout?
    Solution

    \(\ee^{-0.04/100}\approx1 - 4\times10^{-4}\), so 0.04% is lost in the gate. \(\ee^{-1/100}\approx0.990\), so about 1% during readout.

  2. A resonator at 6 GHz has a measured linewidth of 60 kHz. What are \(Q\) and the photon lifetime?
    Solution

    \(Q = 10^5\); lifetime \(1/(2\pi\times60\,\mathrm{kHz})\approx2.65\) µs.

  3. Find the eigenvalues of \(A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-\omega_0^2&-\gamma\end{pmatrix}\) and show they match @eq:m-damped.
    Solution

    \(\Tr A = -\gamma\), \(\det A = \omega_0^2\); by @eq:m-2x2, \(\lambda = -\gamma/2\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2}\), which is \(-\gamma/2\pm\ii\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}\) when underdamped.

  4. In the coupled-oscillator example, start with \(x_1(0) = 1\), \(x_2(0)=0\) and zero velocities. Show that \(x_2(t)\) reaches its largest amplitude after \(t\approx\pi\omega_0/\kappa\).
    Solution

    \(\mathbf x(0) = \frac12[(1,1) + (1,-1)]\), so \(x_2(t) = \frac12[\cos\omega_1t - \cos\omega_2t]\) with \(\omega_{1,2}\approx\omega_0\mp\kappa/2\omega_0\). Using \(\cos a - \cos b = -2\sin\frac{a+b}2\sin\frac{a-b}2\), \(|x_2|\) has envelope \(|\sin(\kappa t/2\omega_0)|\), which first peaks at \(t = \pi\omega_0/\kappa\).

  5. Find the first three resonance frequencies of a 10 mm long transmission line, open at both ends, in which waves travel at \(c/\sqrt{6.2}\).
    Solution

    \(v\approx1.20\times10^8\) m/s, \(f_n = nv/2L = n\times6.0\) GHz: 6, 12 and 18 GHz.

  6. A mass–spring system has \(\omega_0/2\pi = 1\) kHz and \(Q = 50\). How long does its energy take to fall to 1/e? How many oscillations is that?
    Solution

    \(\gamma = \omega_0/Q = 2\pi\times1000/50 = 126\) s\(^{-1}\); energy lifetime \(1/\gamma = 8\) ms, i.e. 8 oscillations (\(Q/2\pi\)).
