Putting it together: the mathematics of measuring a resonator

Goals

  • Use every tool of this book on one realistic task: characterising a superconducting microwave resonator.
  • See how complex numbers, differential equations, eigenvalues, Fourier analysis, probability and fitting fit together in practice.
  • Learn the workflow you will use again and again: model → prediction → measurement → fit → uncertainty → decision.

Mathematics sticks when it is used. This chapter follows one everyday task in a quantum lab: a newly fabricated chip contains a microwave resonator, and we want to know its frequency and how lossy it is (its quality factors), with honest error bars. Doing this needs nearly every chapter of this book: a differential equation for the resonator, complex numbers and phasors for its response, eigenvalues when it couples to a neighbour, Fourier analysis to connect time and frequency, statistics for the noise, and least-squares fitting for the answer. The same workflow, at larger scale, is what the design tools of Book 7 and the review automate.

Step 1: the model (Chapters 1 and 5)

A resonator is a damped harmonic oscillator. Its complex amplitude \(a(t)\), whose squared modulus is the stored energy, obeys a first-order equation (the "slowly varying" form of Chapter 5's oscillator, written in a frame rotating at the drive frequency, Chapter 1):

\[ \dot a = -\Big(\ii\delta + \frac{\kappa}{2}\Big)a + \sqrt{\kappa_e}\,a_{\mathrm{in}}, \qquad \kappa = \kappa_i + \kappa_e , \]

where \(\delta = \omega_0 - \omega\) is the detuning between resonance and drive, \(\kappa_i\) the internal loss rate (what we want to know: materials quality), \(\kappa_e\) the rate of coupling to the measurement line, and \(a_{\mathrm{in}}\) the incoming signal. Every term is either a decay (Chapter 5, first-order equations) or a rotation (Chapter 1, phases).

Step 2: the predicted response (Chapters 1 and 6)

Drive at a steady frequency, so that after a few lifetimes \(a\) is constant: set \(\dot a = 0\). Then \(a = \sqrt{\kappa_e}\,a_{\mathrm{in}}/(\ii\delta + \kappa/2)\). In a "notch" geometry, the transmitted signal past the resonator is the incoming wave minus what the resonator radiates back into the line, \(S_{21} = 1 - \sqrt{\kappa_e}\,a/a_{\mathrm{in}}\):

\[ S_{21}(\omega) = 1 - \frac{\kappa_e}{\kappa_i+\kappa_e + 2\ii(\omega_0-\omega)} . \]

(Dividing top and bottom by \(\omega_0\) gives the form with quality factors, @b2:eq-c-notch.) Three predictions follow directly, using only complex-number reasoning:

  • Depth. On resonance (\(\omega = \omega_0\)), \(S_{21} = 1 - \kappa_e/\kappa = \kappa_i/\kappa\). A very lossy resonator barely dips; a critically coupled one (\(\kappa_i = \kappa_e\)) dips to ½.
  • Width. The dip in \(|S_{21}|^2\) has a Lorentzian shape of full width \(\kappa\) (Chapter 6: "linewidth equals decay rate").
  • Circle. As \(\omega\) sweeps, \(S_{21}\) traces a circle in the complex plane, passing through 1 far from resonance, with diameter \(\kappa_e/\kappa\). This follows because \(z\mapsto1/(\text{const} + \ii x)\) maps a straight line to a circle, a classical fact about complex functions.

The circle separates the two loss rates. The width of the resonance gives the total \(\kappa = \kappa_i+\kappa_e\); the diameter of the circle gives the fraction \(\kappa_e/\kappa\). Two measurable features, two unknowns. That is why experimentalists plot \(S_{21}\) in the complex plane, not just its magnitude: the phase carries half the information.

Step 3: the same physics in the time domain (Chapters 5 and 6)

Instead of sweeping frequency, we could switch off a resonant drive and watch the stored energy ring down: \(|a(t)|^2 = |a(0)|^2\ee^{-\kappa t}\) (first-order decay at rate \(\kappa\)). The Fourier transform of a decay \(\ee^{-\kappa t/2}\) is a Lorentzian of width \(\kappa\) (Chapter 6), so the time and frequency measurements must agree. A frequency sweep is usually easier for high-\(Q\) resonators (the ring-down can last tens of microseconds and needs fast electronics); a ring-down is better when the resonance is too narrow to sweep finely.

Worked numbers. Design target: \(\omega_0/2\pi = 6.000\) GHz, \(Q_e = \omega_0/\kappa_e = 2\times10^5\), and we hope for \(Q_i = 10^6\). Then \(\kappa_e/2\pi = 30\) kHz, \(\kappa_i/2\pi = 6\) kHz, \(\kappa/2\pi = 36\) kHz. Prediction: linewidth 36 kHz, dip to \(\kappa_i/\kappa = 0.17\) (−15.6 dB), circle diameter 0.83, energy lifetime \(1/\kappa = 4.4\) µs.

Step 4: a neighbour shifts the answer (Chapter 3)

Suppose a second, unintended mode of the chip (for example a "slot mode" of the ground plane) sits at 6.010 GHz and couples to our resonator with strength \(g/2\pi = 1\) MHz. Our resonator is no longer an isolated oscillator; the pair is described by the \(2\times2\) matrix of Chapter 3. With detuning \(\Delta/2\pi = -10\) MHz, the eigenvalues shift by about \(g^2/|\Delta| = 0.1\) MHz: the resonator appears at 5.9999 GHz instead of 6.0000. The eigenvector is about \((1, g/\Delta) = (1, 0.1)\): 1% of the energy (\(0.1^2\)) now sits in the other mode. If that mode is lossy, our resonator inherits 1% of its loss rate, which can dominate a high-\(Q\) measurement. Unexpectedly low \(Q_i\) is often a symptom of exactly this, and spotting it needs the avoided-crossing reasoning of Chapter 3.

Step 5: noise and how many sweeps (Chapter 7)

The measured \(S_{21}\) at each frequency has Gaussian noise from the amplifiers (Chapter 7, and Book 2). If one sweep has noise of standard deviation \(\sigma = 0.02\) per point, averaging \(n\) sweeps reduces it to \(0.02/\sqrt n\). How precisely can we then determine \(\kappa_i\)? Roughly, the uncertainty in a fitted width scales with the noise divided by the depth of the feature and the square root of the number of points across it. A useful rule from experience: to measure \(Q_i\) to 5%, the dip must be measured with a signal-to-noise ratio of a few hundred across a few dozen points within the linewidth. That fixes the frequency step (about 1 kHz here) and the number of averages.

Low drive power is required for honest \(Q_i\) (Book 6: microscopic defects saturate at high power and hide their loss), but low power means a weak signal and more noise. Measurements at single-photon power can need hours of averaging. That trade-off, accuracy against measurement time, is the same one the review discusses for cold test of whole chips (@lf:sec-test-scarce).

Step 6: fitting and uncertainty (Chapters 7 and 8)

We fit the model \(S_{21}(\omega;\,\omega_0,\kappa_i,\kappa_e)\) (plus a few nuisance parameters for cable delay and amplitude) to the measured complex data by nonlinear least squares (Chapter 8): minimise \(\sum_k|S_{21}^{\mathrm{meas}}(\omega_k) - S_{21}^{\mathrm{model}}(\omega_k)|^2\). With Gaussian noise this is maximum likelihood (Chapter 7). Good starting values come from the data directly: \(\omega_0\) from the deepest point, \(\kappa\) from the half-power width, \(\kappa_e/\kappa\) from the circle diameter. The fit's uncertainty comes from the curvature of the cost near its minimum (or from repeating the measurement), and is propagated to derived quantities such as \(Q_i = \omega_0/\kappa_i\) with @eq:m-propagation.

Try it: simulate and fit. The code generates noisy notch data for the worked numbers above, then fits it. Run it several times with different seeds to see the scatter of the fitted \(Q_i\): that scatter is the measurement uncertainty.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import least_squares

f0, ki, ke = 6.000e9, 6e3, 30e3                 # Hz; kappa/2pi values
f = f0 + np.linspace(-150e3, 150e3, 301)
def s21(p, f):
    f0, ki, ke = p
    return 1 - ke / (ki + ke + 2j*(f0 - f))

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
noise = 0.02 * (rng.standard_normal(f.size) + 1j*rng.standard_normal(f.size))
data = s21((f0, ki, ke), f) + noise

def resid(p):
    r = s21(p, f) - data
    return np.concatenate([r.real, r.imag])

fit = least_squares(resid, x0=(f0 + 2e3, 10e3, 20e3), x_scale=(1e3, 1e3, 1e3))
f0_fit, ki_fit, ke_fit = fit.x
print("Qi =", f0_fit / ki_fit, " Qe =", f0_fit / ke_fit)

With this noise level the fitted \(Q_i\) typically lands within a few percent of \(10^6\), while \(Q_e\) is determined to about 1%. Raise the noise to 0.1 (closer to a single-photon measurement) and the \(Q_i\) scatter grows to tens of percent while \(Q_e\) stays well determined: internal loss is the small part of the total and the hardest to measure, exactly as experimentalists find.

Step 6b: is the fit any good?

A fitting routine always returns numbers, even when the model is wrong. Before trusting \(Q_i\), check the fit in three ways.

  1. Look at the residuals, the differences between data and fitted model. If the model is right they look like the noise: random, centred on zero, with no structure. A wiggle that repeats at the resonance, or a slope across the sweep, means the model is missing something (an impedance mismatch that tilts the circle, a second resonance nearby, or a nonlinear resonator driven too hard).
  2. Compare the residual size with the noise. With \(N\) data points and noise \(\sigma\) per point, a good fit has \(\sum_k|r_k|^2\approx N\sigma^2\) (a "reduced chi-squared" near 1, Chapter 7). Much larger means the model is inadequate; much smaller means the noise estimate is wrong or the model is overfitting.
  3. Vary the starting point and the fit window. A fit that lands on different answers from different starting guesses, or whose \(Q_i\) changes when the frequency window is narrowed, is not determined by the data. Nonlinear least squares finds a local minimum (Chapter 8), not necessarily the right one.

The most common failure in resonator fitting is forgetting the cable delay: a long cable adds a phase \(\ee^{-\ii\omega\tau}\) that winds the circle into a spiral. Fit without it and \(Q_i\) can be wrong by a factor of several while the magnitude plot still looks fine. Always plot the complex data, not just \(|S_{21}|\).

A fit to 300 points with noise 0.02 per point has a total squared residual of 1.2. Is it good?

Answer

Expected \(\approx N\sigma^2 = 300\times0.0004 = 0.12\). The residual is ten times larger: the model is missing something. Plot the residuals to find out what.

Step 7: from numbers to decisions

Suppose the fit gives \(Q_i = (8.5\pm1.2)\times10^5\). Is the fabrication process good enough for qubits that need \(Q_i\ge10^6\)? The honest answer is a probability: if the uncertainty is Gaussian, the chance that the true value exceeds \(10^6\) is \(1 - \Phi((10 - 8.5)/1.2) = 1 - \Phi(1.25)\approx11\%\) (Chapter 7). Measuring ten resonators across the wafer, rather than measuring one more precisely, also reveals the spread between devices, which matters more for yield (Chapter 7's yield argument). This shift, from "what is the number?" to "what is the distribution, and what should we do given it?", is the core of the review's approach to quantum chip design.

Resonator characterisation is the simplest instance of the loop the review wants to automate: a model predicts a response from design parameters, a measurement tests it, a fit extracts parameters with uncertainty, and the result updates the process model and the next design (@lf:sec-prob-record). Every step uses this book's mathematics.

  • A resonator is a damped oscillator; its steady response is a complex Lorentzian that traces a circle.
  • Width gives total loss; circle diameter gives the coupling fraction; together they separate internal and external \(Q\).
  • Time-domain ring-down and frequency-domain width are Fourier partners and must agree.
  • Couplings to neighbouring modes shift frequencies by \(g^2/\Delta\) and can import loss.
  • Noise falls as \(1/\sqrt n\); fits give parameters with uncertainties; decisions use probabilities, not single numbers.

Exercises

  1. For the worked numbers, compute \(|S_{21}|\) on resonance and at a detuning of one half-linewidth (\(|\omega - \omega_0| = \kappa/2\)).
    Solution

    On resonance \(|S_{21}| = \kappa_i/\kappa = 6/36 = 0.167\). At \(\omega_0 - \omega = \kappa/2\): \(S_{21} = 1 - \kappa_e/(\kappa + \ii\kappa) = 1 - \frac{30}{36}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\ii} = 1 - 0.833(0.5 - 0.5\ii) = 0.583 + 0.417\ii\), modulus 0.717.

  2. Show that \(S_{21} = 1 - \frac{\kappa_e/\kappa}{1 + \ii x}\) (with \(x = 2(\omega_0-\omega)/\kappa\)) lies on a circle with centre \(1 - \kappa_e/2\kappa\) and radius \(\kappa_e/2\kappa\).
    Solution

    \(\frac{1}{1+\ii x} = \frac{1 - \ii x}{1 + x^2}\). Its distance from \(\tfrac12\) is \(\big|\frac{1 - \ii x}{1+x^2} - \tfrac12\big| = \big|\frac{1 - x^2 - 2\ii x}{2(1+x^2)}\big| = \frac{\sqrt{(1-x^2)^2 + 4x^2}}{2(1+x^2)} = \tfrac12\). So \(1/(1+\ii x)\) lies on the circle of radius ½ centred at ½; scaling by \(\kappa_e/\kappa\) and subtracting from 1 gives the stated circle.

  3. If the parasitic mode in Step 4 has \(Q = 10^3\) at 6.01 GHz and 1% of our resonator's energy lives in it, what internal \(Q\) would we measure even if our resonator were otherwise perfect?
    Solution

    Inherited loss rate \(\approx0.01\times\omega/10^3 = \omega\times10^{-5}\), so \(Q_i\approx10^5\): a tenfold underestimate of a \(10^6\) resonator. Diagnosing such hidden couplings is part of measurement and design.

  4. Ten resonators on one wafer give \(Q_i\) values (in units of \(10^5\)): 9.1, 7.8, 10.4, 8.9, 6.2, 9.7, 8.4, 11.0, 7.5, 9.0. Estimate the mean, the standard deviation and the standard error of the mean.
    Solution

    Mean 8.80. Deviations squared sum to about 17.9; sample variance \(17.9/9 = 1.99\), standard deviation 1.41. Standard error \(1.41/\sqrt{10} = 0.45\). So the wafer's average \(Q_i\) is \((8.8\pm0.45)\times10^5\), while individual devices spread by \(\pm1.4\times10^5\).
