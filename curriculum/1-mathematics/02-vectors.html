Vector spaces, inner products and Dirac notation

Goals

  • Know what a complex vector space, a basis and a dimension are, and why functions can be vectors too.
  • Compute inner products, norms and orthogonal projections, and orthonormalise a set of vectors.
  • Read and write Dirac's bra–ket notation fluently, including outer products and the resolution of the identity.

Vectors beyond arrows

In school a vector is an arrow with a length and a direction. The idea that survives into quantum mechanics is more abstract and more useful: a vector space is any collection of objects that you can add together and multiply by numbers, with the usual rules (addition is commutative and associative, there is a zero vector, multiplication distributes over addition). When the numbers are complex we have a complex vector space. Examples:

  • Columns of \(n\) complex numbers, \(\CC^n\). The state of a qubit lives in \(\CC^2\).
  • Complex-valued functions \(f(x)\) on an interval, added pointwise. The wavefunction of a particle lives in such a space.
  • Polynomials of degree at most \(d\). Useful for exercises, and for the polynomial fits of Book 7.

A set of vectors \(\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}\) is linearly independent if no nontrivial combination \(\sum c_iv_i\) is zero. A basis is an independent set that spans the space, so every vector is a unique combination of basis vectors. The number of basis vectors is the dimension. \(\CC^n\) has dimension \(n\); the space of functions on an interval is infinite-dimensional, with bases such as the Fourier modes \(\ee^{2\pi\ii kx/L}\) of Chapter 6.

Inner products, length and angle

To talk about length and orthogonality we need an inner product, a rule that takes two vectors and returns a complex number \(\langle u, v\rangle\). On \(\CC^n\) the standard choice is

$$ \langle u, v\rangle = \sum_{k=1}^n u_k^*\, v_k , \qquad \|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v, v\rangle} = \Big(\sum_k |v_k|^2\Big)^{1/2}. $$

The conjugate on the first argument is essential. Without it, \(\langle v,v\rangle\) for \(v = (1,\ii)\) would be \(1 + \ii^2 = 0\), and a nonzero vector would have zero length. With it, \(\langle v,v\rangle = |1|^2+|\ii|^2 = 2\). The defining properties are: linearity in the second argument, \(\langle u, v\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle^*\) (conjugate symmetry), and \(\langle v,v\rangle > 0\) for \(v\ne0\). For functions on \([a,b]\) the same idea gives

$$ \langle f, g\rangle = \int_a^b f(x)^*\, g(x)\,\dd x . $$

Two vectors are orthogonal if \(\langle u,v\rangle=0\). A basis \(\{e_k\}\) is orthonormal if \(\langle e_j, e_k\rangle = \delta_{jk}\) (the Kronecker delta: 1 if \(j=k\), else 0). In an orthonormal basis the coefficients of a vector are just inner products: if \(v = \sum_k c_k e_k\), then taking the inner product with \(e_j\) gives \(c_j = \langle e_j, v\rangle\). This one-line fact is behind Fourier series, quantum measurement and modal analysis of electromagnetic structures alike.

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. \(|\langle u,v\rangle| \le \|u\|\,\|v\|\), with equality only when one vector is a multiple of the other.

Proof. If \(u=0\) it is trivial. Otherwise, let \(w = v - \frac{\langle u, v\rangle}{\|u\|^2}u\), the part of \(v\) orthogonal to \(u\). Then \(0 \le \|w\|^2 = \|v\|^2 - |\langle u,v\rangle|^2/\|u\|^2\), which rearranges to the claim. ∎

Cauchy–Schwarz is the mathematical root of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation (Book 3) and guarantees that quantum probabilities, which are squared inner products of unit vectors, lie between 0 and 1.

Projection and Gram–Schmidt

The orthogonal projection of \(v\) onto the direction of a unit vector \(e\) is \(\langle e, v\rangle\, e\). Subtracting projections one at a time turns any independent list into an orthonormal one. This is the Gram–Schmidt procedure: given \(v_1, v_2, \dots\),

$$ e_1 = \frac{v_1}{\|v_1\|}, \qquad w_k = v_k - \sum_{j\lt k} \langle e_j, v_k\rangle\, e_j, \qquad e_k = \frac{w_k}{\|w_k\|} . $$

Numerically the same idea, done carefully, is the QR factorisation used inside eigenvalue solvers (Chapter 8).

Worked example. Orthonormalise \(v_1 = (1, 1, 0)\) and \(v_2 = (1, 0, 1)\) in \(\RR^3\).

\(e_1 = (1,1,0)/\sqrt2\). Then \(\langle e_1, v_2\rangle = 1/\sqrt2\), so \(w_2 = (1,0,1) - \tfrac12(1,1,0) = (\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1)\), with norm \(\sqrt{3/2}\). So \(e_2 = (1,-1,2)/\sqrt6\). Check: \(\langle e_1, e_2\rangle = (1 - 1 + 0)/\sqrt{12} = 0\).

Dirac notation

Quantum mechanics uses a notation, due to Dirac, that makes inner products and projections almost automatic. A vector is written as a ket \(\ket{\psi}\). For each ket there is a bra \(\bra{\psi}\), which in \(\CC^n\) is the conjugate transpose: the row \((\psi_1^*, \dots, \psi_n^*)\). Putting a bra next to a ket makes a "bra-ket", the inner product:

$$ \ket{\psi} = \begin{pmatrix}\psi_1\\ \vdots\\ \psi_n\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \bra{\phi} = (\phi_1^*, \dots, \phi_n^*), \qquad \braket{\phi}{\psi} = \sum_k \phi_k^*\psi_k . $$

Putting a ket next to a bra the other way round, \(\ketbra{\psi}{\phi}\), gives a column times a row: an \(n\times n\) matrix, the outer product. Acting on a vector, it does \(\big(\ketbra{\psi}{\phi}\big)\ket{\chi} = \ket{\psi}\braket{\phi}{\chi}\): it measures the overlap of \(\ket\chi\) with \(\ket\phi\) and returns that much of \(\ket\psi\). In particular, for a unit vector \(\ket{e}\), the matrix \(P = \ketbra{e}{e}\) is the projector onto \(\ket e\).

If \(\{\ket{k}\}\) is an orthonormal basis, then every vector is \(\ket\psi = \sum_k \ket{k}\braket{k}{\psi}\), which means the sum of all the projectors is the identity:

$$ \sum_k \ketbra{k}{k} = I . $$

This resolution of the identity is the most used line in quantum mechanics. Inserting it between any bra and ket expresses an abstract quantity in a chosen basis. For instance \(\braket{\phi}{\psi} = \sum_k \braket{\phi}{k}\braket{k}{\psi} = \sum_k \phi_k^*\psi_k\), recovering @eq:m-braket.

For a qubit the standard (computational) basis is

\[ \ket0 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \ket1 = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \ket{\pm} = \frac{\ket0 \pm \ket1}{\sqrt2}. \]

Both \(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\) and \(\{\ket+,\ket-\}\) are orthonormal bases of \(\CC^2\). A general unit vector is \(\alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1\) with \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\).

Infinite dimensions, briefly. For functions, the "basis" \(\ket{x}\) of position states is continuous, sums become integrals and the Kronecker delta becomes the Dirac delta: \(\int \ketbra{x}{x}\,\dd x = I\), \(\braket{x}{x'} = \delta(x-x')\), and \(\braket{x}{\psi} = \psi(x)\) is the wavefunction. The rigorous theory (Hilbert spaces, rigged Hilbert spaces) is more delicate, but for engineering every formula works if you replace \(\sum_k\) by \(\int \dd x\). A space with an inner product that is complete (limits of convergent sequences stay inside) is called a Hilbert space; every finite-dimensional inner-product space is one.

The capacitance matrix of a multi-conductor layout and the modes of a 3D electromagnetic simulation are both expressed in bases, and the overlap integrals that couple them are inner products of the form @eq:m-inner-fn. The "energy participation ratio" used to turn a field simulation into a qubit Hamiltonian (@lf:sec-design-extract) is a ratio of such integrals. Understanding projection onto a basis is understanding what those tools compute.

Exercises

  1. Compute \(\braket{+}{-}\), \(\braket{0}{+}\) and \(\| \ket0 + \ii\ket1\|\).
    Solution

    \(\braket{+}{-} = \tfrac12(1\cdot1 + 1\cdot(-1)) = 0\). \(\braket{0}{+} = 1/\sqrt2\). \(\|\ket0+\ii\ket1\| = \sqrt{|1|^2+|\ii|^2} = \sqrt2\).

  2. Show that \(\ket{\pm\ii} = (\ket0 \pm \ii\ket1)/\sqrt2\) form an orthonormal basis, and expand \(\ket0\) in it.
    Solution

    The bra of \(\ket{+\ii}\) is \((1, -\ii)/\sqrt2\), so \(\braket{+\ii}{-\ii} = \tfrac12(1\cdot1 + (-\ii)(-\ii)) = \tfrac12(1-1)=0\). Each has norm 1. Coefficients: \(\braket{+\ii}{0} = 1/\sqrt2\) and \(\braket{-\ii}{0}=1/\sqrt2\), so \(\ket0 = (\ket{+\ii}+\ket{-\ii})/\sqrt2\).

  3. Show that \(P = \ketbra{e}{e}\) satisfies \(P^2 = P\) for a unit vector \(\ket e\), and write \(P\) as a matrix for \(\ket e = \ket+\).
    Solution

    \(P^2 = \ket e\braket{e}{e}\bra e = \ket e\bra e = P\) because \(\braket ee = 1\). For \(\ket+\): \(\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}(1\ \ 1) = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\).

  4. On \([0, L]\) with @eq:m-inner-fn, show that \(\phi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)\), \(n=1,2,\dots\), are orthonormal.
    Solution

    \(\langle\phi_m,\phi_n\rangle = \frac2L\int_0^L\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\dd x = \frac1L\int_0^L\big[\cos\frac{(m-n)\pi x}{L} - \cos\frac{(m+n)\pi x}{L}\big]\dd x\). For \(m\ne n\) both cosines integrate to zero over whole half-periods; for \(m=n\) the first term gives \(L\), so the result is 1. These are the standing waves of a string, of a particle in a box and of a transmission-line resonator.

  5. Prove the triangle inequality \(\|u+v\| \le \|u\|+\|v\|\) from Cauchy–Schwarz.
    Solution

    \(\|u+v\|^2 = \|u\|^2+\|v\|^2+2\Re\langle u,v\rangle \le \|u\|^2+\|v\|^2+2|\langle u,v\rangle| \le \|u\|^2+\|v\|^2+2\|u\|\|v\| = (\|u\|+\|v\|)^2\).
