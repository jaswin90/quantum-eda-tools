Probability, statistics and inference

Goals

  • Use random variables, probability distributions, means, variances and covariances.
  • Know the main distributions (binomial, Poisson, Gaussian, exponential, log-normal) and where each appears in quantum engineering.
  • Propagate uncertainty through a formula, and understand the central limit theorem and the \(1/\sqrt n\) law of averaging.
  • See why the yield of a chip collapses exponentially with its number of components.
  • Reason with Bayes' theorem, likelihoods and posteriors, and estimate probabilities by Monte Carlo simulation.

Probability appears in quantum engineering in two completely different ways. First, quantum mechanics itself is probabilistic: a measurement of a qubit gives a random outcome, and everything we learn about a device comes from averaging many random results. Second, manufacturing is random: every fabricated junction, film and linewidth differs a little from its target, so every chip is a random draw. The review that this course prepares for is, at heart, about the second kind: predicting the distribution of what a factory will make. This chapter builds the probability and statistics needed for both, from the basics.

Random variables, expectation and variance

A random variable \(X\) is a number determined by a random process: the result of a qubit measurement (0 or 1), the resistance of a fabricated junction, the number of photons in a pulse. If \(X\) takes discrete values \(x_k\) with probabilities \(p_k\) (adding to 1), its expectation or mean is the probability-weighted average \(\mathbb E[X] = \sum_k p_kx_k\). If it is continuous, it has a probability density \(p(x)\): the probability of landing between \(a\) and \(b\) is the area \(\int_a^bp(x)\dd x\), and \(\mathbb E[X] = \int x\,p(x)\dd x\).

The variance measures spread around the mean \(\mu\):

$$ \operatorname{Var}X = \mathbb E\big[(X - \mu)^2\big] = \mathbb E[X^2] - \mu^2, \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}X}. $$

The standard deviation \(\sigma\) is in the same units as \(X\), so it is the number to quote ("the junction resistance is 10.0 kΩ with a spread of 0.2 kΩ").

Worked example: a qubit measurement. A qubit gives 1 with probability \(p = 0.3\) and 0 with probability 0.7. Mean: \(0.3\times1 + 0.7\times0 = 0.3\). \(\mathbb E[X^2] = 0.3\) too (since \(1^2 = 1\)), so \(\operatorname{Var}X = 0.3 - 0.09 = 0.21 = p(1-p)\), and \(\sigma = 0.46\). A single measurement tells you little; you must repeat it.

Two rules handle sums. Expectation is always linear: \(\mathbb E[aX+bY] = a\mathbb E[X]+b\mathbb E[Y]\). Variances add for independent variables: \(\operatorname{Var}(X+Y) = \operatorname{Var}X+\operatorname{Var}Y\). In general there is an extra term \(2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\), where the covariance \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb E[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]\) measures whether \(X\) and \(Y\) tend to be high together. For many variables, the covariances form a symmetric covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). Its eigenvectors are the directions of independent variation (principal component analysis), the same eigen-decomposition as Chapter 3.

Worked example: averaging shots. Estimate \(p\) by measuring \(n\) times and averaging the results. The average of \(n\) independent copies has variance \(\operatorname{Var}X/n\), so its standard deviation, the standard error, is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). With \(p = 0.3\): 100 shots give ±0.046, 10,000 shots ±0.0046. Ten times more precision costs a hundred times more measurements: the \(1/\sqrt n\) law that makes characterising quantum chips slow.

The distributions that matter

In words: the binomial counts successes in \(n\) independent tries (\(\binom nk\) is the number of ways to choose which \(k\) succeed). The Poisson counts rare independent events in a fixed window (photons arriving, particles landing); its variance equals its mean. The exponential is the waiting time for such an event. The Gaussian is the bell curve.

The Gaussian is everywhere because of the central limit theorem: the sum of many small independent random effects is approximately Gaussian, whatever the individual effects look like. Process variation in a well-controlled fab is close to Gaussian because many small independent effects add up. Probabilities for Gaussians are written with the standard normal cumulative distribution \(\Phi(k)\), the probability that a standard Gaussian is below \(k\). The tails matter most for yield:

The log-normal arises when a quantity depends exponentially on a Gaussian one. A Josephson junction's resistance depends exponentially on its oxide thickness \(d\), \(R\propto\ee^{d/d_0}\) (Book 6). If \(d\) is Gaussian, \(R\) is log-normal, and a tiny absolute spread in \(d\) becomes a percent-level spread in \(R\), which becomes a tens-of-MHz spread in qubit frequency (@lf:sec-var-junction).

Propagation of uncertainty

If \(Y = g(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) depends on several uncertain inputs with small spreads, approximate \(g\) by its tangent plane (first-order Taylor expansion) around the means. Then

$$ \operatorname{Var}Y \approx \sum_{i,j}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_j}\,\Sigma_{ij} = \mathbf J\,\Sigma\,\mathbf J^{\mathsf T}, $$

with \(\mathbf J\) the vector of derivatives (the Jacobian). For independent inputs this is the familiar "add in quadrature": \(\sigma_Y^2 \approx\sum_i(\partial g/\partial x_i)^2\sigma_i^2\). For products and ratios, relative errors add in quadrature.

Worked example: qubit frequency spread. A qubit's frequency scales roughly as \(\omega\propto\sqrt{E_J/C}\). Then \(\ln\omega = \tfrac12\ln E_J - \tfrac12\ln C + \text{const}\), so \(\delta\omega/\omega\approx\tfrac12\sqrt{(\delta E_J/E_J)^2 + (\delta C/C)^2}\). With 2% spread in \(E_J\) and 1% in \(C\): \(\tfrac12\sqrt{4+1}\%\approx1.1\%\), about 56 MHz at 5 GHz. The square root halves the relative errors, and the larger input (junctions) dominates. Statistical sign-off in chip design is, at heart, this formula with a Jacobian computed by a simulator.

Independence and why yield collapses

Suppose each qubit on a chip independently lands inside its specification with probability \(p\). The probability that all \(N\) do is \(p^N\). Writing \(p = 1 - q\) with small failure probability \(q\), \(p^N = \ee^{N\ln(1-q)}\approx\ee^{-Nq}\). Yield falls exponentially with the number of components, and to hold it constant the per-component failure rate must fall like \(1/N\). @fig:m-yield shows this for a frequency window of \(\pm\Delta\) with Gaussian spread \(\sigma\). With \(\Delta/\sigma = 3\) (a "three-sigma" process, failure probability 0.27% per qubit), 100 qubits are all good only 76% of the time, and 1000 qubits only 7% of the time. A four-sigma window keeps 1000 qubits at 94%.

A 99.7% chance per qubit sounds excellent. But a chip is only as good as its worst qubit, and with a thousand qubits, a 0.3% failure rate means about three failures per chip on average, so a perfect chip is rare. This is the arithmetic behind the review's central claim that quantum processors are "variability-limited from the first device".

Bayes' theorem and inference

Conditional probability \(\Pr(A|B)\) is the probability of \(A\) given that \(B\) happened. From \(\Pr(A\text{ and }B) = \Pr(A|B)\Pr(B) = \Pr(B|A)\Pr(A)\) follows Bayes' theorem. For an unknown parameter \(\theta\) and observed data \(D\):

$$ p(\theta\,|\,D) = \frac{p(D\,|\,\theta)\;p(\theta)}{p(D)}, \qquad \text{posterior} \propto \text{likelihood}\times\text{prior}. $$

The prior \(p(\theta)\) encodes what we knew before the data; the likelihood \(p(D|\theta)\) is how probable the observed data would be if the parameter were \(\theta\); the posterior is what we know afterwards. The denominator just makes the posterior add to 1.

Worked example: is the qubit in state 1? A readout reports "1" correctly 97% of the time when the qubit is in \(\ket1\), and wrongly reports "1" 2% of the time when it is in \(\ket0\). Before measuring, the qubit was in \(\ket1\) with probability 10%. The readout says "1". Bayes gives

\[ \Pr(1\,|\,\text{"1"}) = \frac{0.97\times0.1}{0.97\times0.1 + 0.02\times0.9} = \frac{0.097}{0.115} \approx 0.84 . \]

Even a good detector leaves a 16% chance of error when the prior is lopsided, because most "1" reports come from the many \(\ket0\) qubits that were misread. Readout-error mitigation and the decoders of quantum error correction (Book 4) are Bayesian inference problems of exactly this kind.

With a flat prior, the most probable parameter is the one that maximises the likelihood: the maximum-likelihood estimate. For data \(y_k\) with Gaussian noise of known \(\sigma\) around a model \(f(x_k;\theta)\), the log-likelihood is \(-\sum_k(y_k - f(x_k;\theta))^2/2\sigma^2\) plus a constant, so maximum likelihood is least squares: the familiar curve fit. The width of the likelihood around its peak gives the uncertainty, and with \(n\) independent measurements it shrinks as \(1/\sqrt n\). That law governs how long you must average to measure a qubit frequency, a \(T_1\) or a gate fidelity, and is why cold measurement time is scarce (@lf:sec-test-scarce).

A hierarchical model puts probability distributions on the parameters of distributions. For example, each wafer's mean junction resistance is drawn from a fab-wide distribution, and each junction from its wafer's. Data from many wafers then sharpen predictions for a new one. The review proposes such a model for predicting cold qubit frequencies from room-temperature measurements (@lf:sec-var-bayes); Book 7 develops it.

Monte Carlo methods

When a probability or average is too hard to compute by formula, simulate it: draw random samples \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) from the distribution and average \(g(X_i)\). The error falls as \(\sigma_g/\sqrt n\) regardless of how many variables are involved, which makes Monte Carlo the workhorse of yield analysis. Its weakness is rare events: to estimate a failure probability of \(10^{-6}\) to 10% precision, you need about \(10^8\) samples, because almost none fail. Importance sampling fixes this by drawing samples from a distribution \(q(x)\) concentrated near the failures and correcting each by the weight \(p(x)/q(x)\). Error-correction simulations that estimate logical error rates of \(10^{-10}\), and statistical sign-off of large chips, both depend on such techniques.

Worked example: estimating \(\pi\). Throw \(n\) random points uniformly into a unit square; the fraction landing inside the quarter circle \(x^2+y^2 \lt 1\) estimates \(\pi/4\). Each point succeeds with probability \(p = 0.785\), so the standard error of \(\pi\) is \(4\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\approx1.6/\sqrt n\): 10,000 points give \(\pi\pm0.016\). The same recipe, "sample, count, divide", estimates the probability that a randomly fabricated chip has no frequency collisions (Book 7).

Confidence intervals: how sure is a number?

Every measured quantity in quantum hardware (a \(T_1\), a gate error, a yield) is an estimate from a finite number of samples, and it should come with an uncertainty. The key fact is the difference between two numbers that are easily confused:

  • the spread of the data, the standard deviation \(\sigma\) of individual samples, which does not shrink as you take more;
  • the uncertainty of the mean, the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt N\), which does.

By the central limit theorem, for large \(N\) the sample mean is approximately Gaussian around the true mean with width \(\sigma/\sqrt N\). So the interval "mean \(\pm1.96\) standard errors" captures the true mean in 95% of experiments: a 95% confidence interval. For small \(N\), replace 1.96 by a slightly larger factor from Student's \(t\) distribution (2.26 for \(N = 10\)).

Worked example: a qubit's \(T_1\). Ten repeated measurements give a mean of 82 µs with sample standard deviation 9 µs. The standard error is \(9/\sqrt{10} = 2.8\) µs, and the 95% interval is \(82\pm2.26\times2.8 = 82\pm6.4\) µs. Two qubits measured at 82 and 86 µs are therefore not shown to differ. Note that the 9 µs spread is real physics (\(T_1\) fluctuates over hours as defects drift), not just measurement error: it would not shrink with more data.

Worked example: rare errors. For a probability estimated from counts, \(\hat p = k/N\) has standard error \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/N}\). Three readout errors in 1000 shots gives \(\hat p = 0.003\pm0.0017\). If you see zero failures in \(N\) trials, the "rule of three" gives a 95% upper bound of about \(3/N\): no failures in 1000 shots means the error rate is probably below 0.3%, not that it is zero.

A 95% confidence interval does not mean "95% probability that the true value is in this particular interval". It means the procedure that built it captures the true value in 95% of repetitions. The difference rarely matters in practice, but the Bayesian credible interval of @sec:m-prob-bayes is the object that has the first meaning.

How many shots are needed to measure an error rate near 1% with a standard error of 0.1%?

Answer

\(\sqrt{0.01\times0.99/N} = 0.001\) gives \(N\approx9900\), about ten thousand shots.

Try it in Python

Monte Carlo makes every formula in this chapter testable. Here we check the \(1/\sqrt n\) law and the yield curve of @fig:m-yield.

import numpy as np
from math import erf, sqrt
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1/sqrt(n): spread of the estimated probability from n shots
p = 0.3
for n in (100, 10000):
    est = rng.binomial(n, p, size=2000) / n
    print(n, est.std(), sqrt(p*(1-p)/n))       # simulated vs formula

# Yield of N qubits with window +/- k sigma
Phi = lambda x: 0.5*(1 + erf(x/sqrt(2)))
k, N = 3.0, 100
sims = (abs(rng.standard_normal((20000, N))) < k).all(axis=1).mean()
print(sims, (2*Phi(k) - 1)**N)                 # both about 0.76

Almost every open problem in the review is a statistics problem in disguise: chance-constrained frequency allocation (which design maximises the probability of no collisions?), hierarchical Bayesian prediction of cold parameters from warm data, Bayesian experimental design for scarce cryogenic test time, and simulation-based inference of noise models. This chapter supplies the vocabulary; Book 7 supplies the methods.

  • Mean, variance, covariance; expectations add, independent variances add; averages of \(n\) shots have errors falling as \(1/\sqrt n\).
  • Binomial (counts of successes), Poisson (rare events), Gaussian (sums of many effects), exponential (waiting times), log-normal (exponentials of Gaussians).
  • Uncertainty propagates through the Jacobian; relative errors of products add in quadrature.
  • All \(N\) components good: \(p^N\approx\ee^{-Nq}\), so yield collapses with size unless per-component failure falls as \(1/N\).
  • Bayes: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior; least squares is maximum likelihood for Gaussian noise; Monte Carlo estimates anything by sampling.

Exercises

  1. A qubit in the state \(\sqrt{0.3}\ket0 + \sqrt{0.7}\ket1\) is measured 1000 times. What are the mean and standard deviation of the number of "1" outcomes? How many measurements estimate the probability 0.7 to \(\pm0.001\)?
    Solution

    Binomial: mean 700, standard deviation \(\sqrt{1000\times0.21}\approx14.5\). Standard error \(\sqrt{0.21/n} = 0.001\) gives \(n = 2.1\times10^5\) shots.

  2. Show that the sum of two independent Gaussians is Gaussian with means and variances added. (Use \(\mathbb E[\ee^{\ii kX}] = \ee^{\ii k\mu - k^2\sigma^2/2}\).)
    Solution

    For independent variables, \(\mathbb E[\ee^{\ii k(X+Y)}] = \mathbb E[\ee^{\ii kX}]\mathbb E[\ee^{\ii kY}] = \ee^{\ii k(\mu_X+\mu_Y) - k^2(\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2)/2}\), the characteristic function of a Gaussian with the summed mean and variance.

  3. A junction's critical current is \(I_c\propto\ee^{-d/d_0}\) with \(d_0 = 0.1\) nm. If the barrier thickness has standard deviation 0.005 nm, what is the relative spread of \(I_c\), and of the qubit frequency \(\omega\propto\sqrt{I_c}\)?
    Solution

    \(\delta I_c/I_c = \delta d/d_0 = 5\%\); frequency 2.5%, about 125 MHz at 5 GHz.

  4. Using @fig:m-yield's model, what window \(\Delta/\sigma\) gives a 1000-qubit chip a 90% chance of every qubit being in specification?
    Solution

    Need \(p = 0.9^{1/1000}\approx1 - 1.05\times10^{-4}\); two-sided tail \(2(1-\Phi(k)) = 1.05\times10^{-4}\) gives \(k\approx3.9\).

  5. A detector has dark-count probability \(10^{-3}\) per window and efficiency 90%. A photon is present with prior probability 0.5. Given a click, what is the probability a photon was present? Given no click?
    Solution

    Click: \(\frac{0.45}{0.45 + 0.0005}\approx0.9989\). No click: \(\frac{0.05}{0.05 + 0.4995}\approx0.091\). Inefficiency hurts the "no click" inference far more than dark counts hurt the "click" inference.

  6. Photons arrive at a detector at an average of 3 per microsecond. What is the probability of zero photons in a given microsecond? Of more than 6?
    Solution

    Poisson with \(\lambda = 3\): \(p(0) = \ee^{-3} = 0.050\). \(p(\le6) = \ee^{-3}(1 + 3 + 4.5 + 4.5 + 3.375 + 2.025 + 1.0125) = 0.966\), so \(p(>6) = 3.4\%\).
